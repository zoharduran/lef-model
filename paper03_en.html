Pressure and the pear: building the Moon from within and from without

The physical environment of the paper

The scenario of the Moon’s formation takes place on the early Earth, about 4.5 billion years ago. In the initial state the mass of the material that will form the Moon is included in the parent body — the young Earth before the Moon left it. During construction that mass passes to a developing body, while the density distribution, the spin, the gravitational field and the thermal state change continuously. This paper treats the whole of that construction — the feed from within and the rise of the outer shell — as one process on one body.

Because so much changes during the process, a value taken from one moment cannot be used at another. The working values, the defined inputs and the rules that govern the calculations are therefore gathered in the following table: for each domain it states which values are inputs, which change with time, and which belong to a defined test state. An isolated numerical value cannot replace the physical profile that the calculation requires. The left column names the domain; the right column gives the working values and the rules for using them.

Abstract

One body, built two ways at once. In the LEF model, the Moon was built from Earth material while still attached to it. This paper defines the mechanism as one process on a single body, combining two complementary construction mechanisms at the same time. From within, a magmatic upwelling — deep, hot material driven up by internal pressure — feeds a rising body under pressure. From without, the parent’s outer sialic shell (its light top layer) rises with the pear over the construction region and slides slowly up to its tip, where it is the body’s skin, the surface of its upper dome; as the body rounds it becomes the Moon’s far-side crust.

From within: the push is priced. A one-dimensional pressure test on the initial parent gives an equatorial source pressure of 150.26 GPa — the pressure at the parent’s centre, made by the weight of the material above it. Lifting deep melt to the corotation radius, where turning with the Earth already gives orbital speed, costs 139.78 GPa at a deep density of 3,500 kg/m³ and leaves a residual pressure of 10.49 GPa; the feed therefore passes for melt lighter than about 3,864 kg/m³ and fails above that value. A joint test of pressure head, flow resistance and radial load, in one non-equatorial state, shows that a narrow, massless passage cannot build the Moon: the radial balance holds only above a passage radius of about 1,000 km, at which the passage already holds 0.46 lunar masses and must carry a lateral load of order 102210^{22} N. The connection is therefore broad, hot and massive.

From without: the body on the tip. The external part supplies that connection and its geometry. At the intermediate stage, the foamed launch state, the body sits on the wide tip of the pear as a squashed body over a 6,000-km contact: its lower dome hugs the curved tip and its upper dome rises about 3,650 km above the rim of the contact — the height at which the full lunar mass has a density of 1,300 kg/m³. The body then rounds outward, each part keeping its share of the surface: the contact narrows to 3,000 km and stays attached, the rest of the lower dome peels away gradually with the sialic layer it sat on, and the three parts — the contact, the peeling annulus and the upper dome — become a near-side cap of about 31°, a belt extending to 70°, and the far side. The body then flattens with the spin, the stage in which most of the large craters form, and rounds inward as the flattening relaxes and the body densifies: the craters keep their size and gather at the poles (Paper 7); at release the body is round. The far-side crust is built of many layers of compacted sial, from sial that migrated to the tip and from material that returned again and again, and it should grow denser and richer in iron with depth.

The pear and the release. There is one bulge: the pressure draws it out, and it becomes a pear of the Earth’s own material — 9,000–11,000 km wide at the surface, its tip 6,000 km wide at the start and 3,000 km at release, in a single continuous arc without a waist. The pear is part of the Earth, held in the Earth’s gravitational field like any other part of it, and its tip carries the body that becomes the Moon. Separation begins when the Moon’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line — about 16,400 km from the Earth’s centre on the equator, 17,287 km at latitude 22.5°; the corotation forces pull it outward with force, the internal pressure assists, and it is finally released about 210 km beyond the line, on a nearly circular orbit (Paper 4; §C.5a). The pear, which lies wholly inside the line, leaves no residue in orbit; after release the sorting gathers it back into the Earth over tens to hundreds of millions of years.

What the paper delivers. The paper defines quantitative constraints on geometry, rheology and the division of mass, and states the requirements it places on the deep equation of state, the energy release and the three-dimensional structure. Part A prices the feed and its pressure balances; Part B follows the migration of the shell; Part C, the connection, the pear and the release; Part D, predictions, tests and conclusions; Part E, what the pear left in the Earth.

What the abstract means, in plain words. Fed from within and raised from without describes two supplies of material to one body, acting at the same time. Picture a balloon being filled from a pipe beneath it, while its skin is the stretched surface of the ground it was lying on. The filling is the feed: deep, hot mantle material rising under pressure and flowing into the body. The skin is the shell: the Earth’s light outer layer over the construction region, which rose with the pear and slid slowly up to its tip, into the body, where it became the body’s surface. Neither supply alone makes the Moon. The feed gives the body its interior — the Earth’s own mantle, which is why the two share their composition (§C.10) — and the shell gives it a crust that already carried craters from its time on the Earth.

What 150 GPa means. Deep inside any large body the material is squeezed by the weight of everything above it. At the parent’s centre that weight, slightly relieved by the spin, produces a pressure of 150.26 GPa. This pressure is the engine of the feed: once a path opens from the deep interior to the surface, the squeezed material is pressed up along it, as toothpaste rises when the tube is squeezed. The question is whether the push is strong enough. To carry melt from the depth of the source up to the corotation radius, the pressure must hold up the weight of the whole column of melt along the way; for melt of 3,500 kg/m³ at depth, foamed to 1,500 kg/m³ above the surface, that weight is 139.78 GPa. The internal pressure exceeds it by 10.49 GPa, and would exceed it for any melt whose density is lower than about 3,864 kg/m³. That is the meaning of the words “the push is priced”: the paper does not assume that the pressure suffices, it computes it.

Why the connection must be wide. If the feed rose through a narrow pipe, the pipe would have to hold the body against the Earth’s pull along the line between them (the radial load) and against the sideways pull of the spin at latitude 22.5°, which draws the body toward the equatorial plane (the lateral load). The mechanical calculation shows that only a passage more than about 1,000 km in radius passes the radial test, and a passage that wide is no longer thin: it holds almost half a Moon’s mass of material itself and carries a lateral force of order 102210^{22} N. It follows that the connection is a broad, hot, massive part of the Earth, and Part C shows what it is: the pear.

What the pear is. The pear is one bulge of the Earth, drawn out. The pressure that raised a hill over the source went on raising it, and the hill grew into a long, wide extension of the Earth’s own material: broad at its base on the Earth’s surface — 9,000 to 11,000 km, more than the Earth’s radius — and narrowing in one smooth arc to its tip, which carries the body. It is part of the Earth, and it is held by the Earth’s gravity like any other part of it. It is not thrown into space, because its speed is not a throwing speed and it lies wholly inside the corotation line (§C.7). Its mass, derived from the width of its base, is some nine to twelve lunar masses, and the energy needed to lift it is of the order of the energy released when the Earth’s iron sinks into the core (§C.9).

Why the release takes place where it does. The whole pear lies inside the corotation line; there the combined pull of gravity and spin points toward the Earth, and the pear is held by the Earth’s gravity as part of it. The body on its tip rises with it until its centre of mass reaches the line, and there it begins to separate. At this point the speed it already has from turning with the Earth equals the speed of a free circular orbit, and beyond the line the spin pulls outward more strongly than gravity pulls inward; the contact resists only a little, and the body is pulled outward with force and finally released about 210 km beyond the line (§C.5). The pear itself stays inside the line, and so leaves no material in orbit; after release the sorting gathers it back into the Earth over tens to hundreds of millions of years.

Introduction — one body, two supplies

In the model the parent body does not eject a finished Moon; the formation of the Moon is the growth and outward motion of one attached body. The body grows and moves outward while receiving material from two sources at once: deep material rises into it through a magmatic upwelling, and the outer shell of the parent — the sialic layer over the construction region — rises with it as its surface. Paper 2, LEF Mechanism in Principle, showed that at the corotation radius the speed a mass turning with the Earth already has is the speed a free orbit requires, and that the parent’s angular-momentum budget permits the transfer. In other words, Paper 2 showed where the Moon had to be released, and that the Earth had enough rotation to bring it there. This paper asks how the mass reached that radius and what supported it along the way.

Two supplies to one body. The two sources act together and contribute to the same body. The shell travels to the body: it rises with the pear and at the same time slowly separates and slides up to its tip, into the body, where it becomes the body’s skin; the slide is eased by the “lubricated” layer between sial and sima (§C.10). The construction region is a dome, the dome rises, and its surface goes up with it: the crust is the Earth’s, it carried its craters onto the Moon, and on the far side it was built up in layers. This is why one paper treats both supplies: they are not two mechanisms competing to explain the Moon, but the inside and the outside of one growing body.

The role of corotation and of the pear. Corotation provides a link between spin and orbital velocity. It does not lift material — the pear does — but it is what detaches the Moon: when the Moon’s centre of mass reaches the line, the corotation forces pull it free. Material at the Earth’s surface turns with it, but far more slowly than an orbit requires; something must carry it outward and hold it there until it reaches the corotation radius. For that, a driving pressure, a mass and energy balance, a structure that permits flow, and a connection able to carry the required load are needed.

The structure of the paper. We examine these components in four Parts. Part A, the feed from within: the transport problem, the source, the thermal boundaries, and whether the pressure, rate and energy suffice. Part B, the rise from without: the four stages of the dome, its geometry and what it becomes. Part C, the connection and the release: the loads the connection carries, and how the body begins to separate at the corotation line and is released just beyond it; this Part also follows the pear that forms the connection, the material the body is made of, and the material the Earth went on throwing after release. Part D: predictions, tests and conclusions. A fifth Part, E, follows the pear back into the Earth, records where its base stood relative to the deep Earth as seen today, and asks what it did to the Earth’s interior; a closing section collects directions for further research.

The novelty. The novelty lies not in new physical laws but in the combination of the processes into one connected construction. The laws of gravity, flow, heat conduction and viscous adhesion are the physics by which the mechanism is examined, not independent evidence that it occurred. Each of these laws is standard; what the paper adds is their application to one body in one sequence, with the numbers of one consistent environment, so that each step can be checked against the next.

PART A

From within: the transport problem and the material balance

THE PROBLEM

How does deep material join a body growing above the Earth’s surface? This Part defines the fed body and the forces required, before we examine whether the pressure can supply them.

Paper 2, LEF Mechanism in Principle, showed where the Moon is released — at the corotation radius, where the speed of turning with the Earth is already the speed of a free orbit — and showed that the Earth’s angular momentum is enough to place material there. It did not show how the material got there. This Part takes up that question from inside the Earth, and defines the conditions for transporting deep material into the developing body. It first keeps an account of the mass (§A.1), then explains why the Earth’s spin by itself cannot lift the body (§A.2), and finally writes the force balance that any feed from depth must satisfy (§A.3). The sections that follow describe the source, the passage and the thermal boundaries (§A.4–A.8), and the next continuation of this Part puts numbers on the pressure, the rate and the energy (§A.9–A.13).

A.1A body that grows while moving

The first step is bookkeeping. Before any force is computed, we must be able to say where every kilogram is at every moment, so that no mass is counted twice and none disappears from the account.

The overall mass-balance equation. At the start of the scenario the future lunar material is wholly included in the parent body: in the initial state there is only one body, the parent, of mass M0M_0. As construction proceeds, that mass is divided among four separate reservoirs — a terrestrial reservoir, a developing body, material in transit and material that has left — according to the equation of mass conservation:

M0=ME(t)+m(t)+Mtransit(t)+Mescaped(t).M_0=M_E(t)+m(t)+M_{\mathrm{transit}}(t)+M_{\mathrm{escaped}}(t). \tag{1}

In words: at every time tt, the initial mass equals the mass still in the Earth, ME(t)M_E(t); plus the mass already accumulated in the developing body, m(t)m(t); plus the mass on its way between them (inside the connection, or material thrown out and not yet landed back), Mtransit(t)M_{\mathrm{transit}}(t); plus the mass that has left the system altogether, Mescaped(t)M_{\mathrm{escaped}}(t). The equation is simply conservation of mass; its use is that it forces each kilogram into exactly one place.

The rate of the body’s mass growth. The change with time of the developing body’s mass satisfies:

dmdt=Ṁint+Ṁext−Ṁout.\frac{dm}{dt}=\dot M_{\mathrm{int}}+\dot M_{\mathrm{ext}}-\dot M_{\mathrm{out}}. \tag{2}

Equation (2) says how fast the body grows; a dot over a mass symbol denotes a rate, in kilograms per second. Ṁint\dot M_{\mathrm{int}} is the internal feed — the rate at which deep material arrives from below through the connection; Ṁext\dot M_{\mathrm{ext}} is the external incorporation — the rate at which the shell that rises with the body is joined to it (Part B), the Earth’s outer layer that becomes the body’s skin; and Ṁout\dot M_{\mathrm{out}} is the exit from the body — the material thrown out through its openings. The body’s mass grows by what comes in from within and from without, less what goes out.

A rule for returning material. Material that leaves and returns is recorded in both rates: a parcel ejected through an opening is subtracted when it leaves and added again when it lands back on the body. Not every exit is therefore to be identified with escape from the system; most ejected material stays in the account as material in transit, and returns. The internal share of the final Moon — how much of it came from depth rather than from the rising shell — is determined by integrating the feed and the losses over the whole construction window, not from an assumed fixed ratio. (Part B.7 discusses that division.)

A.2 Why rotation is not the transport itself

The question this section answers is why the Moon could not simply be thrown off the spinning Earth, and why a continuous transport mechanism is therefore required to carry it out.

The failure of ballistic ejection. Paper 2 (§A.1.2) gave the reason a thrown body fails. At the equator of a parent turning once in 5.5 hours, the surface moves at about 2,133 m/s, while a free orbit just above the surface requires about 7,701 m/s. Material let go from the surface, or thrown up from it, moves far too slowly sideways to stay in orbit: its path is a short ellipse that runs back into the parent, and it falls back. Only at the corotation radius, about 15,818 km from the centre at the 5.5-hour day, does the speed of turning with the Earth equal the orbital speed. The material must therefore be brought to that distance while it is still attached and still turning with the Earth. Bringing it there is what we call transport: a continuous supply of deep material, pushed up from a source inside the Earth through a connection into the growing body — not a single throw.

Loading example: a balance of accelerations. A natural objection is that the Earth’s fast spin might do the lifting by itself. The following loading example tests this. Consider a body with the full lunar mass and an equivalent radius of 2,270 km (the radius of a sphere with the Moon’s mass at the low density of 1,500 kg/m³), touching a parent of equatorial radius 6,721 km; the distance between the centres is 8,991 km — the sum of the two radii. (At stage 2 of Part B the same mass sits on the tip of the pear as a squashed body over a 6,000-km contact; the loading example is kept in the spherical form for comparison with Paper 2.) At 8,991 km the outward push is less than a fifth of the inward pull. The net inward acceleration is about 4.03 m/s² (4.93 less 0.905): every kilogram of the body still weighs, toward the Earth, about two fifths of its weight at the Earth’s surface today. This is a defined loading example, not a description of a tiny bulge at the start of construction. It shows why an additional mechanism transferring force and energy is required: rotation alone provides no support in this state. The spin lightens the load; it does not carry it.

Buoyancy and feed under pressure. Buoyancy can drive light material inside denser material, as oil rises through water. But buoyancy needs a denser surrounding medium to push against, and above the parent’s surface there is none; passage above the parent’s surface therefore requires a solution that includes the external shape and the connection. The mechanism examined here is continuous feed under pressure: deep material is pushed up by the pressure of the parent’s interior, through a connection, into the body. It is not necessary to exclude every other ejection mechanism in order to examine this one; the paper asks whether this one mechanism works, and puts it to quantitative tests.

A.3What drives the material

Having established the mechanism, we write the balance it must satisfy: how much pressure is needed to push material from depth along a path to the body.

The effective potential. In a frame rotating at a constant rate with the parent — that is, as seen by an observer who turns with the Earth — gravity and the centrifugal effect are combined into a single quantity, the effective potential. A potential is the energy per kilogram that a parcel of material has because of where it is; material tends to move from high potential to low, as water runs downhill. The effective potential is:

Φeff=Φgrav−12ω2ϖ2,\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}=\Phi_{\mathrm{grav}}-\frac{1}{2}\omega^2\varpi^2, \tag{4}

where Φgrav\Phi_{\mathrm{grav}} is the gravitational potential, ω\omega the angular velocity, and ϖ\varpi the distance of the parcel from the spin axis. The second term is the centrifugal contribution: it lowers the potential the farther the parcel is from the axis, and so eases the climb outward. Lifting material outward in this frame means climbing a slope of Φeff\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}} — a slope that grows gentler with distance and becomes entirely flat at the corotation radius.

The momentum balance along a slow feed path. Along such a path, the force-balance component is approximately:

dpds=−ρdΦeffds−ℛvisc,\frac{dp}{ds}=-\rho\frac{d\Phi_{\mathrm{eff}}}{ds} -\mathcal R_{\mathrm{visc}}, \tag{5}

where ss is the distance measured along the path, pp the pressure, ρ\rho the density of the material in the path, and ℛvisc\mathcal R_{\mathrm{visc}} the flow resistance per unit length — the friction of a viscous material moving through a channel. Equation (5) states that, along the path, the pressure must fall by two components per unit length: the effective head, the weight of the material in the combined field of gravity and spin (the first term), and the friction losses of moving it (the second term). The first term is the familiar rule that pressure grows with depth under the weight of the material above; the pressure needed to hold up a column against effective gravity is called its hydrostatic head. The head is proportional to the density ρ\rho: a lighter material needs less pressure to rise the same distance. The density of the rising material therefore becomes the decisive quantity in §A.10.

The total pressure difference between the deep source and the head of the feed must carry the effective-gravity head, the flow resistance and the load at the receiving boundary — the weight of the body pressing down on the head of the feed. Equation (5) expresses the continuum momentum balance along the feed path; at a varying spin rate and in three-dimensional flow, accelerations and the momentum carried with the material must also be included [1]. For the slow, steady feed examined here those terms are small, and equation (5) is the form that the pressure test of §A.9 uses.

THE PROBLEM

A.4 The feed source and the internal organisation

A feed that wandered over the Earth would build many small heaps, not one Moon. For a single body to be built, the source must be concentrated, and the mass flow must stay focused on the same surface region for a long time.

The focusing mechanism. The working hypothesis is that the feed was concentrated in one region of the Earth’s interior as the interior reorganised; that region lay north of the equator (§B.1). In the young Earth, iron sank to form the core and the interior separated into layers; during that reorganisation the interior need not have been symmetric. Non-uniformity in composition, temperature and the distribution of dense material can change the pressure and the flow paths. In the model, this non-uniformity can sustain prolonged feed to the same surface region: hot, lighter material gathers on one side and keeps rising beneath the same place, as a pot heated off-centre keeps boiling at the same spot.

Angular coupling. A concentrated feed region of this kind does not follow from the mere existence of differentiation; to examine it, the potential of the mass distribution and its response to the feed must be computed. Both the redistribution of dense material and the outward transfer of mass change the moment of inertia — the measure of how far from the spin axis the mass sits — and therefore change the spin. Iron sinking inward makes the Earth turn faster, as a skater who draws in her arms; mass carried outward slows it. For this reason the coupled states of Paper 2 are used rather than a constant spin throughout the process: the day lengthens only moderately, from 5.5 to 5.77 hours by release, because the two effects largely offset each other (Paper 2, §A.4).

The body’s self-gravity. As the body’s mass accumulates, its own gravity becomes increasingly important for material near it:

gself=Gm(t)Rb(t)2.g_{\mathrm{self}}=\frac{Gm(t)}{R_b(t)^2}. \tag{6}

Here m(t)m(t) is the body’s mass at time tt and Rb(t)R_b(t) its radius; gselfg_{\mathrm{self}} is the self-gravity acceleration — the acceleration toward the body’s own centre, as felt at its surface. It grows as mass accumulates, and for the closed body at a radius of 1,890 km it reaches 1.37 m/s² (Part B.3). This gravity can attract incoming material and shape the body: returning ejecta land back on it, and its material pulls itself toward a rounded form. However, internal forces do not accelerate the body’s own centre of mass. One cannot lift oneself by pulling on one’s own bootlaces: every internal pull between two parts of the body is matched by an equal and opposite pull, and the two cancel. The overall lift requires exchanges of force and momentum with the surroundings — with the Earth, through the connection and the feed.

A.5What keeps a passage open

Melt rising through a cooler body tends to freeze and choke its path. But the path is not a channel with rigid walls: the pear itself is the connecting passage between the Earth’s interior and the body. This section states what keeps that passage open, and what this does and does not provide.

Heat supply from both sides. The connection receives heat from the feed and from the hot bodies around it: the melt passing through it carries heat up from depth, and the parent below and the growing body above are both hot.

Differences in viscosity. The external surface, in contact with the cold surroundings, can lose heat and strengthen at the same time. The system may therefore have a mobile interior and a stronger exterior, with viscosity varying between them. Viscosity is the resistance of a material to flowing — honey is more viscous than water; a hot interior has a low viscosity and flows readily, while a cooled exterior has a high viscosity and holds its shape.

A stability constraint. Heating on both sides of the connection helps prevent local freezing, but is not a confining force in itself: warmth keeps the path fluid, while the path itself is the hot interior of the pear. The shape of the passage must satisfy a balance of pressures and lateral stresses against gravity, spin and flow. The three-dimensional stability of the connected structure is a requirement the mechanism places on itself; the pressure calculation of §A.9–A.13 is the first of its tests, and §D.1 lists the rest.

A.6 Volatiles, atmosphere and cooling of the exterior

Two properties of the young Earth shape both the density of the rising material and the way the exterior cools: the volatiles it held, and the atmosphere they formed.

Foaming of the melt. Volatiles are substances that turn readily into gas — chiefly water, together with carbon dioxide and similar compounds. At depth, under pressure, they stay dissolved in the melt, as carbon dioxide stays dissolved in a closed bottle. In the scenario examined there is an ancient volatile inventory larger than the inventory remaining today. On decompression — as the melt rises and the pressure on it falls — the volatiles may be released from the melt as bubbles and change its density and behaviour, as a bottle foams when it is opened. A melt full of gas bubbles is a foamed melt. Its density is far lower than that of the same melt without bubbles, because part of its volume is gas.

Reducing the hydrostatic head. This low density is what makes the feed possible. By equation (5), the pressure needed to lift material is proportional to its density: a foamed melt needs a much smaller push to reach the same height than a dense melt. Section A.10 turns this into a threshold — the source pressure of the parent can lift deep melt to the corotation radius only if it is lighter than about 3,864 kg/m³ — and Section B.2 finds that the body on the tip of the pear has the foamed density of about 1,300 kg/m³. The role of the volatiles is to lower the density of the rising material, not to raise the source pressure above the lithostatic value (the pressure set by the weight of the overlying material); and bubbles must not be assumed at all depths, since at great depth the volatiles may still be dissolved.

A constraint from the inclusions. The presence of water in lunar inclusions — tiny droplets of melt trapped inside crystals of lunar volcanic material, which kept their original water — provides a constraint on some of the Moon’s magma sources, but does not measure the total volatile inventory at the time of construction [2]. It shows that some of the Moon’s source material was not dry, without setting how much water the whole system held.

The radiation balance and the steam atmosphere. A cycle of rise, cooling and return may transfer heat from the material to its surroundings: material thrown up through the openings loses some heat in flight, yet lands while still hot. The cooling of the whole system, however, requires energy to leave to space; heat moved from the material to its surroundings is not yet lost. A vapour atmosphere can also limit the outgoing radiation: water vapour absorbs the infrared radiation by which a hot surface cools, so a thick steam atmosphere acts as an insulating blanket, and the whole surface cannot be modelled as if it were exposed to a vacuum. Calculations of a saturated atmosphere show a radiation limit under certain conditions — a ceiling on the heat flux a steam-covered body can radiate away; but a constant flux from those calculations cannot be applied to every height and stage in this scenario [3], because the atmosphere, the temperature and the gravity all change as the body rises.

A.7A cooling envelope over a mobile base

The body needs a solid skin while its interior and its feed stay molten. This section estimates how fast such a skin forms. A freezing front is the boundary between the solid layer that has formed at the cold surface and the melt beneath it; as heat leaves through the solid, the front advances downward and the solid layer thickens. The frozen cap matters for the thermal survival of the body: once formed, it stands between the hot melt and the cold exterior, and because heat can leave only by conduction through it, the thicker it becomes, the more slowly the melt beneath it loses heat. The cap thus insulates the mobile interior that the feed requires, while itself becoming the strong outer boundary.

A.8The order of construction

The components of Part A act together, and their order matters: a step taken out of place would contradict the record the body carries.

Simultaneous processes. In this scenario, internal feed and dome uplift overlap in time while ejections through the openings are still occurring: the body is fed from below, its shell rises with it, and material is thrown out through the openings and returns — all at once. The ejections and the stopping condition. The ejections never stop while the body is attached: their intensity declines gradually as the pear grows and the body moves away from the Earth’s centre, and they stop at the moment of detachment, when the body is cut off from the Earth’s pressure. The closing of the outer shell plays no part in this. Distinction from late volcanism. Ejections of the construction stage must not be identified with late volcanism in a body that has already separated: they belong to the attached body, and are a different process from the volcanism of a free Moon.

This is the 600-million-year scenario window of the environment table; the 100-million-year case remains a sensitivity test. The four stages are set out in Part B.

A.9A defined pressure test

The question this section answers is simple to state: is the parent’s own internal pressure strong enough to hold up a column of melt reaching from its centre to the corotation radius? To answer it, two pressures are computed from the same field and compared: the pressure available at the deep source, and the hydrostatic head the column demands.

What a hydrostatic head is. A column of material standing in a gravitational field presses on its base with its weight. The pressure needed at the base to hold the column in place — so that it neither sinks nor rises — is called the hydrostatic head of the column. A pump that sends water to a tank on a roof must deliver at least the head of the water column between pump and tank; with less, the water does not arrive. The feed of the Moon is the same problem on a planetary scale: the source must deliver at least the head of a column of melt reaching from deep inside the parent up to the body. In a uniform field the head is simply density times gravitational acceleration times height; here the gravitational acceleration changes along the column, and the spin works against it, so the head is computed as density times the change of the effective potential along the path (equation 5 of §A.3, integrated).

The reference section. The test is carried out on a reference section of the parent before the division of mass: M0M_0, RVR_V, T0T_0 from the table. That is, the mass of the whole parent with the lunar material still inside it (M0=6.0455×1024M_0=6.0455\times10^{24} kg), the volumetric radius adopted for it (6,517 km) and its initial day of 5.5 hours. A methodological assumption. For an analytical test only, the calculation uses the field of a homogeneous sphere and a rotating equatorial section. This is a spherical approximation with a centrifugal correction, not an exact equilibrium solution of a Maclaurin spheroid. The choice keeps the arithmetic transparent: every term can be written in closed form and checked by hand, and the move to the true flattened figure changes the numbers only modestly.

At the centre of the parent the whole weight of the overlying material bears down; the spin lightens that weight a little at the equator, because the centrifugal effect there acts against gravity. When a path opens from the deep interior toward the surface, it is this pressure — weight minus spin — that pushes the melt along it. The existence of a threshold follows from §A.9: the source pressure is fixed by the parent’s mean density, while the head of the deep segment grows with the density of the melt that fills it; at some point the two quantities meet. The situation is like that of two connected tubes, one filled with water and one with oil: the oil column stands higher than the water column because it is lighter. In the same way, the deep melt rises above the parent’s surface only because it is lighter than the parent around it, and the lighter it is, the higher it can stand.

Neglecting the load and the losses, ρd,max≃3,864kgm−3\rho_{d,\max}\simeq3{,}864\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}. This means that each kilogram per cubic metre added to the density of the outer segment removes 0.900 kg/m³ from the deep threshold, because the two integrals are nearly equal and the two segments share one source of pressure.

The pressure test passes for a deep melt whose density is lower than 3,864 kg/m³, and fails above that value. This is the first requirement the mechanism places on the deep material, and it is the sharpest: a volatile-rich melt meets it, a dry melt does not. The reason is that dissolved volatiles — water and other light components — lower the density of a silicate melt (§A.6), and the margin between passing and failing lies within the range they can supply. A foam density near the surface is not evidence for a similar density at depth: bubbles form only where the pressure is low enough for the volatiles to leave solution, so near the surface a melt can be much lighter than the same melt at depth. In a variable-density model the two terms must be replaced by an integral over ρ[p(s),T(s),X(s)]\rho[p(s),T(s),X(s)] — the density as a function of pressure, temperature and composition along the path ss — and the source pressure and the gravitational field must also be recomputed. The constant-density test is the first step of that calculation, and its threshold is the value against which the equation of state of the deep melt is to be checked (§D.1).

A.11How fast must the feed operate?

A surplus of pressure means that the melt can flow; it does not say how much. Once a positive pressure surplus has been found, we must define the mass flow the feed is required to deliver each second to build the Moon within the time window available, and derive from it the requirements on the passage.

The two time windows are those of the environment table: 600 million years is the scenario window, and 100 million years the sensitivity case. For a smaller internal share, the mean rate falls in direct proportion. These rates represent the average net accumulation of mass in the body; any loss of supplied material requires a higher gross feed rate. Intermittent feed requires a higher rate during activity: if the feed runs only part of the time, it must run faster while it runs to deliver the same total.

For a sensitivity test, a geometrically confined segment can be represented as laminar Newtonian flow in a cylindrical passage of radius bb, length ℓ\ell and with a no-slip condition at the walls:

A.12 Energy: what is the source of the work?

Pressure and rate concern how the mass moves; energy concerns what pays for moving it. Lifting the Moon’s mass from the Earth’s surface to the corotation radius is work done against gravity, and the model must define where that work comes from.

An order-of-magnitude estimate of the gravitational work required to move the full target mass, in a fixed terrestrial field, from the contact point at 8,991 km to the release radius at 16,400 km, gives:

Wg=GMEm(1r0−1r1)≃1.47×1030J.W_g=GM_Em\left(\frac{1}{r_0}-\frac{1}{r_1}\right) \simeq1.47\times10^{30}\ \mathrm J. \tag{17}

Equation (17) is the standard work needed to move a mass mm outward in the gravitational field of a mass MEM_E, from a distance r0r_0 to a distance r1r_1 from its centre. Here r0=8,991r_0=8{,}991 km is the distance between the centres when a body of the full lunar mass touches the parent’s surface (§A.2), and r1=16,400r_1=16{,}400 km is the equatorial release radius. “Fixed terrestrial field” means that the Earth’s mass is held constant and the body is treated as a point.

The estimate refers to moving a given mass between two distances; it is not the full work balance of a body growing out of the deep source. A full balance must include the change in the gravitational energy of the whole system, the rotational energy, the internal energy, the losses and the energy carried in the departing material.

The energy-supply mechanism: the Earth’s total binding energy is not automatically counted as available energy. The binding energy is the energy that would be needed to take the whole Earth apart against its own gravity; it is large, but it is not stored in a form that can be used. Energy becomes available only when the body passes into a more tightly bound state, so a physical evolution that releases energy is required: for example, reorganisation of density, contraction or a fall in internal energy, together with exchanges of rotational energy. The work is of order 103010^{30} J, less than one per cent of the parent’s binding energy; the evolution that releases it — sinking iron and a contracting, cooling interior — is the same evolution that drives the rise of the melt. When iron sinks toward the centre, the parent’s mass draws closer together and gravitational energy is set free; the lift of the Moon needs only a small fraction of it. The full energy balance of that evolution is a requirement listed in §D.1.

The failure of the narrow tube and the contradiction of the massless connection. With these inputs, the radial hydrostatic balance becomes positive only from a passage radius of about 1,017 km. In plain words: a thin tube fails because the body’s weight, concentrated on a small cross-section, crushes the remainder below zero; a tube wide enough to spread that weight is so wide that it itself holds nearly half a Moon of material. The limit of a static fluid medium and the lateral-load constraint. A static fluid transmitting pressure only through the radial section does not supply the lateral stress. A liquid at rest can push but cannot carry a sideways force — that is what defines it as a liquid. The column of melt can carry the body’s weight along the axis, but it cannot by itself stop the body from being pushed toward the equator. A possible solution will need to define a connection shape, viscous stresses or the strength of a load-bearing material, and momentum transferred in the flow. Heating eases the passage; the lateral load is carried by the shape of the connection, which is the broad base of the dome of Part B.

PART B

From without: the body on the tip of the pear

THE PROBLEM

The internal feed lifts material; it does not by itself explain where the Moon’s crust came from, why it is thicker on the far side, or why the Moon carries an ancient crater record spread over both hemispheres. The approach: the outer shell is examined as the surface of the same body that Part A feeds. We follow it through four stages, fix its geometry at each stage, and derive what it becomes.

Part A followed the material that rises inside the body: the deep source, the pressure that drives it, and the rate and energy of the feed. This Part follows the parent’s outer sialic shell — the skin of the young Earth — and examines it as the surface of the same body that Part A feeds from depth. The question is simple to state: what happens to the Earth’s outer skin over the construction region while the body is being built beneath and above it, and where does each piece of that skin end up on the Moon we see today? The answer is the geometric core of the model. It is built here in four steps, one per stage, and the picture at each step is fixed by numbers before the next step is taken.

The whole Part rests on one picture, and it helps to hold it from the start. The Earth’s outer shell does not stay behind while the body rises; it rises with the body, as the skin of its upper dome. The body itself is not a ball floating free but a squashed ball resting on the tip of the pear: two domes, one on the other — a lower dome that hugs the rounded tip from above, and an upper dome that stands high over it. When the body later rounds outward into a ball, each part of its surface keeps its share of the whole, and so each part can be followed onto today’s Moon.

How the stages of the paper fit the series. This paper numbers its own stages: stage 1, the flat stage (§B.1); stage 2, the body on the tip of the pear (§B.2); stage 3, the outward rounding (§B.4); stage 4, the flattening, the inward rounding and the release (§§B.6, C.4–C.8). The series as a whole numbers the history of the Moon in stages 0–8: 0 before the bulge, 1 the bulge, 2 the dome, 3 the pear, 4 the rounding (outward, then flattening, then inward), 5 the release, 6 the fate of the pear, 7 the recession, 8 today. The two numberings correspond as follows. Stage 1 of this paper is series stage 0 and the beginning of the bulge: the sialic shell still lies flat over an active feed. Stage 2 is the bulge, the dome and the pear (series stages 1–3), as they appear at the moment the mass sits as a dome on the tip of the pear. Stage 3 is the first part of series stage 4, the outward rounding. Stage 4 is the rest of series stage 4 — the flattening and the inward rounding — together with series stage 5, the release. Series stage 6, the fate of the pear, is Part E of this paper; stage 7, the recession, is Paper 4, The Orbit; stage 8, today’s Moon, is where §§B.4–B.7 place each region of the surface.

B.1 Stage 1 — the flat stage and the gathered shell

Before the body rises, the parent’s outer sialic layer over the construction region lies flat, over an active feed. “Sialic” refers to sial, the light upper layer of the Earth’s crust, rich in silicon and aluminium; beneath it lies sima, the denser layer, rich in silicon and magnesium.

The terrestrial origin of the ancient crater record. Deep material rises to the sialic layer through openings and is ejected through them; part of the ejected material returns, lands on the exposed shell and makes craters (Paper 6, The Craters). The shell at this stage is the source of the Moon’s crust and of its earliest crater record. This has a consequence: the Moon’s oldest surface did not form on the Moon. It formed here, on the Earth’s surface, before the body existed, and was carried up later with the rise of the structure.

The area geometry of the source region. The initial area of the shell that will form the dome differs from the surface area of the resulting dome. The area of the Pacific basin in the author’s reconstruction (Figure 4) is estimated at about 1.65×10141.65\times10^{14} m² including the marginal seas, and at about 1.0×10141.0\times10^{14} m² without them — 1.4 to 2.4 times the surface area of the upper dome at stage 2 (7.0×10137.0\times10^{13} m², below). In this picture, as the dome formed, the shell over the region was drawn and gathered inward in area by a factor of up to about two, as a map laid flat on a table gathers inward when its middle is lifted. The basin therefore marks the source area of the gathered material, not the footprint of the dome itself. This is a reading of a present feature, and the paper states its difficulty as Paper 2 did: the present floor of the Pacific is younger than 180 Myr, and the reading refers to a hemispheric asymmetry of the lithosphere beneath it — the rigid outer layer of the Earth, the crust and the uppermost mantle — which is older and more deeply rooted than the sea floor that covers it today.

The base of the pear and its back. The region marked on Figure 4 is the pear seen from above as it leaned, not its foot. The base of the pear lay to the east, toward America, and across its whole width it held openings — into the pear and, through it, on to the Moon. In the author’s reconstruction its centre lies near Kiritimati, in the Line Islands of Kiribati — by the villages of London, Paris and Poland — at about 1.9°N, 157.5°W, about 4,800 km east-south-east of the earlier reading.

The westward lean: the lag of the spin. The pear did not stand upright, and the reason is the Earth’s spin. As it was drawn out, its outer part, carried far from the spin axis, lagged behind the Earth’s turn — as the arms of a skater lag when she spreads them — and the whole pear leaned back, westward, its tip and the Moon on it trailing behind (§C.8). The body of the leaning pear lay over the north-central Pacific, in a region centred near 25°N, 165°E (Figure 4). The northward lean: oblique feed. From a base near the equator the tip of the pear reached about 22.5°N, the latitude of release of Paper 4. The rotation does not give this northward lean — at any latitude its sideways push is directed toward the equator. It comes from the feed: the deep source, the concentrated feed region of §A.4, lay north of the equator, and the feed rose from it obliquely, northward of the base, carrying the pear north with it (§D.1).

The imprints of the stripes (Paper 9). The western side of the pear, under which Japan lies today, is its back, which was gathered back into the Earth after release; the front of the pear, stretched under tension as it leaned, was gathered back into the stripes of Paper 9, The Stripes. Paper 9 also locates the imprint of the tip itself: between two bends of the stripe system, in a region 3,360 km wide centred at 9.3°N, 169.3°E, about 3,760 km west of the base by the coordinates (an earlier version gave 3,770 km) — the direction in which the leaning pear carried its tip — while the back, gathered in, lies in the arcs of the western Pacific. The values 22.5°N of the tip at release and 9.3°N of the imprint are different quantities: the first is the direction of the Moon’s centre from the Earth’s centre at release, the second is the imprint of the gathered-in tip on today’s surface, about 15° north of the equator of the stripe system. They belong to different reference frames, and relating the two frames — the axes — is future work.

Conservation of mass and the deep provinces. The pear never left the Earth’s mass. It was not lost at release; after release the sorting gathered it back into the Earth, gradually, over tens to hundreds of millions of years — its heavy part deep into the Earth beneath the base of the pear (Part E), its front into the stripes of the North Pacific, and its back into the arcs of the western Pacific. The mass moved from high outside the Earth to deep inside it. The base lies above the Pacific large low-velocity province at the bottom of the mantle — a broad region, seen in seismic images, through which earthquake waves travel more slowly than in the surrounding mantle — in published maps of that province, which reaches from near Hawaii southward; the measured distance from the base to the province’s centre and edge is given in §E.4. The nature of the deep provinces is left open.

B.3What moves, and what does not

A reader who pictures a crust travelling thousands of kilometres from the Earth to the Moon will ask what force carries a crust that far. The shell does travel, but it is not dragged across the parent. The motion has two components: lifting — the shell rises with the body of the pear, which serves as a substrate carrying it; and sliding — at the same time as it rises, it gradually separates from the sima beneath it and slides up the pear to its tip, into the body. The forces of the spin drive the slide, in the direction of outward sorting, and the “lubricated” layer between sial and sima, a very weak shear layer, eases it (§C.10). This is why the tip separates as a body of its own. (The inward gathering of §B.1 belongs to stage 1, before the dome.)

The narrowing of the contact at stage 3: what contracts during stage 3 is not the shell but the annulus of the lower dome — the ring of the body’s underside between the rim of the contact and its centre, which is the exposed surface of the body: from a rim of 3,000 km radius to a contact of 1,500 km radius, that is, about 1,500 km of inward motion over the whole of stage 3, on the same weak interface. The rheological structure. The dome requires a layered rheological structure — “rheological” in the sense of how each layer flows under load: a cold, stiff shell (η≳1023\eta\gtrsim10^{23} Pa s, the value that keeps a crater 100 km wide from relaxing within 10710^7 yr at g=8.25g=8.25), over a hot, mobile interior, with the weakest layer at the interface between them. Here η\eta is the viscosity, the resistance of a material to flow — honey has a higher viscosity than water, and a cold shell a vastly higher one than either; a shell of 102310^{23} Pa s holds the shape of a crater 100 km wide for ten million years without sagging. The timetable. The peeling of the contact from 6,000 to 3,000 km and the rounding of the body took millions of years: slowly at the start, over hundreds of millions of years, and faster toward the end, over tens of millions; the timetable of the shell is the timetable of the rise.

Self-gravity holds the shell in place. While the forces of the spin drive the slide, the gravity of the developing body holds the shell against its surface. At the top of the upper dome the body’s own gravity is about 0.9 m/s² (for the equivalent sphere, 2,381 km at 1,300 kg/m³ — the sphere of the same volume), and it rises to 1.37 m/s² on the closed body of radius 1,890 km. On a surface centred on the body’s centre of mass this gravity is normal to the surface — it points straight down into the body — and holds the shell, but contributes no tangential driving force; the slide of the sial toward the tip is driven by the forces of the spin.

B.4 Stage 3 — the outward rounding, the contact and the translation rule

This section answers the central question of Part B: when the squashed body becomes a ball, where does each part of its surface go? The body is the load on the tip of the pear of §C.7, and stages 3 and 4 are the same tip drawn out while the body on it rounds and is released. The outward rounding and the narrowing of the contact. As the body’s self-gravity grows, it rounds outward (§C.4): the squashed body becomes a ball, at constant volume and without expansion. The contact with the parent narrows from 6,000 to 3,000 km and stays attached; the rest of the lower dome, the annulus between the two diameters, detaches gradually and peels from the rim of the contact inward, together with the sial it sat on (§C.4a) — as a soft, half-deflated ball pressed onto the top of a rounded support peels away from it from the edge inward when it is inflated. The process is continuous and takes time. At its end the body is supported on a disc wide enough that the connection is a layer rather than a neck (§C.5).

What the body’s surface becomes on the sphere is fixed by one rule: in the outward rounding each part keeps its share of the surface.

By the rule, a part covering 7% of the squashed body’s surface covers 7% of the ball’s surface, and so on for every part. B.5The openings

The body’s sialic shell is not a continuous crust. The feed of Part A reaches the dome through openings, and material is ejected through them; at stage 1 they are the maria of the flat shell, at stage 2 those on the upper dome are exposed as it rises, and those under the lower dome remain covered until release. The openings therefore differ by when they were exposed and by what passed through them. Exposed openings (the crown and the flank): the openings on the upper dome were active while they were exposed, and material was ejected through them outward. Covered openings (the near-side maria): the openings under the lower dome were internal passages; while covered, they carried the Earth’s pressure through the pear into the maria we see, and from there into the Moon, and material passed through them as through a pipe.

The order of exposure of the basins. Serenitatis was exposed before Imbrium, and when it was exposed it was still slightly active: splashes are seen in the ring around it, but no giant craters. Large craters are made by large material, driven out by the force of the pressure; the small craters in the rings around the maria come from bubbling and splashes, most of which return into the mare, while a small part lands at its margin and leaves its mark there. Imbrium was exposed last. Once it was exposed, the Moon’s internal pressure stopped — not at once, but quickly — and with it the popping of the lunar maria ceased. The Earth went on with its own activity and went on throwing material in all directions, most of it small; part of it returned to the Earth, and part landed on the Moon. This is why small craters cover the whole Moon, the near side included (§C.11).

The observational form of this picture — which basins are of which kind, and what signature each should carry — is a matter of the lunar record and is examined in Paper 11; here the openings enter only as the places where the feed of Part A meets the shell of Part B.

B.6The record carried on the body

A crater made in the shell by returning material at the flat stage (stage 1), or on the upper dome at stage 2, moves with the shell as the body rounds. This is stratigraphy — the reading of order from what lies on what — not new physics: tcrater<tcovert_{\mathrm crater}<t_{\mathrm cover} for the segment concerned. In words: a crater formed before its piece of surface was covered or moved is carried with it. The condition for preservation. The record survives if the shell keeps its shape, and that is the requirement that makes it a shell (§B.3): a viscosity of order 102310^{23} Pa s or more at the surface, over the 10710^7 yr of the rise. The shell stiffened early, in the first part of the storm; a stiff shell neither stretched as the body grew nor was squeezed as it rounded, and the craters on it kept their size.

The record is written in three stages:

In the outward rounding the existing craters change together with the surface, in the same way everywhere, while new returning material keeps arriving; at this stage the poles are formed.

In the flattening that follows most of the craters form, and the largest of them: the differentiation has advanced, the ejected material is rich in water, and the poles, already formed, receive more.

In the inward rounding, as the flattening relaxes and the whole body densifies, the craters keep their size and the surface between them closes, most of all at the poles, which gather them (Paper 7) — as buttons sewn on a cloth draw closer together when the cloth is gathered, while the buttons themselves stay the same size.

A prediction for the density of the populations. The belt and the cap lay under the lower dome and received no craters before their exposure; their oldest populations are younger than those of the far side. A far side whose oldest population is the densest, a belt in which it is less dense, and a cap in which it is the sparsest — this is what the rule predicts, and it is stated in Part D together with its test. This is the test that the translation rule hands to the Moon: the order in which the zones were exposed must be read in the order of their oldest craters.

Behind the scenes. In an earlier version of this part the far side closed onto a third of its area, its craters shrinking by a factor of 0.58 and their density rising threefold. The craters were examined directly, by measures that a change of scale would move — the diameter of the transition from simple to complex craters, the largest craters in an equal area, the old against the fresh (Paper 7, §D.5). Small craters are simple bowls; above a certain diameter they develop terraced walls and central peaks, and that transition diameter would have changed if the surface carrying them had been squeezed. Between the far-side highlands and the near-side highlands no difference of scale was found: the transition lies at 16.5–17.1 km on the far side and at 14.3–15.1 km on the near side. The geometry of §§B.2–B.4 follows from this.

B.7The mass and thickness balance

The last question of Part B is whether the crust that the Moon actually has can be made in this way, both in its mass and in its uneven thickness. The observed mass measures. As measured today, the far-side crust — with a mean thickness of about 55 km by GRAIL, at a density of 2,550 kg/m³ over the far hemisphere — has a mass of about 2.6×10212.6\times10^{21} kg, 3.5% of the lunar mass (an earlier version gave 2.7×10212.7\times10^{21} kg and 3.6%; recomputed in Paper 11, §B.3). These are observations of the crust as it is today, not the thickness of a shell in the model. Layered construction. In the model, the far-side crust is built of many layers of compacted sial, laid down layer upon layer on the upper dome from two sources: the sial of the parent, which slid up the pear over the sima and gathered at its tip (§C.10), and the material ejected through the openings, which returned again and again to the far part of the body.

Differentiation and the depth prediction. The ejection never stopped, and the differentiation — the separation of the deep material by density, heavy iron sinking and light, volatile-rich material rising — advanced with time. As it advanced, the material that came out was ever lighter and richer in fluids: in the flattening the outflows were of relatively light terrestrial material, saturated with water. Each layer laid down earlier came out less sorted, and so carries more iron than the layers above it. The far-side crust is therefore layered by age and by sorting, and its iron content rises with depth: the deeper the layer, the earlier it formed, and the richer it is in iron — and the denser. This is a prediction, tested through the composition of crater central peaks against their depth of origin (§D.1).

The overall crust balance. The near side carries a crust of 30–35 km — the peeled sial of the annulus and, over the contact, a crust smoothed by heat and gravity — another about 1.5×10211.5\times10^{21} kg at the same density (an earlier version gave 2×10212\times10^{21} kg, which would bring the total to 6.3%); together they are the 4–6% of the lunar mass that GRAIL models give for the whole crust [4]. The remaining 94–96% of the lunar mass is the deep material of Part A — a split estimated from the earlier shell picture, to be recomputed for the layered crust — and the metal the interior must carry for a 1.4% core is a requirement on the feed of Part A, not on the crust (Paper 11, R2). The balance therefore shows that the crust the two sources build from the outside is a small part of the Moon, and the bulk of the mass is what Part A lifts from within.

The differences in crustal thickness (Figure 9) arise because the two hemispheres were built differently during the climb. The upper dome was exposed throughout, and gathered sial and returning material layer upon layer; the lower dome carried only the sial it sat on, and at its centre lost even that to the heat of the contact. The near side is thinnest where it is opened: the maria are the feed openings of §B.5, and the crust beneath them is thin because they are openings. The far-side crust is the older and the thicker; the boundary between the crusts of the two hemispheres lies at the rim of the lower dome (§B.4).

PART C

The connection and the release

THE PROBLEM

Once the feed has been tested, we must establish what carries the body and when the connection ceases to hold. For this the coupled calculations of mass, spin and latitude are used, distinguishing force, stress and change of shape.

Part A showed that the pressure can feed the body, and Part B followed its surface from the flat stage to the outward rounding. This Part defines the stresses and shear loads acting on the connection between the Earth and the body, and answers the mechanical question that the two previous Parts leave open: what holds the body to the Earth while it rises, and how it is released. The answer is built in steps. Sections C.1–C.3 compute the load the connection must carry at release and show why a narrow neck cannot carry it. Sections C.4–C.5 show how a broad, hot contact carries it, and how the body begins to separate at the line and is released about 210 km beyond it, the contact resisting only a little; §C.5a presents the search for the release distance. Section C.6 hands the release state to Paper 4 (The Orbit). Sections C.7–C.9 describe the structure beneath the contact — one bulge of the Earth drawn into a pear, a part of the Earth held by the Earth’s gravity — with its width, length and mass. Sections C.10–C.11 close with what the body took with it and what the Earth went on doing after release.

Throughout the Part three terms are kept apart. A force is the total pull or push, in newtons. A stress is that force divided by the area that carries it, in pascals (one gigapascal, GPa, is about ten thousand times the air pressure at sea level). A change of shape is what a material does when the stress on it is not balanced. A wide connection can carry a large force at a small stress, and the same force can tear a narrow connection.

C.1 The cap does not carry the whole source pressure

The question of this section is simple, and easily answered wrongly: does the full pressure of the deep source push on the outer cap of the body? The answer is no. In hydrostatic equilibrium — the state in which, at every depth, the pressure of the fluid carries exactly the weight of what lies above it — the pressure varies continuously along the flow path. Its maximum is at the deep source, and it falls upward, because at each step it has already carried the weight of the column above. The deep source pressure (about 150 GPa, Part A) must therefore not be inserted as a uniform pressure difference across the outer cap.

The scale of self-gravity. A useful scale is the pressure that the body produces by its own weight. For a homogeneous spherical body of lunar mass at a density of 2,600 kg/m³ one obtains a radius of about 1,890 km, and the pressure at its centre, due to its own gravity alone, is given by the uniform-sphere equation:

pc=3Gm28πRb4≃3.37GPa.p_c=\frac{3Gm^2}{8\pi R_b^4}\simeq3.37\ \mathrm{GPa}. \tag{18}

Here GG is the gravitational constant, mm the lunar mass and RbR_b the body’s radius. This is the familiar result for a uniform sphere: each layer is pressed by the weight of the layers above it, and the sum reaches its largest value at the centre. In words: the body’s own gravity alone compresses its centre to about 3.37 GPa — roughly forty times less than the pressure the deep source holds. Most of the pressure that reaches the body has therefore been spent on lifting the column beneath it.

The hydrostatic pressure decreases toward the boundary. However, an envelope of finite thickness, inflow, tides and non-spherical geometry can impose loads on that boundary. The pressure difference and the local stresses must therefore be computed, and must not be assumed in advance to be zero. For this reason the following sections compute the load of the connection directly from the motion of the body, rather than from the source pressure.

C.2 What does the connection require at non-zero latitude?

The body is attached to the Earth and turns with it. To keep corotating at a fixed place, the connection must supply at every moment the components of acceleration that the circular motion requires and that gravity and the rotation do not balance. This section computes that remainder at the moment of release.

The two-body system. Define rr as the distance between the centres, K=G(ME+m)K=G(M_E+m) and mr=MEm/(ME+m)m_r=M_Em/(M_E+m). C.3How corotation detaches the body

Corotation is what detaches the body, but the corotation line does not act as an instantaneous switch at a single point; it is the place where the radial requirement on the connection changes sign. This section explains what happens at the line. In the equatorial plane, and in the point-body approximation, the change of sign of ω2r−K/r2\omega^2r-K/r^2 changes the direction of the required support: inside the line the connection must hold the body against the Earth’s inward pull, and beyond it — against the outward fling. At the line itself the requirement passes through zero. At non-zero latitude, even when ar=0a_r=0, aλ≠0a_\lambda\ne0 remains. In the release state this point is the line itself, 17,287 km at 22.5°, and the connection load there is still 4.06×10224.06\times10^{22} newtons.

The corotation line is therefore the place where the direction of the radial requirement turns over, and it is also the place where the body begins to separate. The connection gives way a little beyond it, once it can no longer transmit the required force, torque and momentum flux. High temperature and melting may reduce its resistance to deformation; the pressure can contribute to the push; and the rounding changes the shape of the contact and the stress path. Heating, pressure and rounding act together in the same system. The sections that follow show how they act together at the line: the line sets where the net radial pull turns outward, and beyond it the body, the hot contact resisting only a little, is pulled outward and released about 210 km past the line (§C.5–C.5a).

C.4 The outward rounding and a broad connection

Rounding is a combined response of the shape to self-gravity, tidal stretching, spin and rheology (the way a material flows under load). A body held together by its own gravity tends to become a ball; the Earth’s tide pulls it into an elongated shape along the line joining the two; the spin flattens it; and its viscosity sets how fast it can follow these pulls. The inequality:

GmRb2>2GMERbr3\frac{Gm}{R_b^2}>\frac{2GM_ER_b}{r^3} \tag{23}

gives a local estimate of the competition between the body’s self-gravity and the tidal stretching exerted by the Earth. The left side is the body’s own gravitational acceleration at its surface, which holds it together and tends to round it. The right side is the tidal stretch across the body: the Earth pulls the near side harder than the centre, and the centre harder than the far side, and the difference over one body radius is 2GMERb/r32GM_ER_b/r^3. When the left side wins, the body holds itself; when the right side wins, the body is held only by what it rests on. At stage 2, on the wide tip, the ratio of the two is about 0.11 (§B.2): the body is still a dome on the tip, not yet a body of its own. Survival in orbit and the tearing of the connection are the two conditions Paper 2 places on the released body, and this inequality is where they enter.

A broad connection against a pinch. The broad connection can stretch and separate while flowing and deforming, without first narrowing into a neck. This is the central difference from a pinch mechanism: a drop leaving a syringe thins to a thread and snaps, whereas a wide, soft layer pulled in two opposite directions opens across its whole width at once (§C.5).

The order of the shape changes. The order of the shape changes is fixed. The body completed its outward rounding long before release, while it still turned with the Earth; it then flattened with the spin, and the flattening and the inward rounding that relaxed it both happened while the body was attached. In the inward rounding the axis ratio fell from 1.69 at the peak of the flattening to a/c≃1.2a/c\simeq1.2 late in the attached period (Paper 7, The Poles). By release the body was round, and its outer shell rigid (Paper 2, §B.7: an axis ratio of at most 1.04). The detailed stresses and the evolution of the geometry remain to be solved. The contact in this picture is the 3,000-km disc that remains of the body’s lower dome at stage 3 (Part B.4); its width, a radius of 1,500 km, is the RcR_c of the release calculation in §C.5.

Why the broad base can do the work. Two things change when the connection is the broad base of the body rather than a neck. The load is spread over a contact disc of radius 1,500 km, so the mean stress falls to the 5.74 GPa of the table in §C.2, instead of rising as the radius shrinks. And beneath the disc the support is not a thread but a wide part of the Earth itself, the pear of §C.7, held by the Earth’s gravity. The contact only has to pass the load into the pear, over a short distance; the pear carries it down to the Earth.

C.4a Peeling: the body leaves with the sial it sat on

This section describes what happens over the parts of the lower dome that are not the final contact. The separation from the parent takes place in two ways at once, and these define today’s crust. The body sat from the start on the parent’s sialic layer — the light outer layer, rich in silicon and aluminium (§C.10) — it grew on it, and it left with it. The picture is a deflated football or basketball pressed onto the rounded top of a stand and inflated while still on the stand: as it swells and rounds, the wall that touches the stand peels away from it, from the edge inward, and rounds with the rest of the ball. Here the stand is the tip of the pear.

Over the 6,000-km base the separation is a peeling. As the body rounded outward (§C.4) the contact narrowed from the rim of the lower dome, 6,000 km across, to the 3,000-km disc (§B.4); the peeling is slow, and the disc stays attached to the end. Across that annulus — the ring between the 6,000-km rim and the 3,000-km disc — the body did not tear from the parent. Instead, the sial beneath it gradually peeled from the sima (the denser, magnesium-rich layer beneath the sial), along the “lubricated” boundary between the two layers (§C.10), and went with the body. That peeled sial is today’s belt between 31∘31^\circ and 70∘70^\circ from the contact centre (§B.4).

The 3,000-km contact is a hot mechanism. At the centre of the lower dome the body sat directly on the top of the pear, and there the heat was extreme: it melted the sial layer and removed it. The last separation there may not have been clean, but the heat and the body’s self-gravity smoothed the whole region. Where the near-side crust is thinnest, it is opened: the maria are the feed openings of §B.5, and the crust beneath them is thin because they are openings.

And the release itself. When the Moon’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line, the body begins to separate; the corotation forces pull it outward with force, and the last of the contact, resisting only a little, gives way about 210 km beyond the line (§C.5, C.5a, C.8). The body then leaves with its sial: the shell over the dome, the peeled sial over the annulus, and over the contact a surface already smoothed by heat.

The body thus separates in two ways at once: a slow peeling over the ring, in which the sial goes with it, and a pull over the central disc, in which the hot contact resists only a little. The second is estimated now.

C.5 How the body is released beyond the corotation line

Paper 2 places the release where the Moon’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line: beyond it the corotation forces pull the body outward. The separation is not an instantaneous detachment at a single point, but a continuous flow of the viscous contact layer under radial tension. The Moon begins to separate as its centre of mass reaches the line, and is released about 210 km beyond it, when a little more than half of it has crossed the line (§C.5a). The contact between the pear and the body resists only a little; the body is pulled outward with force, and this pull drew up the spatula ridges. This section estimates how much the contact resisted.

Why the centre of mass, and why the line. A body that moves as one unit responds to the sum of the forces acting on it, and that sum acts as if all its mass were concentrated at its centre of mass. Inside the corotation line the Earth’s pull on the body is larger than what its circular motion requires, so the whole body is pressed toward the Earth and the contact is in compression: it is pushed, not pulled, and it holds. The far side of the body reaches the line first (the table of the tip in §C.7), but as long as the centre lies inside, the parts within the line outweigh the parts beyond it and the body is still pressed inward. When the centre crosses the line the sum reverses: the outward fling exceeds the pull, and the body begins to pull on the contact. From then on the contact is in tension, and the question becomes how a hot, viscous layer behaves when it is pulled.

What Stefan release means. The resistance of the contact to tension is not the tearing strength of a solid but a resistance to the rate at which the gap opens. Two flat glass plates with a thin film of water between them are easy to slide but hard to pull straight apart. To open the gap, liquid must flow in from the edges toward the centre, and the thinner the gap, the harder it flows. Pulled slowly, the plates come apart slowly at first; once the gap has widened a little, the liquid flows in easily and they part at once. The resistance is not a strength that breaks; it is a resistance to the rate of opening, set by the viscosity of the film — its resistance to flow — by the size of the plates and by the width of the gap. This is Stefan’s problem, and the contact is such a film on a very large scale.

Why the body did not slide to the equator. There remains the question of the spatial stability of the attached body: why it did not slide toward the equator but kept a fixed latitude until detachment. A body resting on a parent that turns once in 5.5 hours feels a centrifugal force — the outward fling of the rotation. At the equator that force is wholly radial; at latitude φ\varphi it has a tangential component along the ground, ω2rsinφcosφ\omega^2 r\sin\varphi\cos\varphi, directed toward the equator. This is the same component as aλa_\lambda of equation (19), seen from the other side: there it is what the connection must supply, here it is what would move the body if nothing supplied it. On a fluid parent a body at 22.5° would slide to the equator and separate from there, with zero inclination. Two anchoring mechanisms, one after the other, prevented this.

On the wide tip — anchoring in the shell. The body rests on a base 6,000 km wide, within an opening in the rigid sialic shell. That is the shell the model requires in any case, at a viscosity of 102310^{23} Pa s or more, for the crater record to survive on it (§B.6). A shell that holds craters holds position: a base anchored over such an area does not migrate. A shell stiff enough that a 100-km crater does not relax in ten million years (§B.3) is far too stiff to let a base 6,000 km wide slide across it.

In the outward rounding — the support of the pear. The contact shrinks to 3,000 km, but the body still rests on the tip of the pear, a part of the Earth held by the Earth’s gravity, and the sideways load is carried through it down to the Earth (§C.4, §C.7). The body does not drift toward the equator.

Conclusion. The body is released at the latitude at which it was carried, the latitude of release of Paper 4, 22.5°. The inclination is the tilt of the Moon’s orbit against the Earth’s equator; a body released at a latitude starts on an orbit tilted by that latitude. The base of the pear stood roughly on the equator of that time; whether that equator is today’s is left to the axes, which are future work.

C.5a How far beyond the line: the release distance

Section C.5 found that the body is released a short distance beyond the corotation line. This section puts a number on that distance, examines its effect on the inclination and the eccentricity of the present lunar orbit, and asks within what range the reduced laws of the series place it. The answer is the release the series adopts: about 210 km beyond the line, when a little more than half the Moon had crossed it. The question matters because the distance is small against the radius — less than one per cent of it — but it is not small for the shape of the orbit. Release exactly on the line gives a circular orbit: the body moves at the speed of a circular orbit and enters a circle. Release beyond the line gives the body a little more sideways speed than a circle needs: the release point becomes the nearest point of the orbit, the perigee, and the orbit is a slightly stretched ellipse. C.7 One bulge: the Earth drawn into a pear whose tip becomes the Moon

This section describes the structure that carries the body from beneath: what it is, why it has the shape of a pear, how wide and long it is, and how much mass it holds.

At release the body’s centre lies on the corotation line, about 16,400 km from the Earth’s centre on the equator, and its near surface about 14,500 km from the centre (the centre less the body’s radius of 1,890 km). The distance between the Earth’s surface — 6,701 km from the centre on the equator of the flattened parent at a 5.77-hour day — and that surface is about 7,800 km. This space must be filled by an attached structure, because a body that left the Earth’s surface with the rotation alone would carry less than half the orbital speed and fall back (§C.8). We propose that the Earth itself fills this space, with one bulge: the pressure that raised the hill went on raising it, and the bulge was drawn out into a pear of the Earth’s own material, whose tip — the dome of Part B — rounded and became the Moon. The spin, the sorting of the Earth’s interior and the internal pressure, which draw the pear outward, act as one mechanism; its three faces are set out below, under The pear before this paper.

Why a strand cannot, and a pear can. A liquid strand between two bodies cannot carry the body: the joint gravity of the Earth and the body does pull displaced material back toward the line between them — in every direction and at every height — but for a strand 3,000–4,200 km wide that restoring pull holds only a tenth to a fiftieth of the pressure needed near its base, 35–81 GPa. The pear is not a strand. It is a wide part of the Earth itself, and it is held by the Earth’s gravity like any other part of the Earth, as the Earth holds its mountains. It is not thrown out, because its speed is not a throwing speed and it lies wholly inside the corotation line. Near the Earth it is broad, and it narrows in one continuous arc toward its tip, which carries the contact at about 15,400 km from the centre — the Moon’s centre at 17,287 km less its radius of 1,890 km (an earlier version gave 16,100 km and 9,500 km for the top of the tip). What became of the pear. The pear was not thrown into orbit and was not lost at release. The mass did not leave the Earth; it moved from high outside to deep inside, and it left wrinkles on the surface where it entered.

Behind the scenes. We first looked for a narrow strand between the Earth and the body; such a strand cannot carry the load. We then drew the pear with a long neck reaching beyond the corotation line, released when the tip crossed it. When the release was placed where the Moon’s centre of mass reaches the line (Paper 2), the neck lost its role: the pear is one continuous arc from the Earth’s surface to the contact, wholly inside the line.

The pear before this paper. The piriform (pear-like) shape is not new to physics. Poincaré [8] found in 1885 that the sequence of rotating fluid equilibria passes from the Maclaurin spheroid to the Jacobi ellipsoid and branches there into a pear-shaped, or piriform, figure; he also suggested that a pear might divide into two unequal bodies. A Maclaurin spheroid is the flattened, round-in-plan shape of a uniform fluid body that spins slowly; a Jacobi ellipsoid is the elongated shape, with three unequal axes, that such a body takes when it spins faster; and the pear branches from the Jacobi shape at faster spin still. Darwin applied the idea to the Earth and the Moon, and the Poincaré–Darwin fission theory followed [9]. In that theory the pear is stretched by rotation alone, and so requires a spin close to break-up — the angular-momentum objection that ended it; Cartan later showed that dynamical instability sets in at the point where the pear branches from the Jacobi sequence [10], interrupting the slow evolution the theory assumed. The pear proposed here differs in what drives it. The driver is one mechanism with three faces. The spin creates centrifugal pressure. The Earth’s sorting (differentiation) tends to concentrate the heavy material, and in doing so presses everything else outward: this is the internal pressure. And it is the spin that drives the sorting. Together they make the anomaly that is the pear.

The pear forms at a 5.5-hour spin whose rotation parameter, Ω2/πGρ≃0.092\Omega^2/\pi G\rho\simeq0.092 for the homogeneous parent (an earlier version gave 0.093), is far below the point at which the Jacobi branch begins. The rotation parameter is a dimensionless measure that compares the spin with the body’s own gravity; the comparison must be made on one scale. On the scale Ω2/(πGρ)\Omega^2/(\pi G\rho) used here, the Jacobi branch begins only at 0.374 (Paper 2, §A.6.5), so the parent’s rotation value, 0.092, is only about a quarter of it. The value 0.187, often quoted for the same branch point, is the same point on the scale Ω2/(2πGρ)\Omega^2/(2\pi G\rho), and is not to be compared with 0.092 directly. The 5.5-hour parent is therefore far from the spin at which rotation alone would make it a pear. The pear here grows from one local region rather than from the whole body; only its tip leaves, once the tip’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line, and the rest is gathered back into the Earth by sorting. An unstable equilibrium is no obstacle to a figure that is driven and then released. A pear cannot form in a body that is already sorted and slow, as the Earth is today: such a body has settled, and nothing can leave it. At the time of the pear the spin was fast, the viscosity low and the sorting only beginning; under these conditions pears form, and from them moons.

Closer to the present account, several authors have proposed that the Moon left a more slowly rotating Earth with help from inside. De Meijer and colleagues [13] proposed a nuclear explosion near the core–mantle boundary of a proto-Earth turning in about 5.8 hours; Edwards [14] proposed that heat and volatiles accumulated above the proto-large low-velocity provinces until an explosion launched that material, with the mantle above it, into a low orbit, where it coalesced into the Moon. Lock and Stewart [15] showed that a hot, expanded Earth reaches its corotation limit and forms an extended structure beyond it; and Denton and colleagues [16] found that Charon, attached to Pluto after a collision, had its centre of mass beyond Pluto’s corotation radius before the two separated. The account here shares an internal driver with the first two and the boundary at the corotation line with the last two, but differs from all of them in its course: no explosion and no cloud of debris, but one connected body, raised gradually by the pressure, whose tip begins to separate when its centre of mass reaches the corotation line and is released just beyond it.

What drives the pear outward: a flow, then a trapped pressure. The pear grows in two phases, set by the state of the openings on the far side of the body. While those openings are open, the pressure escapes through them — but not without effect: it sets up a stream along the whole structure, from the deep source through the pear and the body to the far-side exits. That stream builds the far side and its crater record (Paper 6, The Craters), carries material to the outer end of the pear, and keeps the narrow part hot. The far-side openings then seal. We propose that two causes acted together: the openings were exposed to the cold of space, and, as the pear grew, the far part of the body moved away from the Earth’s focus and received less of the Earth’s heat. The result is a chain reaction: the heat diminishes, the far-side maria seal, fewer exits remain, the pressure rises and the pear lengthens. Most of the far-side maria sealed in this way and formed a kind of closed cap, which the pressure pushes from within. From then on the pressure is trapped, and with the weight of the rising material tending to zero at the corotation line, the pear accelerates outward. The reason is the same as in §C.5: near the line, gravity and the outward fling nearly balance, so the material there weighs almost nothing, and a trapped pressure that no longer escapes can push it out easily.

Unlike the far side, the near-side base of the pear faces the hot Earth and does not seal while the body is attached. At detachment the Earth’s pressure stopped reaching the Moon, and with it the Moon’s internal pressure and the ejections stopped. The large near-side maria were then hot and of low viscosity, which is why their surfaces are smooth, like a frozen lake; they solidified afterwards, slowly and from the outside inward, over hundreds of millions of years to one or two billion — Serenitatis first, very close to the Earth, and Imbrium after detachment. The craters on them are mostly small: after release, small and medium drops thrown by the Earth were drawn to the Moon and made them (§C.11). The old openings of the far side had sealed before release. One far-side mare is an exception: Moscoviense stayed active long after the others had sealed. The author proposes that it was fed from Serenitatis, while Serenitatis was still internal, along a straight passage through the interior between the two, and that this is why it remained active for so long.

C.9What the pear requires

The pear is a large structure, and the model must account for its cost — in mass, in energy and in angular momentum. This section collects its requirements; each is a condition the mechanism places on itself.

  • Mass. For the widths of §C.7, at a density of 3,000 kg/m³, the structure from the Earth’s surface to the contact holds about 11% of the Earth’s mass — about nine lunar masses, for the 9,000-km base of §C.7; for bases 9,000–11,000 km wide it holds about nine to twelve lunar masses. Since the whole structure lies inside the corotation line, no residue of the connection is left in orbit; after release the sorting gathers it back into the Earth over tens to hundreds of millions of years, and the Earth becomes round again: its heavy part sinks deep into the Earth beneath the base of the pear (Part E), and its front and back are gathered into the stripes and arcs of the Pacific (Paper 9; §C.7).

  • Energy cost. Lifting the mass of the pear in the rotating field costs a work of about 8×10308\times10^{30} J — more than five times (5.4 times) the work needed to lift the Moon itself (an earlier version said about six times) (§A.12). The evolution that releases this energy — the descent of the iron into the core, which frees energy of order 103110^{31} J — is the same that drives the upwelling; the full account is passed on as a requirement (§D.1). As the iron sinks, the gravitational energy it gives up is enough, in order of magnitude, to lift the pear.

  • Angular momentum and heat. The lengthening of the pear slows the Earth’s spin, while the centralising core speeds it up; Paper 2 (§A.4) takes both into account. On its return the pear releases its potential energy as heat, which enters the thermal history of the parent.

  • Pressure. The source must lift and hold the pear as well as the body; the pressure test of §A.9–A.13 is to be repeated with the pear’s mass.

  • Correction for the parent’s flattening. The pressure test is made on a spherical field. In fact the flattening of the parent (J2≃0.036J_2\simeq0.036) increases the inward gravitational pull by about 3% at 9,000 km from the centre on the equator, by 1.4% at latitude 25°N, and by 5.4% at the equatorial surface itself, where the column leaves the parent (an earlier version said “at most 3% near the surface”). Along the outer column the cost rises by about 3%; and even if the whole cost of the lift rose by 3%, about 4 GPa, the pressure margin of 10.49 GPa would carry it. The flattening moves the equatorial corotation line outward by about 50 km. (J2J_2 is the standard measure of a planet’s equatorial bulge in its gravity field; the value here is that of the spinning parent.)

  • The shape itself. The widths and masses above rest on round cross-sections of uniform density, without flow; the equilibrium figure of a rotating Earth, under internal pressure and carrying a body at its tip, is the calculation that remains.

C.9aNo ring around the young Earth

A young, fast, volatile-rich planet that vents steam through its fissures can surround itself with a ring: the steam rises, condenses higher up into ice and dust, and circles the planet at orbital speed. Around the young Earth of this model no such ring could form, for four physical and mechanical reasons.

  1. The pear swept the place. The pear turned with the Earth once a day, out to the corotation line. Inside that line, free ring material would move at an orbital speed higher than the pear’s speed of rotation; the dust and vapour particles would therefore overtake the pear and strike it. Whatever condensed in the band of the pear was gathered onto it and became material of the body developing at its tip.

  2. The volatiles had a road. The broad, hot connection carried the volatile-rich melt up into the body (Part A). What a venting planet loses to space, the Earth fed into the Moon.

  3. Too hot, too close to the Sun. The young Earth was hot and wrapped in a silicate-vapour atmosphere (Paper 6, §J.3); at the heights where an orbital ring could have settled, water did not condense to ice.

  4. The Moon clears the rest. After release the Moon — a massive body lying about 7,200 km beyond the rigid Roche limit (§C.5a) — would remove any remnant in orbit by its tidal forces and its orbital resonances.

The Moon carries the record of this absence. A body that passes through a ring undergoes mechanical abrasion: its hollows are worn shallow, its rims rounded, its small craters erased, and the mass of the body itself reduced — the smooth surface of Deimos is one example. The lunar highlands keep a saturated crater record, and the Moon kept its mass: it never passed through a ring (Paper 6, §L.8).

C.10 What the body is made of: three layers from one pear

The pear does not only carry the body; it decides what the body is made of. This section follows each of the three layers of the Moon — crust, mantle and core — back to its place of origin in the pear.

The crust: the sial slides to the tip. The pear sorts what it carries. Over the construction region the parent’s outer layer is sialic — light and rich in silicon and aluminium — and it lies on the denser, magnesium-rich sima beneath. The sial was not dry: the boundary between the two layers was “lubricated”, and as the pear was drawn out the sial slid up the pear over the sima, along its flanks, to the tip — in the direction of outward sorting, under the forces of the spin — and the lubricated boundary eased the slide under shear. When the tip was released, the sial went with it and became its outer skin: over the upper dome as the shell, and over the annulus of the lower dome as the sial the body sat on and that peeled away with it (§C.4a); only at the contact was it melted and removed by the heat. The far side carries the thick crust because many layers of compacted sial gathered there, with the material that returned again and again (§B.7). Part of the sial did not stay on the surface but entered the Moon’s interior.

The mantle and the core: the settling column. Beneath the skin, the body is the feed: the Earth’s own mantle, lifted by the pressure and carried along the pear. All the Moon’s metal comes from the Earth. Much iron entered the pear, and less of it toward its tip. Since the whole pear lies inside the corotation line, the effective gravity along it points toward the Earth; so the heavy material sank back down the pear while the light material rose to the tip — and the pressure lifts only material lighter than about 3,864 kg/m3^3 in the first place (§A.10). The pear worked as a settling column: heavy grains sink and light ones rise, as sand settles below water in a shaken jar. Most of the iron that entered the pear therefore returned to the Earth with the rest of the pear after release; the Moon kept only what had reached the tip. At detachment the heavy core material was in the last third of the body; it did not cross the corotation line, and it went back down the pear. This is why the Moon’s core is small: it is terrestrial iron that reached the tip, while most of the core material in the pear returned to the Earth. The stream that ran along the pear carried heat and iron-rich material into the body through Mare Imbrium and Mare Serenitatis — Serenitatis until it peeled, Imbrium until detachment — the openings that joined the feed from the Earth’s interior to the inside of the Moon (§B.4).

C.11 After release: the Earth goes on throwing

Release did not end the Earth’s activity. Its openings went on acting as centres of ejection and throwing material; most of it fell back to the Earth, and part of it reached the Moon, then a separate body receding from a distance of a few Earth radii. As the Moon receded, the material had to leave the Earth faster to reach it. All of it was very hot: the large bodies were ejected first, and as the magma storm gradually calmed, small, still hot bodies were ejected — so that the size of the bodies reaching the Moon decreased with time. D.2 Results that can be looked for on the Moon

This section asks which features of today’s Moon the mechanism bears on directly, and how far each can be used as a test.

Thermal and structural asymmetry. If one side received more prolonged feed and heating and the opposite side cooled earlier, a thermal and structural asymmetry between the two hemispheres is expected. In the model the near side sat on the contact, directly over the feed, and stayed hot longer; the far side was the upper dome, exposed to space and cooling. The sign and strength of the asymmetry also depend on the external material, on mixing and on later evolution. GRAIL data — from a pair of spacecraft that mapped the Moon’s gravity field in detail — provide constraints on the density and thickness of the crust [4]; the full comparison between observations and the model’s requirements belongs to Paper 11.

Centre-of-mass offset. An offset of the centre of mass depends on the full density distribution. The Moon’s centre of mass need not coincide with the centre of its outer figure; the distance between them is given by Δ𝐫CM=1m∫Vδρ(𝐫)𝐫dV.\Delta\mathbf r_{\mathrm{CM}}= \frac{1}{m}\int_V\delta\rho(\mathbf r)\,\mathbf r\,dV. \tag{24} Here mm is the Moon’s mass, VV its volume, 𝐫\mathbf r the position of a small volume element dVdV measured from the centre of the figure, and δρ(𝐫)\delta\rho(\mathbf r) the departure of the density at that point from the mean. D.3 Novelty and the scope of the conclusion

The work examines the internal construction of an attached body through a single system of requirements of pressure, rate, mass and stress, within the rotational framework of LEF. The particular result of Part A is that widening the passage eases the radial support and the flow, but increases the mass of the connection and does not remove the lateral load. A wide pipe is easier to push material through and holds its column up more easily; but the wider it is the more it weighs, and the sideways pull of the rotating system on it continues to act. The particular result of Part B is that a body of the lunar mass seated on a 6,000-km contact, its upper dome rising about 3,650 km above the rim, encloses a volume of 5.65×10195.65\times10^{19} m³ — twice the 2.83×10192.83\times10^{19} m³ of the sphere at release — and so has the foamed density Part A requires, 1,300 kg/m³ (the low density of a melt filled with gas bubbles); the far-side crust and the exposure zones follow from the same geometry: the far side built layer upon layer, the near side peeled. Part C joins the two: the broad contact that Part A demands is the base of Part B’s dome, and at the corotation line the body begins to separate, to be released about 210 km beyond it.

Statement of novelty. The equations of gravity, pressure and flow are standard physics. The contribution of this work is the LEF architecture and the application of the joint test. No historical priority is claimed here for every component of the model without a dedicated literature review; the map of sources and classification appears in Appendix A.

The paper ends with a feasibility analysis, one sharp negative result — a massless cylindrical passage transmitting pressure alone cannot build the Moon — and one geometric result that answers it: the broad, hot, massive connection the negative result demands is the wide tip of the pear, a part of the Earth held by the Earth’s gravity, carrying a body of the Moon’s mass at the foamed density. The negative result closes the simplest picture — a thin pipe through which pressure alone pushes the Moon out; the geometric result shows that the connection the physics does demand is not an extra assumption but the shape the Earth itself takes. The deep equation of state, the energy release and the three-dimensional structure are the three requirements the mechanism hands on, each stated with its test in §D.1.

PART E

What the pear left in the Earth

THE PROBLEM

The pear was gathered back into the Earth, carrying most of the iron that had risen into it. Where did the pear stand relative to the deep Earth seen today, and what did it do to the Earth’s interior while it stood?

The Moon left; the pear stayed. Since all the material of the pear lies inside the corotation line, it could not follow the Moon into orbit. After release the sorting gathered it back into the half of the Earth that had carried it, over tens to hundreds of millions of years. Its mass did not leave the Earth; it moved from high outside to deep inside. So large a mass — nine to twelve lunar masses of the Earth’s own material, iron-rich at depth — was gathered into the region beneath the Pacific where the pear stood. This Part records where the base of the pear lies relative to what seismic images show today at the bottom of that region, and what the pear did to the Earth’s interior while it stood. The nature of the deep provinces is left open.

E.1Two provinces at the base of the mantle

The deep Earth is imaged by seismic tomography. Earthquakes send waves through the whole planet; recorded at thousands of stations, their arrival times reveal where the waves moved faster or slower than average — much as a medical CT scan builds an image of the body from many X-ray paths. Shear waves (S waves), which shake the material sideways, are especially sensitive to temperature and composition: they slow down where the material is hotter, softer or of a different make-up.

Seismic imaging finds at the base of the Earth’s mantle two continent-sized provinces in which the shear-wave velocity is low: one beneath Africa and one beneath the Pacific. Each extends thousands of kilometres laterally and rises up to about 1,000 km above the core–mantle boundary [11]. These are the large low-velocity provinces (LLVPs): large regions of the lowermost mantle, just above the core, where shear waves travel slowly. Most observations favour a thermochemical nature — material that differs in composition from the mantle around it, not only in temperature — and both provinces stand beneath the plumes and hotspots of the plates above them: they press on the upper mantle today. A plume is a column of hot material rising from deep in the mantle; where it reaches the surface it feeds a hotspot, such as the volcanoes of Hawai‘i.

The provinces are not alike. The African province rises higher, while the Pacific one is lower and broader; at its margin, beneath Hawai‘i, lies iron-rich material of ultralow velocity. Ultra-low-velocity zones (ULVZs) are thin patches on the core–mantle boundary in which seismic waves slow far more strongly than in the large provinces themselves; a high iron content is one of the readings proposed for them.

The nature of the provinces is still debated. They may, for example, be material related to the core that did not finish its sorting, or material that is still sorting very slowly. This work does not propose a reading of them; it uses them only as a map of the region beneath the Pacific where the pear stood.

E.2 Where the pear stood

The pear stood over the region beneath the Pacific. After release the sorting gathered it back into the Earth: its heavy part — about nine lunar masses of sima carrying most of the iron that had risen into it (§C.10) — sank deep into the Earth beneath the base of the pear; its leading side was gathered into the stripes of the North Pacific, and its back into the arcs of the western Pacific (Paper 9, The Stripes). Sima is the denser material, rich in silica and magnesium, that lies beneath the light sial; the iron it carried made it denser still. Whether any of the deep structure seen today beneath the Pacific is related to the pear is not addressed here: the paper records only where the base of the pear lies relative to that structure (§E.4).

E.3Other readings of the provinces

The provinces have recently been proposed as remnants of the mantle of Theia — the body whose impact formed the Moon in the accepted account: its iron-rich mantle would have sunk to the base of the Earth’s mantle and survived there [12]. The author of that proposal has noted that a different origin of the Moon, one without Theia, would require the origin of the provinces to be reconsidered. Edwards [14] has connected the Moon to the provinces from another direction: an explosion above the proto-Pacific province that launched its material into orbit. This work does not offer a reading of its own. It notes only that in this model the pear was the Earth’s own material, and nothing foreign entered the Earth’s mantle.

E.4The position of the base

One measurement relates the pear to the deep Earth: the position of its base.

  • Position — measured. The model places the base of the pear near Kiritimati (about 1.9°N, 157.5°W, §B.1). That point lies over the Pacific province, inside its boundary in the published lowermost-mantle models, in every model tested (table and Figure 14): all five tomographic models of the cluster analysis of Cottaar & Lekic [17] vote the point slow, and it lies well inside the −1%-1\% contour of both SMEAN [18] and SMEAN2 [19], at a depth of 2,800 km, more than 1,200 km from the nearest edge. The base stands over the northern part of the province, some 1,500 km north of its centre; the large ultra-low-velocity zone near Hawaii [20] lies about 1,100 km from the point, the distance measured at the core–mantle boundary.

Each of the three measures used in the test needs a brief explanation. Different research groups build different tomographic models from different data and methods, and they do not agree in every detail. Vote maps turn this disagreement into a count: Cottaar & Lekic [17] sorted each point of the lowermost mantle, in each of five models, into slow, neutral or fast, and the vote is the number of models that call the point slow; five of five means that every model places it inside a slow province. SMEAN and SMEAN2 are averages of several shear-wave models; δVS\delta V_S is the departure of the shear-wave speed from the average at that depth, so −2.25%-2.25\% means that waves there travel 2.25% slower than average. The −1%-1\% contour is the usual outline of the province, and a point well inside it, with the edge more than a thousand kilometres away, is not a marginal case.

The longitude 202.5°E in the table is the same as 157.5°W, written on a scale that runs eastward from 0 to 360°. The base is the eastern end of the pear’s footprint. The earlier reading of the source region, centred near 25°N, 165°E, is the back of the pear, which leaned westward and north (§B.1). Paper 9 (The Stripes) locates the imprint of the tip between two bends of the stripes of the North Pacific, 3,360 km wide and centred at 9.3°N, 169.3°E, about 3,760 km west of the base (an earlier version gave 3,770 km), and the back of the pear in the arcs of the western Pacific. A geochemical prediction follows: plumes rising from either of the two provinces are expected to carry the Earth’s own isotopic signature, with no component foreign to the Earth. Isotopes are versions of the same element that differ slightly in mass; their ratios differ from body to body in the solar system and so act as a fingerprint of origin.

E.5The Earth’s core during the pear

The pear did not act only on the surface: while it stood, it also acted on the Earth’s interior. The question of this section is what so large an attached mass did to the deep interior of the Earth, and whether a record of it could survive.

The initial state. At that early stage the Earth had almost no core. It was a forming Earth: its layers were not yet defined, only the first signs of layering had appeared, and at its centre lay a broad pre-core — material that would later become the core, not yet separated from what lay around it. As this body turned and began to draw out the pear, the broad pre-core took the same shape: an extended inner pear, reaching toward the outer one.

The pull of the pear. While the pear stood, seven to twelve lunar masses of the Earth’s own material stood on one side of the Earth, between its surface and some 15,400 km from its centre (an earlier version gave 16,000 km) (§C.7 gives 8.8–12.1 lunar masses at release for bases of 9,000–11,000 km). Their gravity field reaches the centre. The pre-core is denser than the material around it, so in a field that acts on the whole interior it is pulled and stretched toward the pear, as a weight in a liquid is, until the pull of the material it displaces balances it. Because the pear is part of the Earth and not a separate body, the Earth as a whole does not move toward it; its field acts on the interior as a steady field.

The table collects the fields at the Earth’s centre at the start of the pear and at release, for the narrowest and the widest base. The net field is the combination of the pull of the pear and the small pull of the Moon, less the centrifugal term: the Earth, the pear and the Moon turn together about their common centre of mass, which lies off the Earth’s centre toward the pear, and this turning gives the Earth’s centre an acceleration directed away from the side of the pear. A displacement in kilometres is not given here, and cannot be: it would require a defined core, a defined mantle around it and a stiffness between them, and at that stage none of these existed. The table gives only the pull and its direction. We propose that the inner pre-core stretched toward the pear for as long as the pear stood — a large part of the roughly 600 million years from the start of the bulge to release at 3.873 Ga. The direction rests on one assumption: that the deficit left at the deep source was filled and spread by the surrounding material as the feed rose; a deficit left in place beside the centre would push the other way.

Compensation and the relaxation of the pre-core. After release the sorting gathered the pear back into the Earth, gradually, over tens to hundreds of millions of years, and its material spread over the half of the Earth to which it had been attached. Had this layer remained uncompensated, it would have gone on pulling the interior toward that half — the field of such a layer at the centre, GM/2R2GM/2R^2, is 0.53–0.73 m/s². Here MM is the mass of the layer and RR the Earth’s radius; the expression is the pull, at the centre of a sphere, of a thin layer covering one half of its surface. But a light layer laid on a fluid mantle does not stay uncompensated: the mantle beneath it adjusts, and the far field of a compensated load is small. Compensation is the process by which the mantle flows away from beneath an added surface load until the column below each point weighs the same again, as a raft settles in water until it displaces its own weight; the added mass is then balanced by a deficit beneath it, and seen from the centre the two nearly cancel. The inner pre-core then relaxes back toward the centre. In short: while the pear stood the pre-core stretched toward it, and after the pear was gathered back it relaxed.

The test. The test is stated as a requirement and an opening for research (Paper 12): a hemispherical structure of the core aligned with the side of the pear, of which the east–west asymmetry of the inner core is the first candidate. Seismic waves crossing the solid inner core travel at different speeds in its eastern and western halves; if the pre-core stretched toward the pear while the core was forming, the growing core may have recorded the side. The test is weakened if the inner core is much younger than the Moon, for a later inner core would not carry the record.

What we now have is the full fate of the pear: after release the sorting gathered it back into the Earth, and its heavy part sank beneath its base, over a region whose deep provinces are seen today and whose nature is left open; the base lies inside the Pacific province in every model examined; and while the pear stood the broad pre-core stretched toward it, and after it was gathered back the pre-core relaxed.

Further work — openings for research

Parts A–C define the boundary conditions and the input parameters for calculations that remain to be performed. The following list identifies work that other research groups are equipped to undertake. Each item names a calculation that this paper sets up but does not perform, and the section whose result it would replace or test.

  1. A three-dimensional simulation of the connected body. The initial state is fully specified: a body of the lunar mass at 1,300 kg/m³ seated on a 6,000-km tip, on a parent of 6,517 km volumetric radius spinning in 5.5 hours, fed through a base contact at a source pressure of 150 GPa. A smoothed-particle (SPH) or finite-element (FEM) run would test the stability of the dome, the rounding sequence of §C.4 and the release margin of §C.5 together. Both methods divide the body into many small elements and follow the forces between them step by step, which is what a free, changing shape of this kind requires.

  2. The equation of state of the deep melt. The feed requires a melt lighter than 3,864 kg/m³ along the ascent (§A.10). Experiments on hydrous and carbonated silicate liquids at 50–150 GPa, combined with first-principles molecular dynamics of the same compositions, would decide whether the requirement is met and at what volatile content. The equation of state is the relation between pressure, temperature and density of a material; water and carbon dioxide dissolved in a melt lower its density.

  3. The energy release of the parent. The lift costs of order 103010^{30} J (§A.12). A thermal-evolution model of the young Earth, in which the iron descends and the Earth contracts and cools under a steam atmosphere, would give the rate at which that energy becomes available and hence the duration of the feed.

  4. Stefan adhesion of a hot silicate layer. The release margin Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0 (§C.5) assumes a Newtonian layer. Laboratory and numerical work on the separation of two bodies joined by a partially molten layer — cavitation (the formation of voids), necking, the transition from viscous to brittle failure — would fix the margin’s range and the release time. Stefan adhesion is the resistance of a thin viscous film to the separation of the two surfaces it joins, the force felt in pulling apart two plates with honey between them; a Newtonian layer is one whose resistance is simply proportional to the rate at which it is deformed.

  5. The layered far-side crust. The far side was built in layers of increasingly sorted material (§B.7). Comparing the composition of central peaks against their depth of origin, separately for the far and the near side, would measure the gradient, and a model of the build would predict its steps.

  6. The Earth’s core during the pear. A three-dimensional hydrostatic model of the young Earth, before it was layered into defined shells, with the pear standing and after it was gathered back, and with the source deficit followed, would show how the broad pre-core stretched toward the pear (§E.5) and how it relaxed afterwards; the hemispherical structure of today’s inner core is the first record to compare.

  7. The near-side crust. The sial of the annulus of the lower dome peeled with the body; over the contact it was melted and removed, and the surface smoothed by heat and gravity (§C.4a). A thermal model of the contact region gives the depth of that melting, and hence the thickness of the crust over the contact against that of the peeled belt.

  8. Mare classification by exposure zone. The openings did not all behave alike (§B.5). Imbrium did not eject material, and Serenitatis did not either, apart perhaps from slight popping. Most of the other openings, of the author’s 34–36, did eject material: the far-side ones early, until they sealed, and the limb ones over a long time. The difference between the non-ejecting contact maria and the ejecting ones is testable in the composition, the secondary-crater fields and the crustal thickness beneath each basin (Paper 11, R8).
