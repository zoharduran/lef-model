01 · THE MYSTERY

The mystery: the thirty-year-old problem of the Moon's orbital inclination

For more than thirty years, the inclination of the Moon's orbit — an angle of 5.145° to the plane of the ecliptic — was considered one of the most stubborn riddles in planetary science. Why is this angle so hard to explain? Because in all the giant-impact theories, the Moon coalesces from a debris disc swirling around the Earth. Such a disc quickly settles into the Earth's equatorial plane, so a moon born from it should begin its life with almost zero tilt — and then late, complicated scenarios are needed (such as encounters with giant planetesimals, orbital resonances or an extreme tilt of the Earth's axis of more than 70°) to tilt the orbit up.

In the LEF model the exact opposite happens: the Moon never coalesced from a flat disc above the equator, but was built on the tip of the pear, which rose to latitude λ=22.5∘\lambda = 22.5^\circ (17,496 km from the Earth's centre, with a 5.77-hour day). From this arise three decisive questions that Paper 04 answers:

  1. What orbit does a body released at latitude 22.5° receive? Does it enter a stable, receding orbit, or does the Earth's tide pull it inward?
  2. What happens to its orbital plane on the way out — from a distance of 17,496 km to the Moon's distance today (384,400 km)?
  3. Does the tidal decay law of the inclination turn the 22.5° of the start into exactly the 5.145° of today?
02 · AN IMAGE

An image: the stone released from the raised seat of a merry-go-round

To see with your own eyes why release from latitude 22.5° immediately creates an orbit inclined at 22.5° — with no free parameter and no extra push — imagine a large merry-go-round whose centre lies at the Earth's centre, and whose gravity always pulls exactly toward that centre.

Place a stone on a ring of seats raised above the floor of the merry-go-round (the "latitude" λ\lambda). While the merry-go-round turns, the stone moves horizontally eastward, parallel to the floor (the "equatorial plane"). Now let the stone go free! At the moment of release it keeps moving horizontally forward, but gravity pulls it down and inward toward the centre of the merry-go-round. Its free orbit must lie in the only plane that contains both its line of motion and the centre of attraction — a plane that slopes down diagonally from the stone and passes through the Earth's centre!

What is the angle between this sloping plane and the floor? Exactly the angle at which the stone is seen from the centre of the merry-go-round above the floor — that is, the latitude λ\lambda itself (i0=∣λ∣i_0 = |\lambda|)! A stone on the floor (λ=0∘\lambda = 0^\circ) stays in the plane of the floor; a stone at latitude 22.5° automatically enters an orbit inclined at 22.5°!

03 · SCIENCE AND MODEL

Science and model: three principles that put the orbit on its feet

Parts A and B of Paper 04 show, in three lines of vector algebra, how the detachment conditions of Chapters 2 and 5 turn automatically into a stable, receding orbit:

  1. The kinematic identity (i0=∣λ∣i_0 = |\lambda|) and the separation of the three angles (§A.2–A.3): for a body released with a purely eastward tangential velocity (v=ωr(0,cos⁡λ,0)\mathbf{v} = \omega r (0, \cos\lambda, 0)), the mass, the distance and the rotation rate cancel completely from the formula, and the exact geometric identity i0=∣λ∣=22.5∘i_0 = |\lambda| = 22.5^\circ results with respect to the early equatorial plane (since an outward radial push at detachment changes the flattening of the ellipse but does not move the orbital plane at all!). At that moment, the 22.5° angle between the Earth's rotation axis and the axis of the Moon's orbit is divided around the total angular-momentum vector (J=S⊕+μrh\mathbf{J} = \mathbf{S}_\oplus + \mu_r \mathbf{h}) into 3.78° on the Earth's side and 18.72° on the Moon's side.
  2. Why release at the equatorial corotation radius (16,399 km) is impossible (§B.1–B.2): at latitude λ=22.5∘\lambda = 22.5^\circ, the body's distance from the rotation axis is smaller than its distance from the Earth's centre by a factor cos⁡λ\cos\lambda. So had the Moon been released at the equatorial corotation distance (16,399 km), its speed would have been too slow: it would have entered an elongated elliptical orbit (e0=sin⁡222.5∘=0.1464e_0 = \sin^2 22.5^\circ = 0.1464) at its apogee point, and the Earth's tide would have dragged it inward!
  3. The local corotation line (17,287 km) and the release margin (17,496 km): at latitude 22.5°, the rotational speed equals the circular orbital speed at a distance of r=rccos⁡−2/3λ=17,287r = r_c \cos^{-2/3}\lambda = \mathbf{17,287} km (x = 1.0542). The separation begins when the centre reaches this line, and the final detachment takes place about 210 km beyond it — at r0=17,496r_0 = 17,496 km (x = 1.0669, speed ratio B = 1.0367). At this point the Moon enters an almost circular orbit (e0≃0.037e_0 \simeq 0.037, semi-major axis a0=18,163a_0 = 18,163 km, apogee 18,830 km) exactly at its perigee, about 7,200 km outside the rigid Roche limit (10,314 km), and the Earth's tide pushes it outward from the very first orbit!
A sharp methodological separation (two reference planes)

The initial angle i0=22.5∘i_0 = 22.5^\circ is measured relative to the Earth's early equatorial plane, while the angle measured today (5.145°) is measured relative to the plane of the ecliptic (the plane of the Earth's orbit around the Sun). How does the orbit pass from the equatorial plane to the plane of the ecliptic, and how much of the initial inclination survives the journey outward? Parts C and D of the paper answer this!

04 · THE MECHANISM IN WORDS

The mechanism in words: the Laplace surface, the tangent law and the clock test

As soon as the Moon begins to recede from a distance of 17,496 km toward its present distance (384,400 km), its orbital plane does not stay frozen in place: external forces slowly turn it (precession) and the tide damps its inclination. Parts C and D of Paper 04 break this journey into three clear stages:

  1. The passage from the equatorial plane to the plane of the ecliptic — the Laplace surface (§C.1–C.3): close to the Earth, the equatorial bulge of the spinning Earth (J2J_2) is much stronger than the Sun's pull, so the Moon's orbital plane precesses around the equatorial plane (the Laplace transition radius for a 5.77-hour day is about 17.07 Earth radii, so the release point at 17,496 km lies deep — only about 16.1% — inside the domain controlled by the equator). As the Moon recedes, the control of the equatorial bulge weakens, the Sun's influence grows, and the "guiding plane" (the Laplace surface) bends gradually from the equatorial plane to the plane of the ecliptic — while on the way the Moon passes through the freezing of its fossil figure and the Cassini states.
  2. The reduced tangent law — why 22.5° becomes 5.145° (§D.1): the same tidal forces that push the Moon outward act at the same time on the orbital plane and reduce its inclination. Solving the reduced decay equation shows that the decrease is not according to sin⁡i\sin i (which is only a small-angle approximation), but according to tan⁡i∝a−1/2\tan i \propto a^{-1/2}: every time the distance grows fourfold, the tangent of the inclination is cut in half!
    When we insert the model's release angle (i0=22.5∘i_0 = 22.5^\circ) and the initial semi-major axis (a0=18,163a_0 = 18,163 km) into the equation and run it out to the Moon's present distance (af=384,400a_f = 384,400 km, a 21.16-fold growth in distance), the tangent of the inclination falls by a factor of about 4.6 — and the final angle converges exactly on 5.145°!
  3. The clock test — why the tidal coefficient Q⊕Q_\oplus must change with time (§D.2–D.3): the tangent law links the inclination to the distance, but it does not fix how long the journey takes. When a simple tidal law with a constant friction coefficient (Q⊕=constQ_\oplus = \text{const}) is applied, it turns out that no single constant value fits both of today's facts together: the value that reproduces today's recession rate (Q⊕≃12→3.70Q_\oplus \simeq 12 \rightarrow 3.70 cm/yr) brings the Moon to its present distance within only 1.60 billion years; while the value that fits the time elapsed since detachment (3.87 billion years at Q⊕≃29Q_\oplus \simeq 29) gives too slow a recession rate today (1.5 cm/yr). This confirms the findings of modern research that energy dissipation in the Earth's oceans has changed over history through basin resonances and continental drift!

06 · THE SIGNATURE

The signature in reality: no free parameter — release from 22.5° hits 5.145°

The scientific strength of Chapter 7 lies in the fact that the latitude λ=22.5∘\lambda = 22.5^\circ and the release distance r0=17,496r_0 = 17,496 km were not chosen to fit the Moon's orbit today — they were fixed in advance from the geometric and mechanical building conditions of Papers 2 and 3 (the position of the pear's tip, the angular-momentum budget, and the Stefan release about 210 km beyond the corotation line).

  • A direct hit on the target: when these initial conditions (i0=22.5∘,a0=18,163i_0 = 22.5^\circ, a_0 = 18,163 km) are inserted into the reduced tangent law (ϵ=0\epsilon = 0), the result obtained at a distance of 384,400 km is 5.145° — identical, to the third decimal place, to the measured inclination of the Moon's orbit to the plane of the ecliptic!
  • Low sensitivity to distance and controlled sensitivity to latitude: increasing the release distance by 5% (to a0=19,151a_0 = 19,151 km) changes the final angle by only 0.14° (to 5.282°), while raising the initial latitude to 25° gives 5.788° — which shows that the geometry of the pear's tip is what dictates the character of the orbit, while a full vector integration through the Laplace and Cassini transitions is the natural next step of the research.
