01 · THE MYSTERY

The mystery: where does the ring material come from, why does only Saturn have enormous rings, and why don't rings build moons?

In Chapters 21–22 we saw that the moons in the solar system are born directly from their parents on the corotation line. In Paper 12 the LEF model extends this principle to planetary ring systems and sets an unambiguous rule: a ring is not a construction site for moons but a mechanism that breaks, grinds and returns material to the parent! From the observations of Cassini, Voyager and the telescopes, four central questions arise:

  1. The extreme differences between the rings of the solar system (A.1–A.3): why are Saturn's rings huge (1.54 × 10¹⁹ kg) and bright (95%–99% pure water ice), Jupiter's rings made of faint dust, Uranus' and Neptune's rings narrow and dark — while around small bodies (Haumea, Chariklo and Quaoar) dense rings orbit far beyond the Roche limit without coalescing into moons?
  2. The paradox of the young age of Saturn's rings (D.3): Cassini measured "ring rain" falling onto Saturn at a rate of 432 to 45,000 kg per second — a rate that would have emptied the rings completely within 11 million to 1.1 billion years. How do the rings exist today?
  3. The universal condition for a massive ring (B.5): why, of all the planets in the solar system, did only Saturn today and young Mars develop a massive ring directly from the ground?
  4. Where did the angular momentum of Uranus and Neptune go (D.4)? The moons of Uranus and Neptune carry only 1% and 0.002% of the angular momentum these planets lost — what carried the rest away?
02 · AN IMAGE

An image: a net and balls, a grinding funnel, and a water fountain in equilibrium

Four tangible images explain the physics and the cycle of rings in the solar system:

  • A net and balls — the corotation line inside the Roche limit (B.5): to capture material as a massive ring around a planet, two things are needed at the same time: a net in the right place and balls thrown into it. When material leaks from the depths of the planet to the corotation line (aca_c), if the line lies inside the Roche limit (ac<aRa_c < a_R), the differential tidal forces prevent it from coalescing into a moon and trap it as a ring. Dividing the two equations cancels the planet's density completely and yields a universal threshold day, identical for every body in the universe: P∗≈13.4P^* \approx 13.4 hours!
  • A viscous funnel that grinds lumps and returns dust home (C.1–C.4, D.1): inside a ring disc, inner particles move faster than outer particles. Inelastic collisions turn orbital energy into heat while conserving the total angular momentum — so most of the material loses energy and flows inward to the parent, while a minority is pushed outward (that is why Saturn's ring A ends exactly at the Roche limit for ice at 135,300 km, and why Quaoar's ring at 2.4 Roche limits does not coalesce into a moon either!).
  • A fountain pool in which the feeding from below equals the rain from above (Saturn's equilibrium, D.3): Saturn's rings are not a static remnant formed recently, but a continuous flow in dynamic equilibrium: the inner pump leaks water to the corotation line (1.92 R) at exactly the same rate at which the ring rain brings water down into the atmosphere (a cumulative cost of up to 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ of Saturn's mass over 4.5 billion years). The "young" appearance comes from the constant turnover of the material!
  • A mechanical pump lifting water from a shallow well (the escape of water from Uranus and Neptune, D.4): Uranus and Neptune are far from the Sun and cold, yet they lost water amounting to 270 times the mass of all their moons (2.5 × 10²⁴ kg at Uranus and 3.2 × 10²⁴ kg at Neptune) — not because of thermal evaporation, but because of mechanical lifting to the corotation line in the fast rotation (7–8 hours), through a thin gas lid (10%–20%) and a shallow gravity well (vesc=21.3–23.5v_{\mathrm{esc}} = 21.3\text{–}23.5 km/s against 59.5 km/s at Jupiter)!

04 · THE MECHANISM IN WORDS

The mechanism in words: deriving the universal threshold day (13.4 hours) and Saturn's equilibrium

Parts B.5 and D.1–D.4 of Paper 12 show how the geometry of orbits and the conservation laws dictate the existence, age and composition of planetary rings:

  1. Deriving the universal threshold day P∗≈13.4P^* \approx 13.4 hours (§B.5): the corotation line (aca_c) depends on the parent's mass and its rotation rate, while the Roche limit for ice particles (aRa_R) depends on the parent's radius and its mean density. When aca_c is divided by aRa_R, the parent's mass, radius and density cancel out completely! The result is that the critical rotation day below which the corotation line lies inside the Roche limit is identical for every planet, moon or asteroid in the universe: P∗≈13.4P^* \approx 13.4 hours (for ice at a density of ρice=900\rho_{\mathrm{ice}} = 900 kg/m³). So the strong gravity of Jupiter or the weak gravity of young Mars has no effect on the threshold itself — only the rotation rate (P < 13.4 hours) and the leakage of material from the ground decide.
  2. Viscous spreading — why the ring is a funnel that returns material home (§D.1): inside the ring disc, inner particles move faster than outer ones (differential Keplerian motion). Inelastic collisions turn orbital energy into heat while conserving the total angular momentum. To satisfy both conservation laws together, the disc spreads: most of the ring's mass loses energy and flows inward to the parent, while a minority is pushed outward, carrying with it the excess angular momentum.
  3. Solving the paradox of the age of Saturn's rings (§D.3): Cassini measured material falling from the rings onto Saturn in two parallel channels — neutral rain to the equator at a rate of 4,800–45,000 kg/s (Waite et al. 2018) and ionised rain along the magnetic field lines at a rate of 432–2,870 kg/s (O'Donoghue et al. 2019). Without feeding, the mass of the rings (1.54 × 10¹⁹ kg) would empty within 11 million to 1.1 billion years. In the LEF model, the ring is a constant flow in dynamic equilibrium: water leaking from the inner envelope renews it from below at the same rate at which the rain empties it from above — at a cumulative cost of only 10⁻⁷ to 1.1 × 10⁻⁵ of Saturn's mass over 4.5 billion years!

  • Matching the massive-ring condition across eight planetary states (§B.5): only Saturn today (P = 10.6 h, xc/xR=0.86x_c/x_R = 0.86 + a leaking lid) and young Mars (P = 7–9 h, xc/xR=0.65–0.76x_c/x_R = 0.65\text{–}0.76 + a volcano on a bulge) meet both conditions for a massive ring directly from the ground.
