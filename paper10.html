Mars: The Pear and the Ring

How a fast, expanded Mars set Deimos and Phobos free from a volcano on its pear

Zohar Duran · Independent Researcher, Ramla, Israel · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 26 October 2026

PDF, English (7.2 MB)PDF, Hebrew (4.0 MB)Code and data

Abstract

Phobos and Deimos circle Mars on near-circular orbits of small inclination above its equatorial plane; Phobos lies inside the corotation radius and Deimos outside it. The surface of Deimos is smooth and rounded, while Phobos is scored by systems of long, parallel grooves. Both bodies are light and porous. Although Mars lost most of its water, a large reservoir of liquid water lies today 11.5–20 km beneath its crust.

In this paper we propose a single explanation: Mars, like every planet with a moon, raised a pear, and both moons left from a volcano standing high on it. The young Mars was 1.5 times its present radius, its mean density was 1,166 kg/m³, and it turned once in seven to nine hours; a homogeneous body of that density cannot turn faster than once in 5.27 hours. A pear rose from its equator. Its tip lagged behind the spin, and when the pear later settled it lay down westward. On the topography it runs from a base in Valles Marineris to a tip beside Pavonis Mons, 2,996 km on today's surface, and the Tharsis rise along it is two to three times steeper on the west than on the east. The gravity field points to the same place: the long axis of the Martian equator lies at 255°E, on the pear, and the figure of Mars is still offset toward it — a mild pear today. As the pear returned, its interior drained back east along its axis, through Noctis Labyrinthus and Valles Marineris, over a spill point and north through the chaos to Chryse.

The measured length is the hypotenuse of a right triangle, stretched along the raised equator of the young Mars. On the standing pear the vent of Pavonis stood 4,450 km above the equator, where gravity had fallen to about a quarter of its surface value and the outward push of the spin cancelled it: a body there weighed nothing, and moved at the speed of an orbit. Under these conditions an ordinary volcano — a shallow reservoir about 5 km down and an overpressure of 10–50 MPa, the values of Vulcanian explosions on the Earth — set two bodies free. Deimos left first, when the day was 7.3 hours, on a long orbit that crossed the ring. Phobos left second, when Mars had slowed to 8.3 hours and the corotation line had reached the vent, directly onto a circle at the line.

A round ring of condensed steam, which Mars had built from its own volatiles, stood beyond the line in the equatorial plane. It rounded both orbits and flattened their tilt from about 10°, the tilt of the vent relative to the centre of the pear's base. It held Deimos long, ground it to its present size and carried it outward, and the tide of the young Mars, 8–20 times stronger than today's, carried it beyond the final place of the line. The ring could give Phobos nothing, for Phobos already moved at its speed; its gaseous inner edge, as thin as the Earth's air at about 115 km, exerted on Phobos a weak headwind that balanced the push of the tide, held it at the line and led it outward with the line as Mars slowed. When the ring dried, from the outside in, Phobos stood near 19,000 km and the day was about 22 hours; the line passed it, and it has been falling since — slowly for three billion years, fast in the last few hundred million.

Mars lost about 87% of its spin. It was slowed not by its moons, which carry a millionth of its spin, but by water and gas that left through the corotation line: 6–11% of its mass, a fifth to a third of the water of a young Mars rich in water. The water that never left stayed sealed beneath the crust.

Part I tells this sequence, each stage with the questions a reader will ask and their answers. Part II sets out the evidence: the pit of Pavonis, the two faces of the moons, their orbits, and the water. Part III gives detailed, falsifiable predictions — among them the composition of the samples of Phobos that the Martian Moons eXploration mission (MMX) is to return in 2031.
