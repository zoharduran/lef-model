MASS CLARIFICATION

Before a lunar reservoir separates, the mass of the lunar material is included in the parent body. After the transfer the same mass is assigned to the second body. The distinction between the total initial mass and the mass used in the reference calculation prevents double counting and the mixing of states.

In practice this means: the 7.35×1022 kg of the Moon is counted once. Before the transfer it is inside the Earth’s 6.0455×1024 kg; after it, it is the Moon, and the Earth keeps 5.972×1024 kg.

Abstract

The idea. Anything that is part of a spinning planet, or still attached to it, turns with it: it goes round once in every planetary day. This is corotation. The farther from the centre such material is, the faster it moves, because its speed is the spin rate times the distance. The speed needed for a free circular orbit does the opposite: it falls with distance. At one distance the two speeds are equal — the corotation radius. Material carried out to that distance while still turning with the parent, and released there, already moves at orbital speed; nothing has to throw it.

The question. The paper examines whether transferring lunar material outward from a terrestrial parent, while corotation is maintained, can lead to a state whose orbital velocity and angular-momentum budget approximately match the present Earth–Moon system. (Angular momentum is the amount of rotation a system holds; internal processes can only pass it from one part to another, and only outside forces such as the Sun’s tides can remove it.) The central distinction is between the required velocity at release and the mechanical capacity and angular-momentum budget needed to reach that state. In a central-field model the corotation radius follows from equating the rotational speed and the orbital speed. For an initial period of 5.5 hours the reference values are 15,818 km and 5,020 m/s.

The budget. The parent is described as a uniform Maclaurin spheroid (the flattened equilibrium shape of a spinning fluid body) with a volume-equivalent radius of 6,517 km. The computed initial angular momentum is about 3.466×1034kgm2s−13.466\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}, about 0.7% above the sum of the magnitudes of the present angular-momentum components — a small surplus, which is what the later loss to solar tides requires. Vector addition at the angles tested gives a surplus of about 1.5–2.5%; this is a sensitivity test, not a full vector reconstruction of the evolution. The surplus depends on the nearly uniform structure and on the adopted expansion.

The release. Two processes change the Earth’s spin during the transfer: the lengthening pear carries rotation outward and slows the Earth, and the centralising core — iron sinking inward — speeds it up. They act together; with D=2D=2 the Earth day at release is 5.77 hours and the corotation line lies at about 16,400 km, where the Moon’s centre of mass is released. The angular momentum added to the material is about 15.6% in the base calculation and about 16.5% in the coupled solution; the final orbital angular momentum is about 17.7% of the initial budget in the coupled solution with the pear alone (D=1D=1), and about 17.0% in the release state with D=2D=2 (§B.1). These are transfers within the system.

The result. The paper establishes the kinematic solution and the budgetary end state of the route: the speed a corotating mass needs is supplied by the rotation itself at the corotation radius, and the angular momentum the transfer requires is available in the parent. What the route then requires of the mechanism — that the body be released beyond corotation, that it survive the years it spends inside the fluid Roche limit, and that the connection carry the load until then — is stated as a set of explicit conditions in Part D. Paper 3 examines those conditions.

The layout. Part A states the problems the route must meet; Part B solves them in the same order; Part C summarises; Part D lists claims, tests and requirements; Part E concludes. Appendices A–G hold the derivations and the full environment.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

The Moon’s material left the Earth while still turning with it, and was released where the speed it already had was the speed a free orbit requires. That place is the corotation radius: 15,818 km for a 5.5-hour parent, 5,020 m/s. The objection that closed the fission route — an angular-momentum budget four times too large — was evaluated at the parent’s surface, under a release condition this route never uses. Evaluated at the corotation radius, the parent’s budget exceeds the present system’s by about 1%, with the sign that solar tidal loss requires, and the classical route needs 2.74 times what this one needs.

The route is specific about what it needs. A parent still at an early stage of sorting at the start of the transfer (k≳0.397k\gtrsim0.397), thermally expanded by 98–520 km; a body released beyond, not at, the corotation radius; an interior stiff enough to survive 10110^1–10310^3 years inside the fluid Roche limit; and a connection able to carry the load until then. Each is a definite requirement, each is passed to the paper that supplies it, and each is listed in §D.3. The requirements of the model, and what would test it, are collected in Paper 11 (The Requirements).

Why the old objection does not apply. Classical fission asked the Earth to throw material off its own surface. At the surface of a 5.5-hour parent the rotation gives about 2,133 m/s, while a free orbit there needs about 7,701 m/s (§A.1.2). The only way to close that gap at the surface is to spin the whole Earth much faster, and a faster spin means more angular momentum: at the classical point where a spinning fluid body becomes unstable (the Jacobi bifurcation, §A.6.5) the parent needs 2.74 times the angular momentum the Earth–Moon system has today, and the older reference calculation, made at another point of the same sequence, gave about four times. The system does not have that much, and this is why fission was abandoned. The LEF route closes the gap in a different way: not by spinning faster, but by carrying the material outward while it turns with the Earth. Its speed grows with distance at the same spin rate, until at the corotation radius it equals the orbital speed. The spin the Earth needs for this is the spin it would have had if all of today’s angular momentum were returned to it — about 5.5 hours (§A.2) — and the budget then needed is 1.007 times today’s. An everyday picture: a child on a roundabout who walks from the centre to the rim moves faster at the rim without anyone pushing the roundabout harder.

What the requirements mean. kk is the moment-of-inertia coefficient: it measures how the mass of a body is spread between centre and surface. A uniform sphere has k=0.4k=0.4; today’s Earth, with its iron core, has about 0.33. The route needs k≳0.397k\gtrsim0.397 at the start of the transfer, that is, a parent still at an early stage of sorting: an Earth whose mass is less concentrated toward the centre stores more rotation at the same spin. The organisation of the Earth’s core is one continuous process that never stopped. During the bulge it was at its beginning; while the pear grew it continued; and after release it went on while the Earth took the pear back in. There was probably never a fully uniform period: the earlier the stage, the less sorted the Earth. Iron may already have been gathering in the core at the start; the process continued. The young Earth must also be hot and therefore larger than today: an expansion between 98 and 520 km is admissible, and 120 km is adopted. The body must be released beyond the corotation radius and not exactly on it: exactly on the line the tide is neutral and gives no push either way, while a body lying even slightly beyond the line is pushed outward by the tide, so that it recedes from the start (Paper 4); the margin needed is small, Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0, about 210 km (Paper 3, §C.5a). The fluid Roche limit is the distance inside which the Earth’s tide would pull apart a body held together by its own gravity alone. A body with internal strength survives closer in, down to its rigid limit; the Moon at release is rigid and round, with its flattening and rounding completed earlier, while it was still attached (Paper 7). The connection between the two bodies — the pear — must carry the load until release. These requirements are not hidden in the results; they are collected in §D.3, and Paper 3 tests them.

Introduction — Part A: the release condition in the context of existing models

Two kinds of evidence bear on the Moon’s origin: how its material could have reached orbit (the dynamics of separation) and whether its composition matches the body it came from. Darwin’s work on tides and rotation is part of the historical background for examining the evolution of the Earth–Moon system [6]; his fission idea — that a fast-spinning Earth shed the Moon — is the classical form of a terrestrial origin. In this paper the quantitative comparison with classical fission is made through a defined equilibrium configuration; a result for one configuration must not be converted into a bound on every possible path. In other words, showing that one particular fission path needs too much angular momentum does not show that every path from the Earth does.

In the giant-impact family of models, the Moon forms from debris after another body strikes the Earth, and the isotopic composition places constraints on the mixing of terrestrial and impactor material. Different versions of the model exist, and the constraints depend on the compositions and on the manner of mixing [7]. In the LEF scenario, construction from a shared terrestrial reservoir is examined: the Moon is made of the Earth’s own material, so a close isotopic match follows directly. A shared source provides a basis for compositional comparison, but does not replace the test of transfer to orbit. That test is the subject of this paper.

The focused question of this paper is whether carrying mass outward in shared rotation changes the velocity condition and the angular-momentum budget relative to direct release from the surface. The answer the paper gives is that it does: the release condition is moved from the surface to the corotation radius, and both the speed and the budget then fit. The mechanism of construction — the feed from within and the rise of the outer shell as the surface of the same body — is the subject of Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice. The present paper defines the physical conditions it must meet. The division is deliberate: Paper 2 asks whether the route is possible in principle, using standard physics; Paper 3 shows how it is carried out.

Introduction — Part B: what the paper presents

The paper is built as a set of problems followed by their solutions, so that each objection is met in the place where it is raised. Part A sets out the problems the route must meet, one by one: the velocity gap that led to the rejection of classical fission, what must be calculated, the pear that connects the Earth to the corotation line, the offset between the lengthening pear and the centralising core, corotation itself, the angular-momentum budget, the Roche limit and the direction of tidal evolution. Part B solves them in the same order, starting from one calculation. Part C summarises what has been shown; Part D collects the claims, tests and requirements; Part E states the conclusions. Appendices A–G give the derivations, the sensitivity scans, the Roche calculations and the full computational environment, so that every number in the main text can be traced and reproduced.

THE EQUATORIAL PLANE

To keep the principle clear, the calculation and the whole of this paper treat the release of the Moon from the equatorial plane. Paper 4 shows that the Moon left from another latitude — 22.5, the centre of the contact area — and recomputes the release there.

The equatorial plane is the simplest case: there the spin carries material fastest and the corotation radius is a single distance. Release from a higher latitude changes the numbers somewhat but not the principle; the size of the change is shown by the 25° sensitivity test of the environment table, and the adopted case of 22.5° is computed in Paper 4.

PART A

The problems before us

THE PROBLEM AND HOW IT IS TESTED

Each section of this Part states one condition that a body built from the Earth’s own material must meet if it is to reach orbit whole. Part B answers them in the same order.

This Part sets out the physics the route has to satisfy, one problem at a time. It begins with the difficulty that closed the classical fission route — a body leaving the surface of a fast-spinning Earth does not have the speed of an orbit — and then asks, in turn, where that speed is reached, what connects the Earth to that place, how the Earth’s spin changes while the material moves out, whether the angular momentum suffices, whether the body survives the Earth’s tide, and in which direction it moves afterwards. Each section ends with a defined quantity that Part B then computes.

A.1The problem: the velocity gap

A.1.1Two separate dynamical questions

A terrestrial-origin scenario requires an explanation for the transfer of material from the parent body to a free orbit. A compositional match can support a shared source without determining how material reached orbit: two bodies may be made of the same material and still leave open the question of how one of them came to circle the other. The focus of this paper is therefore the dynamics. The original historical review is examined here by comparing defined equilibrium configurations, and not by asserting that every terrestrial-origin path is subject to the same fission threshold.

Two questions have to be kept apart. The first question is under what conditions the velocity of the material is consistent with a free orbit — that is, whether, at the moment it is let go, the material moves fast enough sideways to keep falling around the Earth without falling back onto it. The second question is whether the angular momentum available at the start of the process permits the end state and the later evolution. Changing the release condition can change the velocity test, but the overall angular-momentum balance must still be satisfied. A route can pass the first test and fail the second, so both are computed.

Angular momentum and the moment of inertia. Angular momentum is the quantity of rotational motion a body carries. For a spinning body it is the product of how fast it turns and how its mass is spread around the axis. The spin angular momentum is:

Lspin=Iω.L_{\mathrm{spin}}=I\omega.

Here ω\omega is the angular velocity (the angle turned per second, 2π2\pi divided by the period) and II is the moment of inertia: the sum, over every piece of the body, of its mass times the square of its distance from the spin axis. The moment of inertia measures how far from the axis the mass sits; mass far from the axis counts much more than mass near it, because the distance enters squared. For a body of mass MM and equatorial radius aa it is convenient to write I=kMa2I=kMa^2, where the dimensionless coefficient kk records how the mass is distributed: a uniform body has k=0.4k=0.4, and a body with a dense core has a smaller kk.

Angular momentum is conserved: in a system on which nothing outside exerts a twisting force (a torque), its total cannot change; it can only be passed from one part of the system to another. For the Earth–Moon system the only significant outside torque is the tide raised by the Sun, which removes about 1% over the system’s history (§A.2). This conservation is what makes the problem calculable: whatever the Moon carries in its orbit today must once have been in the spin of the parent.

Transferring material outward in corotation — that is, while it keeps turning with the parent at the parent’s own angular velocity — requires the parent to supply additional angular momentum to that material, because the same angular velocity at a larger distance means a larger moment of inertia for that material. In the absence of an external torque, this is a redistribution of existing angular momentum: what the material gains, the parent’s spin loses.

A.1.2Release from the surface

We first examine the classical case: material let go directly from the equator of a fast-spinning parent. In the reference state the period is T0=5.5T_0=5.5 hours and the equatorial radius is a0≃6,721a_0\simeq6{,}721 km. Therefore, since 5.5 hours are 19,800 seconds:

ω0=2π19,800≃3.17333×10−4rads−1.\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{19{,}800}\simeq3.17333\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.

Two speeds must now be compared at the equator. The first is the speed the rotation itself gives to a point on the surface, vrotv_{\mathrm{rot}}: the angular velocity times the distance from the axis. The second is the circular orbital speed vcircv_{\mathrm{circ}}: the speed at which a free body at that distance would keep falling around the Earth in a circle, its centrifugal tendency exactly balancing gravity.

vrot=ω0a0≃2,133ms−1,vcirc=GM⊕a0≃7,701ms−1.v_{\mathrm{rot}}=\omega_0a_0\simeq2{,}133\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, \qquad v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt{\frac{GM_\oplus}{a_0}}\simeq7{,}701\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.

Even with a day of only 5.5 hours, the surface moves at less than a third of the speed an orbit there requires. The velocity difference, in the rounding of the original reference calculation of this paper, is about 5,568 m/s. This is the velocity gap.

What orbit would such material follow if it were simply let go? Without an additional radial velocity, the specific energy (the orbital energy per kilogram, kinetic minus gravitational) and the semi-major axis (half the long axis of the resulting ellipse) are:

ε=v22−GM⊕r≃−5.70×107Jkg−1,Aorb=−GM⊕2ε≃3,495km.\varepsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{GM_\oplus}{r}\simeq-5.70\times10^7\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\frac{GM_\oplus}{2\varepsilon}\simeq3{,}495\ \mathrm{km}.

The energy is strongly negative — the material is deeply bound — and the semi-major axis, 3,495 km, is about half the parent’s equatorial radius. The release point is the farthest point of this ellipse, so the whole of the rest of the path lies deep inside the parent. Under these initial conditions the Keplerian orbit penetrates the parent body: the material simply falls back. Surface velocity alone therefore does not provide a free orbit that remains outside it. This is the core of the classical objection to fission: to close the gap at the surface the parent would have to spin several times faster, which requires far more angular momentum than the Earth–Moon system holds (§A.6.5).

The numbers in §A.2 preserve the original reference comparison, in which GM of the final Earth mass was used. They are not a calculation of the exact field of the whole initial parent. This definition distinguishes the reproduced reference comparison from the overall budget in §A.6.

A.1.3The transfer condition examined

The LEF route does not let the material go at the surface. In the scenario examined, the material rises while still attached and receives angular momentum, so that it keeps turning with the parent as it moves out. The two speeds then behave in opposite ways. The velocity ωr\omega r grows with distance, because a point that turns once per day travels a larger circle the farther out it is — as a child walking outward on a turning roundabout is carried round faster and faster. The orbital speed GM/r\sqrt{GM/r} decreases, because gravity weakens with distance and less sideways speed is needed to balance it. The gap of §A.1.2 therefore narrows as the material rises, and at one distance it closes.

The paper computes the point of equality and the angular-momentum cost of the transfer; the ascent process itself is a separate mechanical question. Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice, examines the transport capacity and the connection; the detailed pressure calculation is outside the budget calculation presented here. The orbital initial conditions are handed to Paper 4, The Orbit.

A.2What must be calculated

The problem now has a definite shape. Five quantities must be calculated: where the Moon is released, with what Earth day, whether the angular momentum suffices, whether the body survives the tide, and whether it recedes afterwards.

Why 5.5 hours. The starting spin is not chosen freely; it follows from conservation of angular momentum run backwards. All of today’s Earth–Moon angular momentum, 3.44×10343.44\times10^{34} kg m2^2 s−1^{-1} — the Earth’s spin together with the Moon’s orbit — is returned to the spin of a young, hot, expanded Earth still at an early stage of sorting (k=0.4k=0.4 as the calculation input). “Expanded” means that the hot early Earth was somewhat larger than the cold Earth of today; “an early stage of sorting” means that the organisation of the core had only begun, so the mass was spread nearly uniformly and the uniform value k=0.4k=0.4 describes it closely. Putting the whole budget into the spin of such a body gives the period: T0=2πI0L≃5.54h.T_0=\frac{2\pi I_0}{L}\simeq5.54\ \mathrm{h}. In words: a body with moment of inertia I0I_0 carrying angular momentum LL turns at ω=L/I0\omega=L/I_0, and its period is 2π/ω2\pi/\omega. Solar tides have since removed about 1% of the system’s angular momentum (about 1.35% when the receding Moon is followed; Eulenfeld & Heubeck 2023; Canup 2004). A period of 5.5 h therefore carries today’s angular momentum and the roughly 1% that was lost: it is slightly shorter than 5.54 h because the early system held slightly more. A young Sun more massive by a few per cent raises this loss only to 1–2%.

This is the central number of the paper. A.3The pear

The corotation radius lies far above the surface, so something must carry the material there while it keeps turning with the Earth. In the model this is the pear. The connection is a hill that rises from the Earth: a base about 9,000 km wide at the surface, sides that form a single arc without a waist, and a contact disc 3,000 km wide at the Moon. It bridges about 7,800 km between the Earth’s surface and the Moon and lies entirely inside the corotation line, so it is pulled inward: everywhere along the pear, gravity is stronger than what the rotation needs to keep the material circling, so the pear’s own material is held by the Earth and does not fly off. Only the body at its tip reaches the line. Its length and mass are a requirement: the Moon was released whole, so a pear must exist that meets the conditions. How the pear is built and how it carries the load are the subject of Paper 3.

A.4 The offset: two processes acting together

Before the Earth day at release can be calculated, two processes that act on the Earth’s spin at the same time, and in opposite directions, must both be taken into account. This matters because the day at release fixes the position of the corotation line, and therefore where the Moon is let go.

1. The pear lengthens. Material moves outward with the rotation and carries angular momentum with it; the angular momentum that leaves the Earth slows its spin. A child walking outward on a roundabout slows the roundabout in the same way.

2. The core centralises. Differentiation is the separation of an initially mixed body into layers by density: iron, the densest component, sinks toward the centre and forms a core, while lighter material is left above it. In the LEF model this organisation of the core is one continuous process: it had begun before the transfer, it continued while the pear grew, and it went on after release. Fast rotation speeds up differentiation: iron sinks inward and the Earth’s moment of inertia falls, because mass moves closer to the axis. With less moment of inertia and the same angular momentum, the body must turn faster. As with a skater drawing in her arms, the spin speeds up.

Angular momentum is conserved, and the two processes offset each other: the result is the offset between them. A calculation that takes only one of them into account does not describe the Earth of that time — the pear alone would give too long a day, and the core alone too short a one.

A.4.1 The effect of differentiation on the final state

Concentration of mass inward changes the moment of inertia. To test this, the two layer boundaries, the density, the shape and the spin were computed together. This extends the sensitivity test, and does not merely replace kk inside a fixed homogeneous figure: a body that differentiates also changes its flattening, and the flattening in turn changes the moment of inertia, so all four must be solved at once. The boundaries were allowed to depart from spheroids; the limitation of the spheroidal assumption in layered systems is also discussed in the literature [5].

A.5 Corotation

This section derives the corotation radius used throughout, and states where its use is valid.

Figure 4 plots the two speeds of §A.1.3 against distance from the Earth’s centre. The speed carried by the rotation rises in a straight line; the orbital speed falls. Inside the crossing, the rotation gives less than an orbit needs and material is pulled inward; outside it, the rotation gives more than an orbit needs and material tends outward. The crossing is the corotation line.

Corotation acts on the centre of mass, not on the geometric centre, because the balance of gravity and rotation applies to the body as a whole, and the whole body moves as if its mass were gathered at its centre of mass. The corotation forces may have pulled the Moon slightly after its geometric centre crossed the line, because its centre of mass is displaced slightly toward the Earth (today about 2 km): the geometric centre reaches the line first, and the centre of mass a little later; the Moon is released slightly after its centre of mass crosses, about 210 km beyond the line (§D.3; Paper 3, §C.5a).

A.5.1 The derivation

In the test-particle approximation, in the equatorial plane and in a central field, the speed carried by the rotation is the angular velocity times the distance; the circular orbital speed follows from setting the gravitational pull equal to the force needed to keep a mass mm on a circle of radius RR:

vrot=ωR,GMmR2=mvcirc2R,vcirc=GMR.v_{\mathrm{rot}}=\omega R, \qquad \frac{GMm}{R^2}=\frac{mv_{\mathrm{circ}}^2}{R}, \qquad v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.

The mass mm of the orbiting body cancels: the orbital speed at a given distance is the same for a grain and for a Moon. Equating the speeds gives:

ωR=GMR⇒ω2R3=GM.\omega R=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^2R^3=GM.

Solving for RR, and writing ω=2π/T\omega=2\pi/T:

Rsync=(GMω2)1/3=(GMT24π2)1/3\boxed{R_{\mathrm{sync}}=\left(\frac{GM}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3} =\left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}}

The result says that the corotation radius depends only on the mass of the central body and on its rotation period, and grows as the period to the power two-thirds: a slower spin pushes the line outward. This is why the lengthening pear moves the line out and the centralising core brings it back (§A.4).

For a positive, constant mass and angular velocity there is a single positive root. This is the synchronous-orbit relation of standard orbital mechanics — the same relation that places geostationary satellites above today’s Earth; what is new is where it is evaluated. The classical objection evaluated the speed condition at the parent’s surface; the LEF route evaluates it at the corotation radius.

A.5.2 The release condition and the domain of validity

The equality fits an equatorial circular orbit when the radial velocity is zero and the contact forces cease — that is, when the body is moving purely sideways and the connection no longer pushes or pulls on it. With additional velocity, or with release off the equator, the initial-condition vectors must be computed. The orbital detail belongs to Paper 4; in this paper only the correction needed for the angular-momentum budget is kept, in §A.6 and Appendix D.

Reaching corotation does not cancel the work required for the lift or the transfer of angular momentum: the speed condition is met at the line, but energy and angular momentum still have to be delivered to the material on the way. The field of a flattened parent, the size of the developing body and two-body gravity also place corrections on the central description. The solution in §A.4 includes a particular two-body correction, but is not a full solution of two deformed figures connected to each other.

A.5.3 The meaning of the condition for release

The coupled solution in §A.4 defines a budgetary end state: how much angular momentum is in the Earth’s spin and how much in the Moon’s orbit at the moment of release. It does not by itself determine the onset of recession from it. An added radial velocity is also not a sufficient condition for sustained recession, because it changes the eccentricity (how elongated the orbit is) and the tides. The lag model and the rheology (the description of how the material deforms and flows under load) must be mutually consistent [3,4]. The direction of the tidal push on each side of the line is set out in §A.8.

A.5.4 The boundary between Paper 2 and Paper 4

Paper 2 fixes the mass, the spin and the budget to be handed to the orbital test. Paper 4 treats the orbital elements, the tilt and the evolution after release. There is no computed recession time here, and no general prohibition on a free orbit inside the corotation radius. In the connected scenario the free-body equations are to be replaced by the equations of the connected state where the free-body equations cease to apply: while the Moon is still attached through the pear, it is held by the connection and is not a free body.

A.6The angular-momentum budget

The speed problem is solved at the corotation radius. The second question of §A.1.1 remains: does the parent hold enough angular momentum to put the Moon there and still match the system we see today? This section builds the budget step by step: the parent’s initial angular momentum, the cost of moving the material out, the comparison with the present system, the sensitivity to period, expansion and structure, and the comparison with the classical fission route.

A.6.1The parent and the initial budget

The initial parent mass is Mtot=M⊕+mM_{\mathrm{tot}}=M_\oplus+m. The material to be transferred later is still included in its moment of inertia. The parent is described as a Maclaurin spheroid: the equilibrium shape of a uniform fluid body in steady rotation, flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator, symmetric about its axis. The faster it spins, the flatter it is. In the homogeneous Maclaurin model, under the assumptions of the table, one obtains [1]:

I0=25Mtota02≃1.092×1038kgm2,I_0=\frac{2}{5}M_{\mathrm{tot}}a_0^2\simeq1.092\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},

L0=I0ω0≃3.466×1034kgm2s−1.\boxed{L_0=I_0\omega_0\simeq3.466\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}.}

The first line is the moment of inertia of a uniform spheroid (k=2/5k=2/5) with the equatorial radius 6,721 km; the second multiplies it by the angular velocity of the 5.5-hour day. The result, 3.466×10343.466\times10^{34}, is the whole angular-momentum budget the route has to work with. The shape equation and the full substitution are kept in Appendix B. Uniformity is the reference assumption; a change of structure is tested below.

A.6.2 The cost of the transfer in the base calculation

Before the transfer, the lunar material is part of the parent and turns with it, carrying its share of the parent’s angular momentum. For material representing the uniform mass distribution, its mean squared distance from the axis is the same as the parent’s, and its share is:

L0,mat=mω0⟨r⊥2⟩=25mω0a02≃4.21×1032kgm2s−1.L_{0,\mathrm{mat}}=m\omega_0\left\langle r_\perp^2\right\rangle =\frac25m\omega_0a_0^2\simeq4.21\times10^{32}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}.

Here ⟨r⊥2⟩\langle r_\perp^2\rangle is the average, over the material, of the squared distance from the spin axis. Once the same mass sits at the corotation radius and still turns with the parent, its angular momentum is:

Lorb,0=mω0Rsync,02≃5.83×1033kgm2s−1,L_{\mathrm{orb},0}=m\omega_0R_{\mathrm{sync},0}^2\simeq5.83\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}},

about fourteen times more, because the distance from the axis has grown and enters squared. The difference between the two must be supplied by the parent’s spin. As a fraction of the whole budget:

Lorb,0−L0,matLinitial≃15.6%.\boxed{\frac{L_{\mathrm{orb},0}-L_{0,\mathrm{mat}}}{L_{\mathrm{initial}}}\simeq15.6\%.}

About one sixth of the parent’s angular momentum is handed to the material during the transfer. This is the amount by which the parent’s spin is slowed by the pear (§A.4) before the centralising core is taken into account.

In the base calculation, an equatorial surface source gives about 13.8%; a representative sample of a similar interior region at half the original radius gives about 16.5%. The source matters because material that starts far from the axis already carries more angular momentum and needs less added. A specific interior source requires its own mean squared distance. The word “interior” must not be taken to imply that the global mean necessarily applies.

A.6.3 Comparison with the present system

The parent’s budget must now be compared with what the Earth–Moon system holds today. The present total has two parts: the Earth’s spin and the Moon’s orbit. The reference constants copied from the source give:

Lspin=I⊕ω⊕≃5.86×1033,L_{\mathrm{spin}}=I_\oplus\omega_\oplus\simeq5.86\times10^{33},

Lorbit=μredGMtotam(1−em2)≃2.855×1034kgm2s−1,L_{\mathrm{orbit}}=\mu_{\mathrm{red}}\sqrt{GM_{\mathrm{tot}}a_m(1-e_m^2)} \simeq2.855\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}},

where am=3.844×108a_m=3.844\times10^8 m and em=0.0549e_m=0.0549. The second formula is the orbital angular momentum of two bodies circling their common centre: μred=M⊕m/(M⊕+m)\mu_{\mathrm{red}}=M_\oplus m/(M_\oplus+m) is the reduced mass, ama_m the semi-major axis of the lunar orbit and eme_m its eccentricity. The sum of the magnitudes is about 3.441×10343.441\times10^{34}, of which about 83% is orbital: most of the system’s angular momentum now lies in the Moon’s orbit, not in the Earth’s spin. The surplus of the parent’s budget relative to the sum of magnitudes is about 0.7%. The parent held slightly more than the system holds today, which is the sign required by the small loss to solar tides (§A.2).

The spin and the orbital angular momentum are vectors, and they do not point the same way: the Earth’s axis is tilted against the plane of the lunar orbit. If the angle between the vectors is θ\theta, the total magnitude is:

Lvec=Ls2+Lo2+2LsLocosθ.L_{\mathrm{vec}}=\sqrt{L_s^2+L_o^2+2L_sL_o\cos\theta}.

For the test angles 18.3°, 23.4° and 28.6° one obtains surpluses of about 1.46%, 1.92% and 2.51%. The angles serve as a sensitivity test; no observational epoch was chosen here and no full vector history was computed. The Moon’s present spin is very small, but its early spin was larger, and the two must be kept apart.

The surplus depends on how uniform the parent was. At fixed figure, varying kk gives a threshold of about 0.397 against the scalar sum and about 0.390–0.394 against the vector range: with kk below this value the parent’s budget would fall short of today’s. The present differentiated Earth has kk of about 0.33, well below the threshold, so the route requires that the parent was still at an early stage of sorting when the transfer began; this requirement is listed in §D.3. This is a sensitivity condition, not a stratified Maclaurin family of solutions. The positive surplus means that this test finds no angular-momentum deficit, but matching the rate of loss to the Sun requires a further calculation.

A.6.4 Sensitivity to period and expansion

A shorter initial day means more angular momentum; a larger (more expanded) parent has a larger moment of inertia and holds the same angular momentum at a longer day. The admissible initial period therefore depends on the adopted expansion. In the reference model, the window 4.90–5.54 hours refers to the initial period and to the adopted budget and loss conditions: at 5.54 hours the parent holds exactly today’s angular momentum, and at 4.90 hours it holds about 15% more, the adopted limit for loss to the outside. Its ceiling must not be applied to the final period after the transfer of angular momentum, which is longer by design (§A.4). Appendix B retains the original sensitivity table, with the distinction between an adopted expansion and a thermal calculation not yet performed.

Figure 5 is read as a map of allowed starting states. The horizontal axis is how much the hot parent was expanded over its cold radius; the vertical axis is its day. A starting state above the solid line holds less angular momentum than today’s system and is excluded; a state below the dashed line holds more than 15% extra, more than the outside loss can remove. The band between them is admissible, and it rises to the right because a larger parent holds the same angular momentum at a longer day. The reference state, 5.5 h with 120 km of expansion, lies inside the band, just below the ceiling.

A.6.5 Comparison of equilibrium figures

The classical objection to fission can now be placed exactly. In the classical route the whole Earth must spin so fast that it becomes unstable and divides. A uniform rotating fluid follows a known sequence of shapes as its angular momentum grows. At first it is a Maclaurin spheroid (§A.6.1), flattened but round in the equator. At a definite flattening a second family branches off: the Jacobi ellipsoids, whose equator is no longer a circle but an ellipse, so the body becomes elongated like a rugby ball turning end over end. Further along the Jacobi sequence comes a pear-shaped figure, from which classical fission was expected to proceed. Each of these points requires a definite spin and a definite angular momentum.

At the classical bifurcation point from Maclaurin to Jacobi, with e≃0.81267e\simeq0.81267 and ω2/(πGρ)≃0.37423\omega^2/(\pi G\rho)\simeq0.37423, one obtains for the adopted mass and volume T≃2.73T\simeq2.73 hours, a≃7,802a\simeq7{,}802 km and an angular momentum of about 9.42×10349.42\times10^{34}, a ratio of about 2.74 to the present sum [1]. Here ee is the eccentricity of the meridian section (zero for a sphere, approaching one for a very flat body) and ω2/(πGρ)\omega^2/(\pi G\rho) measures the spin against the body’s own gravity. In plain terms: merely to reach the first step of the classical path, the Earth would need a day of under three hours and almost three times the angular momentum the Earth–Moon system holds.

This is a comparison with a defined configuration on this classical path. The value 4.08 of the original reference calculation refers to a different point on the Maclaurin sequence — the maximum-Ω\Omega point, not the Jacobi bifurcation — and is not presented as a universal bound. It is the source of the familiar statement that fission needs about four times too much angular momentum. The value 3.5 for the pear-shaped point is not adopted because verification of the table has not been completed.

The reason for the difference is the place where the speed condition is evaluated. The classical route asks the parent’s surface to reach orbital speed (§A.1.2); that forces the parent towards break-up, and break-up costs several times the available angular momentum. The LEF route never uses that condition: the material is carried out in corotation and the speed match is made at the corotation radius (§A.5), where a parent turning once in 5.5 hours already supplies orbital speed. Evaluated there, the requirement falls to the budget the parent actually has. The comparison is between two routes to the same product: the classical one needs 2.74 times the present budget; this one needs 1.007.

A.6.6 The vector correction for release off the equator

So far the material was assumed to leave in the equatorial plane. Off the equator the spin vector and the orbital angular momentum must be summed in the same frame, because the orbit of a body released at a latitude is tilted against the spin. Let S=IωS=I\omega be the spin angular momentum and H=μredωr2cosλH=\mu_{\mathrm{red}}\omega r^2\cos\lambda the orbital angular momentum of the released body, where λ\lambda is the angle of release from the equatorial plane. For the geometry defined in Appendix D:

L02=S2+H2+2SHcosλ.\boxed{L_0^2=S^2+H^2+2SH\cos\lambda.}

This is the same rule for adding two arrows at an angle as in §A.6.3: the initial budget must equal the vector sum of the spin and the tilted orbit.

In the example λ=25∘\lambda=25^\circ relative to the final axis and a homogeneous structure, the two-mass coupled calculation gives a final period of 6.220 hours, a reference distance of about 17,240 km and an orbital angular momentum of about 15.83% of the budget. The 25° value is a sensitivity test of how much an off-equator release changes the budget; the adopted release latitude, the centre of the contact area, is 22.5° (Paper 4). This is a sensitivity correction to the budget, not a newly adopted reference value. The derivation of the inclination and eccentricity, the perigee and the evolution after release are handed to Paper 4.

A.6.7The budgetary decision condition

The test requires a consistent initial state, final state and external transfer:

𝐋i=𝐋f+Δ𝐋external.\mathbf{L}_i=\mathbf{L}_f+\Delta\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{external}}.

The equation is conservation of angular momentum written as an account: what the system started with equals what it holds now plus what was removed from outside (for the Earth–Moon system, chiefly by the Sun’s tide). Closing the numerical balance proves consistency between the states defined. Failure to find a match within the supported ranges of structure and external transfers would rule out this budgetary scenario. A match does not establish how the body mechanically reaches the final state; that is the subject of Paper 3.

The budget therefore stands as follows: the parent of 5.5 hours holds slightly more angular momentum than today’s system, the transfer costs about one sixth of it, and the classical route would have needed 2.74 times as much.

A.7The Roche limit

A body that reaches the corotation radius must also hold together there. Close to a massive parent, the parent’s gravity pulls harder on the near side of the body than on the far side; this difference, the tidal force, tends to stretch the body apart. The Roche limit is the distance inside which this stretching exceeds the body’s own gravity holding it together. A body held only by its own gravity cannot remain whole inside it.

A.7.1Two reference limits

For ρs=2,600kgm−3\rho_s=2{,}600\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} and the parent mass after departure:

dfluid=2.44(3M⊕4πρs)1/3≃19,971km,d_{\mathrm{fluid}}=2.44\left(\frac{3M_\oplus}{4\pi\rho_s}\right)^{1/3} \simeq19{,}971\ \mathrm{km},

drigid=1.26(3M⊕4πρs)1/3≃10,314km.d_{\mathrm{rigid}}=1.26\left(\frac{3M_\oplus}{4\pi\rho_s}\right)^{1/3} \simeq10{,}314\ \mathrm{km}.

(An earlier version gave 10,313 km; the two are the same within rounding, and the series uses 10,314 km. Figures 1, 6, 8 and 9 print the fluid limit as 19,972 km, the same value within rounding.) The bracketed term is the radius a sphere of the Earth’s mass would have if it had the satellite’s density; the limit is a fixed multiple of it. The two formulas differ only in that multiple, and the reason is physical. The fluid approximation concerns a deforming body: as it is stretched it lengthens toward the parent, its near and far ends move farther apart, the tidal difference across it grows, and it gives way sooner — so the fluid limit lies farther out. The so-called rigid approximation neglects the change of shape: the body keeps its round form, the tidal difference across it stays smaller, and it survives closer in. It contains no crustal thickness or material strength [1]; it is a body held by its own gravity alone that merely does not change its shape. The base radius and the two coupled radii lie between these limits: inside the fluid limit, outside the rigid one. The presence of a crust alone therefore does not establish survival; survival depends on whether the body behaves as rigid on the time-scale it spends there.

The Roche limits move inward as the body density increases, because a denser body holds itself together more strongly. A density of about 4,700 kg per cubic metre would move the fluid limit to the release distance of about 16,400 km; for the rigid body at release the rigid limit applies (§B.7). This is a requirement computed in the Roche approximation, not evidence that the body reached that density or a solution to all the stability requirements.

A.7.2 What the comparison of accelerations teaches

The basic comparison is between the parent’s gravity gradient and the self-gravity:

Δa≃2GM⊕Rsr3,gs=GmRs2.\Delta a\simeq\frac{2GM_\oplus R_s}{r^3},\qquad g_s=\frac{Gm}{R_s^2}.

The first quantity, Δa\Delta a, is the difference between the parent’s pull at the body’s centre and at its surface, a distance RsR_s closer; it falls steeply, as the cube of the distance rr. The second, gsg_s, is the body’s own surface gravity holding its material in. When the first is smaller than the second, the body’s own gravity wins. With the reference data of Appendix E the ratio is below unity (about 0.277 at 15,818 km). This is a useful measure of loading, but change of shape and rotation are not included in the ratio. The static Roche limit provides a basis for comparison; the response in an eccentric orbit may also depend on the deformation history [9].

A.7.3What remains conditional

The corotation radius is not a strength threshold: reaching it settles the speed, not the survival. The properties of the body and of the connection are examined in Paper 3, and the orbit after release in Paper 4. Paper 2 adopts no binding residence time in the Roche domain and no universal viscosity threshold. The comparison should use the closest distance of the chosen orbit, not the release distance alone. Dividing the tests among the papers does not remove the requirement of survival.

Figure 6 places the Moon at release against the two families of limits. The rigid limits for the whole range of densities lie well inside the Moon’s position; the fluid limits lie beyond it. The Moon at release is therefore safe as a rigid body, and its flattening and rounding were completed earlier, while it was still attached (§B.7; Paper 7).

A.8The direction of tidal evolution

The last question of Part A is what happens after release: does the Moon move away from the Earth or fall back? The answer depends on which side of the corotation line it lies.

A.8.1 The direction of angular-momentum transfer

For a direct, circular, equatorial orbit, we compare the parent’s spin with the mean motion — the angular velocity of the orbiting body around the parent:

n=GMr3.n=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}.

The orbiting body raises a tidal bulge on the parent. When ω>n\omega>n, the parent turns faster than the body orbits, and its rotation carries the bulge ahead of the body; the bulge pulls the body forward, gives it angular momentum, and the body climbs outward while the parent’s spin slows. In the language of the tidal equations, the dissipative tidal contribution in the parent transfers angular momentum from its spin to the orbit. When ω<n\omega<n, the body outruns the bulge, the bulge pulls it back, and the direction reverses: the body loses angular momentum and spirals inward. At exact circular synchronism the relevant contribution vanishes: the bulge stays directly beneath the body and there is no forward or backward pull. Since nn falls with distance and ω\omega does not, ω>n\omega>n holds outside the corotation radius and ω<n\omega<n inside it. The corotation line is thus a watershed: a body released just beyond it is pushed outward from the start. Generalisation to an orbit with inclination and eccentricity requires the appropriate tidal equations [2,3].

Figure 7 shows the watershed at work in the present Solar System. Mars has two small moons on opposite sides of its synchronous radius: Phobos, inside, is being drawn inward; Deimos, outside, is moving away. The Earth–Moon case at release is the same rule applied to a Moon placed just beyond the line.

With Part A complete, the problems are fully defined: a velocity gap that closes at the corotation radius, a pear that reaches that radius, a day at release set by two opposing processes, a budget that must close against today’s system, a Roche test the body must pass, and a tidal watershed that decides its later direction. Part B answers them with one calculation.

The angular momentum that leaves with the material is taken from the Earth’s spin, so the Earth turns more slowly: alone, this step would lengthen the day from 5.50 to 6.44 h and move the line out to 17,569 km (§A.4).

With a core twice as dense as the mantle above it (D=2D=2), this step removes 0.67 h from the day.

  5. Release. The Moon begins to separate when its centre of mass reaches this line. It is carried there by the rotation, not thrown: at that distance the speed it already has as part of the turning system is the speed of a free orbit, so no extra push is required (§B.2).

B.2The velocity gap

The problem of §A.1 was that material released from the surface of a 5.5-h Earth moves at about 2,133 m s−1^{-1}, far below the 7,701 m s−1^{-1} of a circular orbit, a gap of about 5,568 m s−1^{-1}. The gap closes with distance, because the speed carried by the rotation grows with distance while the orbital speed falls. On the corotation line the rotation gives the Moon exactly the orbital speed, v=Ωr=G(M⊕+m)r≈4,960ms−1,v=\Omega r=\sqrt{\frac{G(M_\oplus+m)}{r}}\approx4{,}960\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, so no gap remains (§A.1, §A.5). The left side is the speed of a point turning with the Earth at distance rr; the right side is the speed of a free circular orbit at the same distance; on the line they are equal. The value is a little below the 5,020 m s−1^{-1} of the 5.5-h reference (§A.2.1), because the slightly slower Earth places the line farther out, where orbital speeds are lower. The everyday picture is a child on a roundabout: the farther from the centre she sits, the faster she moves, without anyone pushing her.

B.3The pear

The pear bridges about 7,800 km and lies entirely inside the corotation line; the Moon is released when its centre of mass reaches the line (§A.3; the mechanism of release is Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice). Because the pear lies inside the line, the net force in the turning frame pulls it toward the Earth (§B.5): it stays with the Earth, while the Moon, whose centre is on the line, is free to separate. This is why the Moon leaves whole and the connection does not go with it.

B.4The offset

The calculation takes both processes into account together: Earth day 5.77 h, corotation line 16,331 km (§A.4). The 16,331 km is the test-particle value of step 4 in §B.1; including the Moon’s own mass gives the 16,399 km, about 16,400 km, used for the release point. A calculation with the pear alone would give 6.44 h and 17,569 km, and one with the core alone would give too fast an Earth; only the two together describe the Earth of that time.

B.5 Corotation

The Moon’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line at about 16,400 km; beyond it corotation pulls the Moon outward and it is released: anet=Ω2r−GM⊕r2.a_{\mathrm{net}}=\Omega^{2}r-\frac{GM_\oplus}{r^{2}}. This is the net acceleration felt by material that turns with the Earth at distance rr, seen in the turning frame. The first term is the outward (centrifugal) acceleration of the rotation, which grows with distance; the second is the Earth’s gravity, which falls with distance. Inside the line gravity wins and aneta_{\mathrm{net}} points inward, which is why the pear is held to the Earth; on the line the two balance; beyond it the rotation wins and the material is drawn outward. As soon as the Moon’s centre of mass passes the line, the body as a whole is drawn away from the Earth, and it separates.

The system holds 1.007 times today’s angular momentum. The value 0.355 is what the Earth must have at release if its spin of 5.77 h is to carry the remaining 2.88×10342.88\times10^{34}. No giant impact from outside and no later removal of angular momentum are needed; high-angular-momentum impact models start at about 2–3 times today’s value. The route closes its budget with what the Earth–Moon system holds today plus the roughly 1% that solar tides have since removed.

B.7The Roche limit

The Roche limit is the distance inside which the tidal pull of a large body, the difference of its gravity between the near and the far side of a smaller body, exceeds the smaller body’s own gravity and can pull it apart. Two classical forms bound it: drigid=1.26R⊕(ρ⊕ρ)1/3,dfluid=2.44R⊕(ρ⊕ρ)1/3.d_{\mathrm{rigid}}=1.26\,R_\oplus\left(\frac{\rho_\oplus}{\rho}\right)^{1/3},\qquad d_{\mathrm{fluid}}=2.44\,R_\oplus\left(\frac{\rho_\oplus}{\rho}\right)^{1/3}. The rigid form applies to a body that keeps its shape; the fluid form to a body that deforms freely and so is stretched further by the tide. In both, ρ\rho is the density of the small body: the denser it is, the closer it can come. The Moon’s centre at 16,400 km lies well beyond the rigid limit (9,484–12,994 km for 1,300–3,344 kg m−3^{-3}). The same distance lies inside the fluid limit for a body of 2,600 kg m−3^{-3} (19,971 km, §A.7.1); which of the two limits applies is therefore decided by whether the Moon is rigid at release. The rounding was completed before release; the body is rigid and round. The flattening and the later rounding of the lunar body took place earlier, while it was still attached (Paper 7, The Poles); what leaves the line is a body with a rigid outer shell. A rigid body reduces the tidal response by 11+19μ2ρgR,\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{19\mu}{2\rho gR}}, so that for μ≥10\mu\ge10 GPa the axis ratio is at most 1.04. Here μ\mu is the rigidity (shear modulus) of the body, gg its surface gravity and RR its radius; ρgR\rho gR is the pressure of the body’s own weight. The factor says that a body whose rigidity is large compared with the pressure of its own weight deforms only a small fraction of what a fluid body would. An axis ratio of at most 1.04 means that the longest axis of the Moon exceeds the shortest by no more than 4%: the body remains round.

B.8The direction of tidal evolution

The Moon is released on the corotation line, and tides transfer angular momentum to it from the Earth’s spin: it recedes (§A.8; the orbit is Paper 4, The Orbit). Exactly on the line the tidal effect is neutral, since the Earth turns once in the same time as the Moon orbits. Separation itself carries the Moon’s centre of mass about 210 km beyond the line, when a little more than half of the Moon has crossed it (the margin Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0 of §D.3); there the Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, the tidal bulge raised on the Earth runs ahead of the Moon, pulls it forward, and the Moon moves outward. PART E

Conclusions

A body the size of the Moon can reach orbit from the Earth’s own material without a giant impact from outside. A fast-spinning Earth supplies the speed; the pear connects the Earth to the corotation line; the lengthening pear and the centralising core together set the Earth day at release to 5.77 h; the Moon, rigid and round, is released when its centre of mass reaches the corotation line at about 16,400 km; the angular-momentum budget closes at 1.007 times today’s value, consistent with the loss to solar tides; and the Moon recedes from the moment of release.

Put as a single chain: the Earth’s rotation carries the material outward; the line where carried speed equals orbital speed sets where the Moon leaves; the balance of the pear and the core sets where that line is; and the angular momentum the Earth started with is enough, with about 1% to spare for the Sun to remove later.

APPENDIX A

Derivation of corotation, definitions and constants

This appendix fixes the definitions on which every number of the paper rests. It supports the corotation radius of §A.2.1 and §A.5 (15,818 km for the 5.5-hour reference) and the release distance of §B.1. A reader who wants to check any result should begin here, because most apparent disagreements between reproductions come from a different choice of mass, distance or constant, not from a different physics.

The short full derivation is in §B.1. Two masses must be kept apart. MtotM_{\mathrm{tot}} is the total initial mass: the Earth and the lunar material together, before any of it has left. M⊕M_\oplus is the final parent mass: the Earth after the lunar material has been assigned to the second body. Using one where the other is meant counts the lunar mass twice or not at all.

Two distances must also be kept apart. A radius relative to the parent’s centre is not a radius relative to the centre of mass of the system; once a second body of real mass exists, the two bodies turn about their common centre of mass, which lies slightly away from the Earth’s centre. In the two-body case the distance rr is between the centres, and the gravitational parameter (the product of the gravitational constant and the mass that pulls) is G(M⊕+m)G(M_\oplus+m), because each body orbits under the pull of the other and the relative motion is governed by the sum of the masses.

The constants are the reproduced source inputs, not a new fit to an ephemeris (a table of observed positions). Changing the definition of the mass therefore requires a new calculation, not a silent change of the label 15,818: that number belongs to one stated definition, and a different definition produces a different number.

An adopted addition of 120 km gives a volumetric radius RV,0=6,517R_{V,0}=6{,}517 km (the radius of a sphere with the same volume as the flattened body) and a density of about 5,214 kg per cubic metre.

Step 3: the shape. A uniform fluid body that spins settles into a flattened figure, wider at the equator than through the poles. For a body of uniform density this figure is a Maclaurin spheroid, and its shape is fixed by one equation [1]:

ℳ(e)=21−e2e3(3−2e2)arcsine−6(1−e2)e2,ω2πGρ=ℳ(e).\mathcal M(e)=\frac{2\sqrt{1-e^2}}{e^3}(3-2e^2)\arcsin e -\frac{6(1-e^2)}{e^2}, \qquad \frac{\omega^2}{\pi G\rho}=\mathcal M(e).

In words: the left side, ω2/(πGρ)\omega^2/(\pi G\rho), measures how fast the body spins compared with how strongly its own gravity holds it together (ω\omega is the angular velocity, ρ\rho the density). The right side, ℳ(e)\mathcal M(e), is the flattening that balance requires, written through the eccentricity ee of the meridian section — e=0e=0 is a sphere, and ee grows as the body flattens. Given the spin and the density, the equation is solved for ee.

Once ee is known, the equatorial radius aa and the polar radius cc follow from the volumetric radius:

a=RV(1−e2)1/6,c=RV(1−e2)1/3.a=\frac{R_V}{(1-e^2)^{1/6}}, \qquad c=R_V(1-e^2)^{1/3}.

These two expressions keep the volume fixed: a2c=RV3a^2c=R_V^3, so the body flattens without gaining or losing material.

For a given spin below the maximum the Maclaurin equation has two solutions, one slightly flattened and one strongly flattened. A body spun up gradually from a sphere follows the first. On the branch near the spherical shape one obtains e0≃0.4107e_0\simeq0.4107, a0≃6,721a_0\simeq6{,}721 km and c0≃6,128c_0\simeq6{,}128 km — an equator about 600 km further from the centre than the poles.

Step 4: the moment of inertia and the angular momentum. The moment of inertia measures how hard a body is to set spinning, and depends on how far its mass lies from the axis. For a uniform spheroid turning about its short axis it is 25\frac25 of the mass times the square of the equatorial radius. The angular momentum is the moment of inertia times the angular velocity:

I0=25Mtota02≃1.092×1038kgm2,I_0=\frac25M_{\mathrm{tot}}a_0^2\simeq1.092\times10^{38}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2},

Linitial=I0ω0≃3.466×1034kgm2s−1.\boxed{L_{\mathrm{initial}}=I_0\omega_0\simeq3.466\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}.}

Here ω0=2π/19,800\omega_0=2\pi/19{,}800 s−1^{-1}, the angular velocity of a 5.5-hour day. This boxed number is the whole budget the transfer may draw on; §A.6 compares it with the present Earth–Moon system.

Uniform density is a calculation input. It describes a parent still at an early stage of sorting: iron may already have been gathering in the core at the start, and the organisation of the core continued through the transfer and after release. A body that has not completed differentiation is not necessarily uniform; the effect of departures from uniform density is examined below in a sensitivity test (Appendix C and §A.4.1).

APPENDIX C

The differentiation calculation and the sensitivity tests

This appendix describes how the two-layer calculation behind §A.4 and §A.4.1 was set up, and how far its numbers can be trusted. It supports the table of final periods and corotation radii against the density ratio DD (for D=2D=2: 5.77 hours and 16,331 km), and the value k=0.360k=0.360 of §B.6. The physical idea is the skater drawing in her arms: when iron sinks toward the centre, the moment of inertia falls and, with the angular momentum fixed, the spin speeds up.

The model has two layers: an inner one and an outer one. qq is the ratio of the inner volumetric radius to the outer, and DD is the ratio of their densities. The layers are incompressible and in shared rotation, so they turn together as one body. The volume of the final body is fixed across the scenarios; the initial state used to set L0L_0 remains homogeneous, so every scenario starts from the same budget and differs only in how the final body has arranged its mass.

For each scenario a constant effective potential is solved on both boundaries: each boundary is required to be a surface of equal combined gravitational and centrifugal potential, which is the condition for a fluid layer to be at rest in the rotating frame. II is then computed directly from density and shape, not taken from a formula for a uniform body. There is no differentiation path in time and no equation of state here: the calculation compares end states, and does not follow the sinking of iron step by step.

Two checks set the precision. Allowing the boundary shapes to vary (instead of forcing them to be exact spheroids) changed the radius by up to about 2.5 km in the q=0.55q=0.55 scan — small against the corotation radius of more than 15,000 km. Changing the numerical resolution changed the radii by a few metres; the field residuals do not converge monotonically in every check, so no claim of metre-level physical precision is made. The results are reliable to the kilometre level, which is the level at which the paper uses them.

The vector correction to the budget and the hand-off to Paper 4

The main calculation releases the Moon in the equatorial plane. This appendix asks what changes if the release point lies off the equator. It supports the budget equation of §A.6.6 and the sensitivity values quoted there. The point is geometric: off the equator, the orbital angular momentum of the Moon and the spin angular momentum of the Earth no longer point along the same axis, and angular momenta that point in different directions add as arrows, not as plain numbers.

In a frame where zz is the final spin axis, and λ\lambda is the latitude of the release point measured relative to its equator, the total angular momentum is:

HH is the orbital angular momentum of the Moon: μred\mu_{\mathrm{red}} is the reduced mass of the pair, ω\omega the common angular velocity and rcosλr\cos\lambda the distance of the release point from the spin axis — only that distance counts for the speed the rotation supplies. APPENDIX E

Roche calculations and the survival assumptions

This appendix gives the background to §A.7 and §B.7: the two Roche limits and a first estimate of how strongly the Earth’s tide loads the young Moon. The Roche limit is the distance inside which the difference between the Earth’s pull on the near and far sides of a satellite (the tidal stretch) exceeds the satellite’s own gravity holding it together. A body that deforms freely, like a fluid, elongates and so increases the stretch on itself; its limit lies further out (coefficient 2.44). A body that keeps its shape has a limit further in (coefficient 1.26). The question this appendix answers is how large the tidal load is at the release distance, and what more is needed to turn that load into a survival result.

The constants 1.26 and 2.44 and the definitions of the limits were kept from the previous text. A focused verification of the source for the so-called rigid coefficient and of its domain of validity must be completed before submission. It contains no strength law for a crust: the rigid limit describes a body that does not change shape, without saying what material strength makes this possible.

Estimate of acceleration and strain — a previous calculation text preserved

The estimate compares two accelerations. Δa\Delta a is the tidal stretch: the difference between the Earth’s pull at the centre of the body and at its surface, along the line to the Earth. gsg_s is the body’s own surface gravity. For a body radius Rs=1,890R_s=1{,}890 km:

Δa≃2GM⊕Rsr3,gs=GmRs2.\Delta a\simeq\frac{2GM_\oplus R_s}{r^3}, \qquad g_s=\frac{Gm}{R_s^2}.

The stretch falls with the cube of the distance rr: halving the distance raises it eightfold. At 15,818 km one obtains Δa≃0.381\Delta a\simeq0.381, gs≃1.373g_s\simeq1.373 m/s² and a ratio of about 0.277. The ratio compares the tidal stretch with self-gravity: below one, self-gravity wins, and at this distance the tide is a little over a quarter of the body’s own gravity. A ratio below one is necessary but does not settle the matter for a deforming body; the deformation of a hot body over the years it spends inside the fluid limit is the survival condition of §D.3.

The next step converts the acceleration into a stress and the stress into a time. A stress estimate can be built:

σtidal∼ρsΔaRs∼1.9×109Pa,tstrain∼ε*ησtidal.\sigma_{\mathrm{tidal}}\sim\rho_s\Delta aR_s\sim1.9\times10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad t_{\mathrm{strain}}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_*\eta}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tidal}}}.

The first expression is the weight-like load of a column of the body’s material, of density ρs\rho_s and height RsR_s, under the acceleration Δa\Delta a. The second says how long a material of viscosity η\eta (its resistance to flow, in pascal-seconds) takes to reach a strain ε*\varepsilon_* — a fractional change of shape — under that stress: the stiffer the material, the longer it takes.

Choosing ε*=0.5\varepsilon_*=0.5 (a change of shape by half) and η=1019Pas\eta=10^{19}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s} gives about 85 years (with ρs=2,600\rho_s=2{,}600 kg/m3^3, Δa≃0.381\Delta a\simeq0.381 m/s2^2 and Rs=1,890R_s=1{,}890 km the stress is 1.87×1091.87\times10^9 Pa; an earlier version of this estimate gave 1.4×1091.4\times10^9 Pa and about 116 years, which corresponds to a body density of about 1,950 kg/m3^3). This is an estimate of strain under a constant representative stress, not a calculation of a disruption time. The rheology (how the material responds to load over time), the shape, the varying stress and the actual residence time must be examined.

This estimate is kept as a parametric dependence: it shows how the time scales with viscosity and stress, so that a reader can insert other values. The residence time itself belongs to the orbital calculation and is not adopted here. The card to accompany every calculation

The table above is turned into a working rule: a short card that any calculation in the series states before its result, so that a reader can see at once which state it describes.

Before an equation or a table of results, state: (1) stage and time; (2) point of measurement; (3) frame and axis; (4) scenario version; (5) masses and radii at the same stage; (6) heat and material inputs; (7) effects neglected; (8) result and sensitivity. If a datum is missing, the calculation is marked conditional or deferred — a value must not be borrowed from the present or from another stage without justification.

The mass bookkeeping is written as one equation. Mass-management equation: Mtotal=ME+mbody+Mtransit+MescapedM_{\mathrm{total}}=M_E+m_{\mathrm{body}}+M_{\mathrm{transit}}+M_{\mathrm{escaped}}, according to the system and reservoir boundaries: the Earth, the developing body, the material on its way between them, and any material lost from the system. If a closed system without loss is chosen, or material is assigned beyond one of the bodies, this must be stated and the terms reduced accordingly. The mass entering GM must match the reservoir definition in the same calculation.

The combined gravitational field must be computed from the actual mass distribution. A two-point-mass approximation is permitted only where and at the precision defined; it is not a full description of the Earth’s interior, the connection or a local anomaly. In a decomposition into background and anomaly, only the difference mass is added, so that no mass is counted twice.
