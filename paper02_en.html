MASS CLARIFICATION

Before the lunar reservoir separates, the mass of the lunar material is wholly included in the mass of the parent body. With the transfer, the same mass is subtracted from the parent and assigned wholly to the second body. The distinction between the total initial mass and the mass used in the reference calculation prevents double counting and the mixing of states.

In practice, the mass of the Moon, 7.35×1022 kg, is counted only once. Before the transfer it is included in the parent mass, 6.0455×1024 kg; after it, it is the Moon, and the Earth remains with a final mass of 5.972×1024 kg, with no loss of mass from the system.

Abstract

The idea. Material that is part of a spinning planet, or still attached to it, turns with it: it completes one turn in every planetary day. This is corotation. The tangential speed of such material is the spin rate times the distance from the centre, so it grows the farther out the material is. The speed needed for a free circular orbit behaves the opposite way: it falls with distance. The point where the two speeds are equal is the corotation radius. Material carried out to that distance while still turning with the parent, and released there, already moves at orbital speed; no mechanism is needed to throw it.

The question. The paper examines whether transferring lunar material outward from a terrestrial parent, while corotation is maintained, can lead to a state whose orbital velocity and angular-momentum budget approximately match the present Earth–Moon system. (Angular momentum is the amount of rotation a system holds; internal processes can only pass it from one part to another, and only outside forces, such as the Sun’s tides, can remove it from the system.) The central distinction is between the velocity required at the moment of release and the mechanical capacity and angular-momentum budget needed to reach that state. In a central-field model the corotation radius follows from equating the rotational speed and the orbital speed. For an initial rotation period of 5.5 hours the reference values are a corotation radius of 15,818 km and an orbital speed of 5,020 m/s.

The budget. The parent is described as a uniform Maclaurin spheroid (the flattened equilibrium shape of a spinning fluid body) with a volume-equivalent radius of 6,517 km. The computed initial angular momentum is about 3.466×1034kgm2s−13.466\times10^{34}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}, a value about 0.7% above the sum of the magnitudes of the present angular-momentum components — a small surplus, which is what the later loss to solar tides requires. Vector addition at the angles tested gives a surplus of about 1.5–2.5%; this is a sensitivity test, not a full vector reconstruction of the evolution. The budget depends on the nearly uniform structure and on the adopted thermal expansion.

The release. Two opposing processes change the Earth’s spin during the transfer of mass: the lengthening pear carries angular momentum outward and slows the spin, and the centralising core — iron sinking inward — speeds it up. The two act together; with D=2D=2 the Earth’s rotation period at release is 5.77 hours, and the corotation line moves out to about 16,400 km, where the Moon’s centre of mass is released. The angular momentum transferred to the lunar material is about 15.6% in the base calculation and about 16.5% in the coupled solution. The final orbital angular momentum is about 17.7% of the initial budget in the coupled solution with the pear alone (D=1D=1), and about 17.0% in the release state with D=2D=2 (§B.1). These are transfers within the system.

The result. The paper establishes the kinematic solution and the budgetary end state of the route: the speed a corotating mass needs is supplied by the rotation itself at the corotation radius, and the angular momentum the transfer requires is available in the parent. What the route then requires of the mechanism — that the body be released beyond the corotation radius, that it survive the years it spends inside the fluid Roche limit, and that the connection carry the load until then — is stated as a set of explicit conditions in Part D. Paper 3 examines those conditions.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

The Moon’s material left the Earth while still turning with it, and was released where the speed it already had was the speed a free orbit requires. That place is the corotation radius: 15,818 km for a parent with a rotation period of 5.5 hours, at a speed of 5,020 m/s. The objection that led to the abandonment of the fission route — an angular-momentum budget four times larger than required — was evaluated at the parent’s surface, under a release condition this route never uses. Evaluated at the corotation radius, the parent’s budget exceeds that of the present system by about 1%, with the sign that solar tidal loss requires, and the classical route needs 2.74 times what this route needs.

The route states its threshold conditions explicitly: a parent still at an early stage of sorting at the start of the transfer of mass (k≳0.397k\gtrsim0.397), thermally expanded by 98–520 km; a body released beyond the corotation radius, and not exactly on it; an interior stiff enough to survive 10110^1–10310^3 years inside the fluid Roche limit; and a connection able to carry the load until then. Each of these is a defined requirement, each is passed to the paper that supplies it, and all of them are listed in §D.3. The requirements of the model, and what would test it, are collected in Paper 11 (The Requirements).

Why the old objection does not apply. The classical fission model assumed that the Earth throws material directly off its own surface. At the surface of a parent with a rotation period of 5.5 hours, the tangential rotational speed is only about 2,133 m/s, while the speed needed for a free orbit at the same place is about 7,701 m/s (§A.1.2). Closing the velocity gap at the surface requires a much faster spin of the whole Earth, and a faster spin means more angular momentum: at the classical point where a spinning fluid body becomes unstable (the Jacobi bifurcation, §A.6.5) the parent needs 2.74 times the angular momentum of the present Earth–Moon system, and the earlier reference calculation, made at another point of the same sequence, indicated a requirement of about four times. The system does not have such angular momentum, and this is the finding that led to the abandonment of the fission theory. The LEF route closes the gap in a different way: it does not increase the Earth’s spin, but carries the material outward while it turns with the parent. The tangential speed grows with distance at the same spin rate, until it equals the orbital speed at the corotation radius. The spin rate needed for this is the one the Earth would have had if all of the system’s present angular momentum were returned to it — about 5.5 hours (§A.2) — and the angular-momentum budget then needed is only 1.007 times today’s value. An everyday picture: a child on a roundabout who walks from the centre to the rim moves faster at the rim, without anyone pushing the roundabout harder.

What the requirements mean. Mass distribution: kk is the moment-of-inertia coefficient, and it measures how the mass of a body is spread between centre and surface. A uniform homogeneous sphere has k=0.4k=0.4; today’s Earth, with its iron core, has about 0.33. The model requires k≳0.397k\gtrsim0.397 at the start of the transfer of mass, that is, a parent still at an early stage of sorting: mass that is not concentrated toward the centre stores more angular momentum at the same spin rate. The organisation of the Earth’s iron core is one continuous process that never stopped: during the bulge stage it was at its beginning, it continued while the pear grew, and it went on after release, while the Earth took the pear back in. There was probably never a fully uniform period: the earlier the stage, the less sorted the Earth. Iron may already have begun to gather in the core at the start; the process continued. Thermal expansion: the young Earth must be hot, and therefore larger than today: an expansion in the range 98 to 520 km is admissible, and the adopted working value is 120 km. Release margin: the release must take place beyond the corotation radius and not exactly on it. On the line itself the tide is neutral and pushes in neither direction, while a body lying even slightly beyond the line is pushed outward by the tide, so that it recedes from the start (Paper 4); the margin needed is small, Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0, about 210 km (Paper 3, §C.5a). Survival at the Roche limit: the fluid Roche limit is the distance inside which the Earth’s tidal forces would pull apart a body held together by its own gravity alone. A body with internal strength survives closer in, down to its rigid limit; the Moon at release is a rigid, round body, having completed its flattening and rounding earlier, while still attached to the parent (Paper 7). Load transmission: the connection between the two bodies — the pear — must carry the load until the moment of release. These requirements are not hidden in the results; they are defined explicitly in §D.3, and Paper 3 tests them.

Introduction — Part A: the release condition in the context of existing models

Two kinds of evidence bear on the Moon’s origin: how its material could have reached orbit (the dynamics of separation), and whether its composition matches the body it came from. Darwin’s work on tides and rotation is part of the historical background for examining the evolution of the Earth–Moon system [6]; his fission idea — that a fast-spinning Earth shed the Moon — is the classical form of a terrestrial origin. In this paper the quantitative comparison with classical fission is made through a defined equilibrium configuration. The bounds derived from this configuration hold for it alone, and must not be turned into a bound on every possible path of terrestrial separation. In other words, showing that one particular fission path needs too much angular momentum does not show that every path from the Earth does.

In the giant-impact family of models, the Moon forms from debris after another body strikes the Earth, and the isotopic composition places constraints on the mixing of terrestrial and impactor material. Different versions of the model exist, and the constraints depend on the compositions and on the manner of mixing [7]. In the LEF scenario, construction from a shared terrestrial reservoir is examined: the Moon is made of the Earth’s own material, so a close isotopic match follows directly. A shared source provides a basis for compositional comparison, but does not replace the test of transfer to orbit. That test is the subject of this paper.

The focused question of this paper is whether carrying mass outward in shared rotation changes the velocity condition and the angular-momentum budget relative to direct release from the surface. The answer the paper gives is positive: the release condition moves from the surface to the corotation radius, and both the speed and the budget then fit. The mechanism of construction — the feed from within and the rise of the outer shell as the surface of the same body — is the subject of Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice. The present paper defines the physical conditions the mechanism must meet. The division is deliberate: Paper 2 examines the feasibility of the route in principle, using standard physics; Paper 3 shows how it is carried out.

Introduction — Part B: what the paper presents

The paper is built as a set of problems followed by their solutions, so that each objection is met in the place where it is raised. Part A sets out the constraints the route must meet, one by one: the tangential velocity gap at the surface, which led to the rejection of classical fission; what must be calculated; the pear that connects the Earth to the corotation line; the offset between the lengthening pear, which slows the spin, and the centralising core, which speeds it up; corotation itself; the angular-momentum budget; the fluid Roche limit; and the direction of tidal evolution. Part B solves them in the same order, starting from one calculation. Part C summarises what has been shown; Part D collects the claims, tests and requirements; Part E states the conclusions. Appendices A–G give the mathematical derivations, the numerical sensitivity scans, the Roche calculations and the parameters of the full computational environment, so that every number in the main text can be traced and reproduced.

THE EQUATORIAL PLANE

For computational simplicity and clarity of principle, the calculation and the whole of this paper treat the release of the Moon from the equatorial plane. Paper 4 shows that the Moon left from another latitude — 22.5, the centre of the contact area — and computes the release there in full.

The kinematic analysis of this paper is carried out in the equatorial plane, defined as the simplest geometric case: there the tangential rotational speed is greatest, and the corotation radius is a single distance. Moving the release to a higher latitude changes the numerical values to a limited extent, without changing the mechanical principle. The size of the change is shown by the 25° release sensitivity test of the environment table, and the adopted release solution, at a latitude of 22.5°, is computed in full in Paper 4.

PART A

The problems before us

THE PROBLEM AND HOW IT IS TESTED

Each section of this Part states one mechanical condition that a body built from the Earth’s own material must meet if it is to reach an independent orbit whole. Part B answers these conditions in the same order.

This Part breaks the dynamical problem into a series of successive physical conditions that the route must meet. It begins with the difficulty that closed the classical fission route — a body leaving the surface of a fast-spinning Earth does not have the speed of an orbit — and then examines, one after another: where the matching orbital speed is reached; what mechanical connection joins the Earth to that place; how the parent’s spin changes while the mass is carried out; whether the total angular-momentum budget suffices; whether the body withstands the Earth’s differential tide (the Roche limit); and what the direction of the orbit’s tidal evolution is afterwards. Each section yields a defined quantity, which Part B computes.

A.1The problem: the velocity gap

A.1.1Two separate dynamical questions

A terrestrial-origin scenario requires an explanation for the transfer of material from the parent body to a free orbit. A compositional match can support a shared source, but does not determine how the material reached orbit: two bodies may be made of the same material and still leave open the question of how one of them came to circle the other. The focus of this paper is therefore the dynamics. The original historical review is examined here by comparing defined equilibrium configurations, and not by asserting that every terrestrial-origin path is subject to the same fission threshold.

Analysing such a scenario requires two mechanical questions to be kept apart. The first is the release-velocity condition: under what conditions the velocity of the material at the moment of separation is consistent with a free orbit — that is, whether, at the moment it is let go, the material moves fast enough sideways to keep falling around the Earth without falling back onto it. The second is the angular-momentum budget: whether the angular momentum available at the start of the process permits the dynamical end state and the evolution that follows. Changing the release condition can change the velocity test, but the overall angular-momentum budget must still be satisfied. A route can pass the first test and fail the second, so both are computed.

Angular momentum and the moment of inertia. Angular momentum is the quantity of rotational motion a body carries. For a spinning body it is the product of the spin rate and the way the mass is spread around the axis. The spin angular momentum is defined by the equation:

Lspin=Iω.L_{\mathrm{spin}}=I\omega.

where ω\omega is the angular velocity (the angle the body turns per second, 2π2\pi divided by the period), and II is the moment of inertia: the sum, over every part of the body, of the mass of each part times the square of its distance from the spin axis. The moment of inertia measures how far from the axis the mass sits; mass far from the axis counts much more than mass near it, because the distance enters squared. For a body of mass MM and equatorial radius aa the moment of inertia is written in the form I=kMa2I=kMa^2, where the dimensionless coefficient kk defines the internal mass distribution: for a uniform body k=0.4k=0.4, and for a body with a dense core kk is smaller.

In the absence of an external torque — a twisting force acting on the system from outside — the total angular momentum is conserved: it cannot change, but only pass from one part of the system to another. In the Earth–Moon system the only significant external torque is the tide raised by the Sun, and it has removed only about 1% of the total angular momentum over the history of the system (§A.2). This conservation is what makes the problem calculable: whatever the Moon carries in its orbit today was once in the spin of the parent.

Transferring mass outward in corotation — that is, while it keeps turning with the parent at the parent’s own angular velocity — requires the parent to supply additional angular momentum to the transferred material, because the same angular velocity at a larger distance means a larger moment of inertia for that material, in proportion to the square of the distance. Since there is no external torque in this process, it is a redistribution of existing angular momentum: the angular momentum gained by the transferred material is subtracted directly from the parent’s spin.

A.1.2Release from the surface

We first examine the classical case: mass let go directly from the equator of a fast-spinning parent. In the reference state the period is T0=5.5T_0=5.5 hours and the equatorial radius is a0≃6,721a_0\simeq6{,}721 km. Since 5.5 hours are 19,800 seconds, the angular velocity of the parent is:

ω0=2π19,800≃3.17333×10−4rads−1.\omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{19{,}800}\simeq3.17333\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}.

Two speeds are now compared at the equator. The first is the tangential speed that the rotation itself gives to a point on the surface, vrotv_{\mathrm{rot}}: the angular velocity times the distance from the axis. The second is the circular orbital speed vcircv_{\mathrm{circ}}: the speed at which a free body at the same distance would keep falling around the Earth in a circle, its centrifugal tendency exactly balancing gravity.

vrot=ω0a0≃2,133ms−1,vcirc=GM⊕a0≃7,701ms−1.v_{\mathrm{rot}}=\omega_0a_0\simeq2{,}133\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, \qquad v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt{\frac{GM_\oplus}{a_0}}\simeq7{,}701\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}.

Even with a day of only 5.5 hours, the parent’s surface speed is less than a third of the speed an orbit at that place requires. The velocity difference, in the rounding of the original reference calculation of this paper, is about 5,568 m/s. This is the velocity gap.

What orbit would such material follow if it were simply let go? Without an additional radial velocity, the specific orbital energy (the orbital energy per kilogram, kinetic minus gravitational) and the semi-major axis (half the long axis of the resulting ellipse) are:

ε=v22−GM⊕r≃−5.70×107Jkg−1,Aorb=−GM⊕2ε≃3,495km.\varepsilon=\frac{v^2}{2}-\frac{GM_\oplus}{r}\simeq-5.70\times10^7\ \mathrm{J\,kg^{-1}}, \qquad A_{\mathrm{orb}}=-\frac{GM_\oplus}{2\varepsilon}\simeq3{,}495\ \mathrm{km}.

The negative energy indicates strong gravitational binding, and the semi-major axis, 3,495 km, is about half the parent’s equatorial radius. Since the release point is the farthest point of the ellipse (the apoapsis), the whole of the rest of the path lies deep inside the parent. Under these initial conditions the Keplerian orbit penetrates the parent, and the material falls back. Surface velocity alone therefore does not provide a free orbit that remains outside it. This is the core of the classical objection to fission: closing the gap at the surface requires the parent to spin several times faster, and this requires far more angular momentum than the Earth–Moon system holds (§A.6.5).

The numbers in §A.2 preserve the original reference comparison, in which GM of the final Earth mass was used in the test-particle approximation. They are not a calculation of the exact field of the whole initial parent. This definition distinguishes the reproduced reference comparison from the overall budget in §A.6.

A.1.3The transfer condition examined

The LEF route does not release the material at the surface. In the scenario examined, the material is carried outward while still attached and receives angular momentum, so that it keeps turning with the parent at a uniform angular velocity. The two speeds then behave in opposite ways. The tangential speed ωr\omega r grows with distance, because a point that completes one turn per day travels a larger circle the farther out it is — like a child who walks outward on a turning roundabout and is carried round faster and faster. The required orbital speed GM/r\sqrt{GM/r} decreases, because the gravitational field weakens with distance and less sideways speed is needed to balance it. The velocity gap of §A.1.2 therefore narrows continuously as the material rises, and at a certain distance — the point of equality — it closes.

The present paper concentrates on computing the point of equality and the angular-momentum cost of the transfer; the ascent process itself is a separate mechanical question. Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice, examines the transport capacity and the connection; the detailed pressure calculation lies outside the budget calculation presented here. The orbital initial conditions after separation are handed to Paper 4, The Orbit.

A.2What must be calculated

The problem now has a definite shape. The model defines five quantities required for the solution: the release distance of the Moon; the length of the Earth day at the moment of separation; whether the available angular momentum suffices; whether the body survives the differential tidal forces (the Roche test); and whether it recedes after release.

Why 5.5 hours. The initial day is not a free variable; it follows from conservation of angular momentum run backwards in time. All of today’s Earth–Moon angular momentum, 3.44×10343.44\times10^{34} kg m2^2 s−1^{-1} — the Earth’s spin together with the Moon’s orbit — is returned to the spin of a young, hot, expanded Earth still at an early stage of sorting (k=0.4k=0.4 as the calculation input). “Expanded” means that the hot early Earth was somewhat larger than the cold Earth of today; “an early stage of sorting” means that the organisation of the core had only begun, so the mass was spread nearly uniformly and the uniform value k=0.4k=0.4 describes it closely. Putting the whole budget into the spin of such a body gives the period: T0=2πI0L≃5.54h.T_0=\frac{2\pi I_0}{L}\simeq5.54\ \mathrm{h}. In words: a body with moment of inertia I0I_0 carrying angular momentum LL turns at ω=L/I0\omega=L/I_0, and its period is 2π/ω2\pi/\omega. Solar tides have since removed about 1% of the system’s total angular momentum (about 1.35% when the receding Moon is followed; Eulenfeld & Heubeck 2023; Canup 2004). A period of 5.5 hours therefore carries today’s angular momentum and the roughly 1% that was lost: it is slightly shorter than 5.54 hours because the early system held slightly more. Assuming a young Sun more massive by a few per cent raises this loss only to 1–2%.

A.2.1The reference state

For GM⊕≃3.986×1014m3s−2GM_\oplus\simeq3.986\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{m^3\,s^{-2}} and T0=5.5T_0=5.5 hours, the point of equality at which the tangential rotational speed equals the orbital speed defines the corotation radius (derived in §A.5), and the common speed there:

This is the central number of the paper. At 15,818 km, more than twice the parent’s equatorial radius, a body that completes one turn every 5.5 hours with the Earth already moves at 5,020 m/s, which is exactly the speed of a circular orbit at that distance. A body held there in corotation and then let go does not need any additional push: at the moment of separation it is already in orbit.

The distance is measured from the centre of the central body in the test-particle approximation, which neglects the orbiting body’s own mass in the gravitational pull. Substituting this radius into the two velocity formulas confirms that the speeds are equal.

The derivation is an application of the equations of motion in a central field [8]. If an initial state is computed with the full parent mass, MtotM_{\mathrm{tot}} must be inserted in the formula. The value 15,818 km remains the reference point defined in the table, and the coupled solution in §A.4 explicitly includes the division of mass.

A.3The pear

The corotation radius lies far above the surface, so something must carry the material there while it keeps turning with the Earth. In the model, the transport of mass is carried out by a continuous material bridge called “the pear”: a hill that rises from the Earth, with a base about 9,000 km wide at the surface, sides that form a single arc without a narrowing (a waist), and at its tip a contact disc about 3,000 km wide at the Moon.

The pear bridges about 7,800 km between the Earth’s surface and the Moon. It lies entirely inside the corotation line, so it is pulled inward: at every point along it, gravity is stronger than the acceleration the rotation needs to keep the material circling, so the pear’s own material is held by the Earth and does not fly off. Only the body at its tip reaches the line. The pear’s length and mass are a requirement: the Moon was released as a whole unit, so a pear must exist that meets the conditions. How the pear is built and how it carries the load are the subject of Paper 3.

A.4 The offset: two processes acting together

To determine the length of the Earth day at release, two processes that act on the Earth’s spin at the same time, and in opposite directions, must be taken into account. This matters because the day at release fixes the position of the corotation line, and therefore the place where the Moon is released.

1. The pear lengthens. Transporting mass outward with the rotation carries angular momentum with it; the angular momentum that leaves the Earth slows its spin. A child walking outward on a roundabout slows the roundabout in the same way.

2. The core centralises. Differentiation is the separation of an initially mixed body into layers by density: iron, the densest component, sinks toward the centre and forms a core, while the lighter materials remain above it. In the LEF model the organisation of the core is one continuous process: it began before the transfer, continued while the pear grew, and went on after release. Fast rotation speeds up differentiation: the inward migration of iron reduces the Earth’s moment of inertia, because mass moves closer to the axis. With a smaller moment of inertia and the same angular momentum, the body must turn faster. As with a skater drawing in her arms, the spin speeds up.

Angular momentum is conserved, and the two processes offset each other: the result is the offset between them. A calculation that takes only one of them into account does not describe the Earth of that time — the pear alone, without differentiation, would give too long a day, and the core alone, without the pear, too short a one.

A.4.1 The effect of differentiation on the final state

A.5 Corotation

This section derives the corotation radius used throughout the paper, and defines the domain in which its use is valid.

Figure 4 plots the two speeds of §A.1.3 against distance from the Earth’s centre. The speed carried by the rotation rises in a straight line, while the orbital speed falls. Inside the crossing point the rotation supplies less than an orbit requires, and the material is pulled inward; outside it the rotation supplies more than an orbit requires, and the material tends outward. The crossing point is the corotation line.

Point of application: the laws of corotation and of gravitational balance apply to the body’s centre of mass, not to its geometric centre, because the balance between gravity and rotation applies to the body as a whole, and the whole body moves as if its mass were gathered at its centre of mass. The Moon’s centre of mass is displaced slightly toward the Earth (in today’s system the displacement is about 2 km), so the corotation forces may have pulled the Moon slightly after its geometric centre crossed the line: the geometric centre crosses the corotation line first, and only after it the centre of mass. Release takes place slightly after the centre of mass crosses the line, about 210 km beyond it (§D.3; Paper 3, §C.5a).

A.5.1 The derivation

In the test-particle approximation, in the equatorial plane and in a central gravitational field, the speed carried by the rotation is the angular velocity times the distance. The circular orbital speed follows from setting the gravitational pull equal to the force needed to keep a mass mm on a circle of radius RR:

vrot=ωR,GMmR2=mvcirc2R,vcirc=GMR.v_{\mathrm{rot}}=\omega R, \qquad \frac{GMm}{R^2}=\frac{mv_{\mathrm{circ}}^2}{R}, \qquad v_{\mathrm{circ}}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}}.

Since the mass of the orbiting body mm cancels from both sides of the equation, the orbital speed at a given distance is the same for any body — for a grain and for a Moon alike. Equating the speeds gives the relation:

ωR=GMR⇒ω2R3=GM.\omega R=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{R}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega^2R^3=GM.

Solving for RR, and substituting ω=2π/T\omega=2\pi/T, gives:

Rsync=(GMω2)1/3=(GMT24π2)1/3\boxed{R_{\mathrm{sync}}=\left(\frac{GM}{\omega^2}\right)^{1/3} =\left(\frac{GMT^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}}

The equation shows that the corotation radius is a function only of the mass of the central body and of its own rotation period, and grows as the period to the power two-thirds: a slower spin pushes the line outward. Hence a slowing of the spin (the lengthening of the day by the pear) moves the line outward, while a speeding of the spin (the centralising core) brings it back inward (§A.4).

For a positive, constant mass and angular velocity there is a single positive root. This is the synchronous-orbit relation of standard orbital mechanics — the same relation that places geostationary satellites above today’s Earth. What is new in LEF is not the relation itself but the place where it is evaluated: the classical objection evaluated the speed condition at the parent’s surface, while the LEF route evaluates it at the corotation radius.

A.5.2 The release condition and the domain of validity

Equatorial orbital criterion: the equality fits an equatorial circular orbit provided the radial velocity is zero and the contact forces cease — that is, the body moves purely sideways, and the connection no longer pushes or pulls on it. When there is additional velocity, or when the release takes place off the equator, the initial-condition vectors must be computed. The orbital detail belongs to Paper 4; in this paper only the correction needed for the angular-momentum budget is kept, in §A.6 and Appendix D.

Energy and momentum: reaching the corotation radius does not cancel the work required to lift the material or to transfer angular momentum to it: the speed condition is met at the line, but energy and angular momentum still have to be delivered to the material along the way.

Corrections to the central field: the analytical description is simplified. The field of a flattened parent, the size of the developing body and two-body gravity add corrections to the central description. The coupled solution (§A.4) includes a partial two-body correction, but is not a full solution of two deformed figures connected to each other.

A.5.3 The meaning of the condition for release

The coupled solution in §A.4 defines a budgetary end state: how much angular momentum is in the Earth’s spin and how much in the Moon’s orbit at the moment of release. It does not by itself determine the onset of recession. An added radial velocity is also not a sufficient condition for sustained recession, because it changes the eccentricity (how elongated the orbit is) and the tides. The lag model and the rheology (the description of how the material deforms and flows under load) must be mutually consistent [3,4]. The direction of the tidal push on each side of the line is set out in §A.8.

A.5.4 The boundary between Paper 2 and Paper 4

Paper 2 fixes the mass, the spin and the angular-momentum budget handed over as the end state for the orbital test. This paper does not compute a recession time, and does not impose a general prohibition on a free orbit inside the corotation radius. Paper 4 receives the budget data (§A.6 and Appendix D) and treats the orbital elements, the tilt and the evolution after release.

The connected state: in the connected scenario, the free-body equations are replaced by the equations of the connected state where they cease to apply: while the Moon is attached to the Earth through the pear, it is held by the connection and is not a free body.

A.6The angular-momentum budget

The velocity gap is solved at the corotation radius. The second question of §A.1.1 remains: does the parent’s angular-momentum budget suffice to place the Moon at the release point while remaining consistent with the system we see today? This section builds the budget step by step: the parent’s initial angular momentum, the cost of carrying the material out, the comparison with the present system, the sensitivity to period, expansion and structure, and the comparison with the classical fission route.

A.6.1The parent and the initial budget

The initial parent mass is the sum of the masses of the Earth and the Moon, Mtot=M⊕+mM_{\mathrm{tot}}=M_\oplus+m. At this stage the material to be transferred is still included in the parent’s moment of inertia. The body is described as a Maclaurin spheroid: the equilibrium shape of a uniform fluid body in steady rotation, flattened at the poles and bulging at the equator, symmetric about its axis; the faster it spins, the flatter it is. In the homogeneous Maclaurin model, under the assumptions of the reference state, the following values are obtained [1]:

The resulting value, 3.466×10343.466\times10^{34}, is the whole angular-momentum reservoir the route has to work with. The shape equation and the full substitution are detailed in Appendix B. The assumption of a uniform body serves as the reference; changes of structure are examined below within the sensitivity analysis.

A.6.2 The cost of the transfer in the base calculation

Before the transfer, the lunar material is part of the parent, turns with it and carries its share of the parent’s angular momentum. Assuming a uniform mass distribution, the mean squared distance of the material from the spin axis is the same as that of the whole body, and its share of the angular momentum is:

L0,mat=mω0⟨r⊥2⟩=25mω0a02≃4.21×1032kgm2s−1.L_{0,\mathrm{mat}}=m\omega_0\left\langle r_\perp^2\right\rangle =\frac25m\omega_0a_0^2\simeq4.21\times10^{32}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}}.

Here ⟨r⊥2⟩\langle r_\perp^2\rangle is the average, over the material, of the squared distance from the spin axis. When the same mass is carried out to the corotation radius and still turns with the parent, its angular momentum grows with the square of the distance from the axis:

Lorb,0=mω0Rsync,02≃5.83×1033kgm2s−1,L_{\mathrm{orb},0}=m\omega_0R_{\mathrm{sync},0}^2\simeq5.83\times10^{33}\ \mathrm{kg\,m^2\,s^{-1}},

Lorb,0−L0,matLinitial≃15.6%.\boxed{\frac{L_{\mathrm{orb},0}-L_{0,\mathrm{mat}}}{L_{\mathrm{initial}}}\simeq15.6\%.}

The transfer therefore requires about one sixth of the parent’s angular momentum to be handed to the material. This is the amount by which the pear slows the parent’s spin (§A.4), before the centralising core is taken into account.

Sensitivity to the source of the material: in the base calculation, an equatorial surface source requires about 13.8%; a representative sample of a similar interior region, at half the original radius, requires about 16.5%. The source matters because material that starts far from the axis already carries more angular momentum, and so needs a smaller addition. A specific interior source requires its own mean squared distance; the word “interior” must not be taken to imply that the global mean necessarily applies to it.

The positive surplus shows that this test finds no angular-momentum deficit, but matching the rate of loss to the Sun requires a further calculation.

A.6.4 Sensitivity to period and expansion

A shorter initial period means more angular momentum. At the same time, a more expanded parent has a larger moment of inertia, and holds the same angular momentum at a longer day. The admissible initial period therefore depends on the adopted expansion.

At 5.54 hours the parent holds exactly today’s angular momentum of the system; at 4.90 hours it holds about 15% more — the adopted limit for loss to the outside. This ceiling must not be applied to the final period after the transfer of angular momentum, which is longer by design (§A.4). A.6.5 Comparison of equilibrium figures

The classical objection to fission can now be placed exactly. In the classical route the whole Earth must spin so fast that it becomes unstable and divides.

The sequence of shapes of a spinning fluid: a uniform spinning fluid passes through a known sequence of shapes as its angular momentum grows. At first it is a Maclaurin spheroid (§A.6.1) — flattened at the poles but round at the equator. At a definite flattening a second family branches off: the Jacobi ellipsoids, whose equator is no longer a circle but an ellipse, so the body becomes elongated like a rugby ball turning about a short axis. Further along the Jacobi sequence comes a pear-shaped figure, from which classical fission was expected to proceed. Each of these points requires a definite spin and a definite angular momentum.

The bifurcation point: at the classical bifurcation point from Maclaurin to Jacobi, with e≃0.81267e\simeq0.81267 and ω2/(πGρ)≃0.37423\omega^2/(\pi G\rho)\simeq0.37423, one obtains for the adopted mass and volume T≃2.73T\simeq2.73 hours, a≃7,802a\simeq7{,}802 km and an angular momentum of about 9.42×10349.42\times10^{34}, a ratio of about 2.74 to the present sum [1]. Here ee is the eccentricity of the meridian section (zero for a sphere, approaching one for a very flat body), and ω2/(πGρ)\omega^2/(\pi G\rho) measures the spin against the body’s own gravity. This means that merely to reach the first stage of the classical route, the Earth would have needed a day shorter than three hours and almost three times the angular momentum the Earth–Moon system holds.

Reference values in the literature: this is a comparison with a defined configuration on this classical route. The value 4.08 of the original reference calculation refers to a different point on the Maclaurin sequence — the maximum-Ω\Omega point, not the Jacobi bifurcation — and is not presented as a universal bound; it is the source of the familiar statement that fission needs about four times the available angular momentum. The value 3.5 for the pear-shaped point is not adopted, because verification of the table has not been completed.

The source of the difference: the heart of the difference is the place where the speed condition is evaluated. The classical route requires the parent’s surface to reach orbital speed (§A.1.2); this requirement pushes the parent towards break-up, and break-up costs several times the available angular momentum. The LEF route never uses that condition: the material is carried out in corotation, and the speed match is made at the corotation radius (§A.5), where a parent turning once every 5.5 hours already supplies orbital speed. When the requirement is evaluated there, it falls to the budget the parent actually holds. The comparison is between two routes to the same product: the classical route needs 2.74 times today’s budget; this route needs 1.007.

So far the material was assumed to leave in the equatorial plane. A.6.7The budgetary decision condition

The test requires consistency between the initial state, the final state and the external transfer:

𝐋i=𝐋f+Δ𝐋external.\mathbf{L}_i=\mathbf{L}_f+\Delta\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{external}}.

The equation is conservation of angular momentum written as an account: the angular momentum the system started with equals what it holds now, plus what was removed from it to the outside (for the Earth–Moon system, chiefly by the Sun’s tide). Summary of the budget: the parent with a 5.5-hour day holds slightly more angular momentum than today’s system; the transfer costs about one sixth of it; and the classical route would have needed 2.74 times as much.

A.7The Roche limit

A body that reaches the corotation radius must also remain whole there. Close to a massive parent, the parent’s gravity pulls harder on the near side of the body than on the far side; this difference, the tidal force, tends to stretch the body and pull it apart. The Roche limit is the distance inside which the tidal stretching exceeds the body’s own gravity, which holds it together. Inside this limit, a body held by its own gravity alone cannot remain whole.

A.7.1Two reference limits

For the adopted lunar density ρs=2,600kgm−3\rho_s=2{,}600\ \mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}} and the parent mass after departure, two Roche limits are obtained:

dfluid=2.44(3M⊕4πρs)1/3≃19,971km,d_{\mathrm{fluid}}=2.44\left(\frac{3M_\oplus}{4\pi\rho_s}\right)^{1/3} \simeq19{,}971\ \mathrm{km},

drigid=1.26(3M⊕4πρs)1/3≃10,314km.d_{\mathrm{rigid}}=1.26\left(\frac{3M_\oplus}{4\pi\rho_s}\right)^{1/3} \simeq10{,}314\ \mathrm{km}.

(Note: an earlier version gave 10,313 km; the two values are the same within rounding, and the series uses 10,314 km. In Figures 1, 6, 8 and 9 the fluid limit is printed as 19,972 km, the same value within rounding.) The bracketed term represents the hypothetical radius of a sphere of the Earth’s mass if it had the satellite’s mass density; the limit is a fixed multiple of it. The two formulas differ only in this multiple, and the reason is physical. The fluid approximation describes a body that can deform continuously: under the tidal field it lengthens toward the parent, its near and far ends move farther apart, the tidal difference across it grows, and it gives way sooner — so the fluid limit lies farther out. The so-called rigid approximation neglects the change of shape: the body keeps its round form, the tidal difference across it stays smaller, and it can survive closer to the parent. This approximation contains no crustal thickness or material strength [1]; it represents a body held together by its own gravity alone that merely does not change its shape. The base radius and the two coupled radii lie in the range between these two limits: inside the fluid limit and outside the rigid one. The presence of an outer crust alone therefore does not establish the body’s survival; survival depends on the body behaving as rigid over the time-scale of its stay in this region.

Sensitivity to density. The Roche limits move inward as the satellite density increases, because a denser body is held together more strongly by its own gravity. A density of about 4,700 kg per cubic metre would move the fluid limit inward to the release distance, about 16,400 km; for the rigid body at release the rigid limit applies (§B.7). This is a requirement computed in the Roche approximation, not evidence that the body reached that density, nor a solution to all the stability requirements.

A.7.2 What the comparison of accelerations teaches

The basic assessment of the satellite’s survival is made by a direct comparison between the parent’s gravitational acceleration gradient and the self-gravity acceleration at the satellite’s surface:

Δa≃2GM⊕Rsr3,gs=GmRs2.\Delta a\simeq\frac{2GM_\oplus R_s}{r^3},\qquad g_s=\frac{Gm}{R_s^2}.

The first quantity, Δa\Delta a, is the difference between the parent’s pull at the centre of the orbiting body and its pull at the body’s surface, which is closer by a distance RsR_s; this gradient falls steeply, as the cube of the distance rr. The second, gsg_s, is the body’s own surface gravity, which holds its material in. When the first is smaller than the second, the body’s own gravity dominates and the survival condition is met. Substituting the reference data of Appendix E at the initial corotation radius gives a ratio below unity (about 0.277 at 15,818 km), that is, the body’s own gravity clearly prevails. This ratio serves as a working measure of loading, but the change of shape and the rotation are not included in it. The static Roche limit provides a basis for comparison; the response in an eccentric orbit may also depend on the deformation history [9].

A.7.3What remains conditional

Reaching the corotation radius closes only the velocity gap; it is not a strength threshold and not a guarantee of survival. The properties of the body and of the connecting element (the pear) are examined in Paper 3, and the orbit after separation in Paper 4. Paper 2 adopts no binding residence time in the Roche domain, and no universal viscosity threshold. The survival assessment should refer to the closest distance to the parent in the chosen orbit, not to the release distance alone. Dividing the tests among the papers does not remove the requirement of survival.

The rigid limits, for the whole range of densities, lie well inside the Moon’s position; the fluid limits lie beyond it. The Moon at release is therefore safe as a rigid body, and its flattening and rounding were completed earlier, while it was still attached to the parent (§B.7; Paper 7).

A.8The direction of tidal evolution

The last step of Part A examines the direction of the Moon’s orbital motion immediately after separation: does the Moon move away from the Earth, or fall back onto the parent? The direction is set by the body’s position relative to the corotation line.

A.8.1 The direction of angular-momentum transfer

For a direct, circular, equatorial orbit, we compare the parent’s angular spin rate with the mean motion — the angular velocity of the orbiting body around the parent, defined by Kepler’s law:

n=GMr3.n=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}.

The orbiting body raises a tidal bulge on the parent, and two states are defined by the ratio of the two speeds. Orbital recession. When ω>n\omega>n, the parent turns faster than the body orbits, and its rotation carries the bulge ahead of the body; the bulge pulls the body forward, gives it angular momentum, and the body climbs outward while the parent’s spin slows. In the language of the tidal equations, the dissipative tidal contribution in the parent transfers angular momentum from its spin to the orbit. Orbital approach. When ω<n\omega<n, the body outruns the bulge, the bulge lags behind it and pulls it back, and the direction reverses: the body loses angular momentum and spirals inward. Exact synchronism. At exact circular synchronism the relevant contribution vanishes: the bulge stays directly beneath the body, and there is no forward or backward pull. Since nn falls with distance and ω\omega does not, ω>n\omega>n holds outside the corotation radius and ω<n\omega<n inside it. The corotation line is thus a tidal watershed: a body released just beyond it is pushed outward from the moment of release. Generalisation to an orbit with inclination and eccentricity requires the appropriate tidal equations [2,3].

Figure 7 illustrates the watershed in the present Solar System. Mars has two small moons on opposite sides of its synchronous radius: Phobos, inside, is drawn inward; Deimos, outside, moves away. The Earth–Moon case at release is the same rule, applied to a Moon placed just beyond the line.

Summary of the threshold conditions of Part A. With Part A complete, the problems are fully defined: a velocity gap that closes at the corotation radius; a pear that reaches that radius; a day at release set by the offset between two opposing processes — the lengthening pear, which slows the spin, and the sinking core, which speeds it up; an angular-momentum budget that must close against today’s system; a Roche test the body must pass; and a tidal watershed that decides the direction of its later motion. Part B answers all of them with one calculation.

B.1The calculation

Basic data. Initial Earth day T0=5.5T_0=5.5 h (§A.2); a homogeneous, thermally expanded parent, with a volumetric radius of 6,517 km and a mean density of 5,214 kg m−3^{-3} (equatorial radius 6,721 km, polar radius 6,128 km); the transferred body (the Moon): one lunar mass, m=7.35×1022m=7.35\times10^{22} kg, at a density of 2,600 kg m−3^{-3}. “Homogeneous” means that the parent is defined at the start of the process as uniform in density — a calculation input for a parent still at an early stage of sorting; “expanded” means that it is hot and therefore somewhat larger than a cold Earth of the same mass (§A.2). The equatorial and polar radii differ because a body completing one turn in 5.5 hours is hydrostatically flattened: it bulges at the equator and is compressed at the poles.

From Part A to the release state, step by step. The evolution of the system toward the release state follows from the processes of Part A in five successive steps, which balance slowing (the lengthening pear) against speeding up (iron sinking into the core). The table below collects them; its rows are read from top to bottom, and the tint shows the direction of each step: red for a step that slows the Earth and moves the corotation line outward, blue for a step that speeds it up and moves the line inward.

  1. The starting point. For a parent with a 5.5-hour day, the corotation line lies at 15,818 km (§A.2.1). The corotation line is the distance at which a body turning together with the Earth already moves at the speed of a free circular orbit.

  2. The lengthening pear slows the Earth. As the pear lengthens, material moves outward while still turning with the Earth, and it carries angular momentum with it. Angular momentum is the “quantity of rotation” of a body — its mass times its speed times its distance from the axis; with no external torque, its total is conserved. The angular momentum that leaves with the material is taken from the Earth’s spin, so the Earth turns more slowly: alone, this step would lengthen the day from 5.50 to 6.44 hours and move the line out to 17,569 km (§A.4).

  3. The centralising core speeds up the Earth. At the same time iron sinks toward the centre. This lowers the Earth’s moment of inertia — the measure of how far its mass lies from the axis — and, like a skater on the ice drawing in her arms, the Earth turns faster. With a core twice as dense as the mantle above it (D=2D=2), this step shortens the day by 0.67 hours.

  4. The net result. The two steps act together and partly offset each other: the day at release is 5.77 hours, and the corotation line lies at 16,331 km when the Moon is treated as a test particle, or at 16,399 km when the Moon’s own mass is included in the two-body formula below. We use the two-body value, rounded to about 16,400 km.

  5. Release. The Moon begins to separate when its centre of mass reaches this line. It is carried there by the rotation, not thrown: at that distance, the tangential speed it already has as part of the turning system is the speed of a free orbit, so no extra push is needed (§B.2).

B.2The velocity gap

The problem of §A.1 was that material released from the equatorial surface of an Earth with a 5.5-hour day moves at about 2,133 m s−1^{-1}, far below the 7,701 m s−1^{-1} of a circular orbit — a gap of about 5,568 m s−1^{-1}. The gap closes with distance, because the tangential speed given by the rotation grows linearly with distance, while the orbital speed falls. On the corotation line the rotation gives the Moon exactly the orbital speed, v=Ωr=G(M⊕+m)r≈4,960ms−1,v=\Omega r=\sqrt{\frac{G(M_\oplus+m)}{r}}\approx4{,}960\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}, so no gap remains (§A.1, §A.5). The left side is the speed of a point turning with the Earth at distance rr; the right side is the speed of a free circular orbit at the same distance; on the line the two are equal. The value is a little below the 5,020 m s−1^{-1} of the 5.5-hour reference state (§A.2.1), because the Earth, turning slightly more slowly, places the line farther out, where orbital speeds are lower. The mass is released into an independent orbit by virtue of the speed it already has from the rotation, with no need for an external push. The everyday picture is a child on a roundabout: the farther from the centre she sits, the faster she moves, without anyone pushing her.

B.3The pear

The pear bridges a gap of about 7,800 km and lies entirely inside the corotation line; the Moon is released when its centre of mass reaches the line (§A.3; the release mechanism is detailed in Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice). Because the pear lies inside the line, the net force in the turning frame is directed inward, toward the Earth (§B.5): the pear’s material stays bound to the parent, while the Moon, whose centre of mass is on the line, is free to separate. This is why the Moon leaves as a whole unit, and the connecting element does not go with it.

B.4The offset

The calculation solves the two opposing processes together and fixes the Earth day at 5.77 hours and the corotation line at 16,331 km (§A.4). The 16,331 km is the test-particle value of step 4 in §B.1; including the Moon’s own mass in the two-body solution gives 16,399 km, about 16,400 km, used for the release point. A calculation based on the lengthening pear alone would give 6.44 hours and 17,569 km, and one based on the centralising core alone would give too fast an Earth; only the two processes together describe the Earth of that time.

B.5 Corotation

The equation of net acceleration. The Moon’s centre of mass reaches the corotation line at about 16,400 km; beyond it corotation pulls the Moon outward, and it is released: anet=Ω2r−GM⊕r2.a_{\mathrm{net}}=\Omega^{2}r-\frac{GM_\oplus}{r^{2}}. This is the net acceleration felt by material that turns with the Earth at distance rr, as seen in the turning frame. The first term is the outward (centrifugal) acceleration of the rotation, which grows with distance; the second is the Earth’s gravity, which falls with distance. This equation fixes the direction of the forces in the system: inside the corotation line the Earth’s gravity dominates and aneta_{\mathrm{net}} points inward, which keeps the pear joined to the Earth; on the line the two balance; beyond the line the centrifugal acceleration prevails and the material is drawn outward. As soon as the Moon’s centre of mass crosses the point of balance, the body as a whole is subject to an outward pull that carries it away from the Earth, and it separates.

The angular-momentum budget. The total angular momentum of the system stands at 1.007 times today’s value. At release, the spin angular momentum remaining to the Earth requires a moment-of-inertia coefficient of k=0.355k=0.355; a two-layer structure with q=0.55q=0.55 and D=2D=2 gives k=25(1−q5)+Dq5(1−q3)+Dq3=0.360.k=\frac{2}{5}\,\frac{(1-q^{5})+Dq^{5}}{(1-q^{3})+Dq^{3}}=0.360. The coefficient kk describes how the mass is distributed: 0.4 for a uniform sphere, and lower when the mass is concentrated toward the centre (today’s Earth has about 0.33). The value 0.355 is what is required of the Earth at release if a spin of 5.77 hours is to carry the remaining 2.88×10342.88\times10^{34}.

Fit to the two-layer model. The formula computes kk for an Earth of two layers: an inner layer reaching to a fraction qq of the radius, with a density DD times that of the outer layer. With q=0.55q=0.55 and D=2D=2 it gives 0.360, close to the required 0.355. The structural value produced by the centralisation and sinking of iron thus agrees with the angular momentum the release state demands.

No high budget is needed. The route closes its budget within what the Earth–Moon system holds today, plus the roughly 1% that solar tides have removed since then. There is thus no need for a giant impact from outside, or for a later mechanism to remove surplus angular momentum, which characterise the high-angular-momentum impact models that start at about 2–3 times today’s value.

B.7The Roche limit

The Roche limit is the distance inside which the tidal pull of a large body — the difference between its gravity on the near side and on the far side of a smaller body — exceeds the smaller body’s own gravity and can pull it apart. Two classical forms bound it: drigid=1.26R⊕(ρ⊕ρ)1/3,dfluid=2.44R⊕(ρ⊕ρ)1/3.d_{\mathrm{rigid}}=1.26\,R_\oplus\left(\frac{\rho_\oplus}{\rho}\right)^{1/3},\qquad d_{\mathrm{fluid}}=2.44\,R_\oplus\left(\frac{\rho_\oplus}{\rho}\right)^{1/3}. The rigid form applies to a body that keeps its shape; the fluid form to a body that deforms freely and is therefore stretched further by the tide. In both, ρ\rho is the density of the small body: the denser it is, the closer it can come.

Position of the release point. The Moon’s centre of mass at release lies at 16,400 km, well outside the rigid limit (9,484–12,994 km for 1,300–3,344 kg m−3^{-3}), but inside the fluid limit for a body of 2,600 kg m−3^{-3} (19,971 km, §A.7.1). Which of the two limits applies is therefore decided by the mechanical behaviour of the Moon at release: whether it is rigid.

Mechanical state at release. The rounding was completed before release; the body is rigid and round. The flattening and the later rounding of the lunar body took place earlier, while it was still attached to the parent (Paper 7, The Poles); what leaves the line is a body with a rigid outer shell.

Restraint of tidal deformation. The rigidity of the body reduces the tidal response by the factor 11+19μ2ρgR,\frac{1}{1+\dfrac{19\mu}{2\rho gR}}, so that for μ≥10\mu\ge10 GPa the axis ratio is at most 1.04. The equation links the rigidity (shear modulus) of the body, μ\mu, its surface gravity, gg, and its radius, RR; ρgR\rho gR is the pressure of the body’s own weight. The factor says that a body whose rigidity is large compared with the pressure of its own weight deforms only a small fraction of what a fluid body would. An axis ratio of at most 1.04 means that the longest axis of the Moon exceeds the shortest by no more than 4%: the body remains round.

B.8The direction of tidal evolution

The mechanism of recession. The Moon is released on the corotation line, and the tide transfers angular momentum from the Earth’s spin to the Moon’s orbit: it recedes (§A.8; the orbit is the subject of Paper 4, The Orbit). Exactly on the line the tidal effect is neutral, because the Earth turns once in the same time as the Moon completes an orbit. Separation itself takes place when the Moon’s centre of mass has moved about 210 km beyond the line, a stage at which a little more than half of the Moon’s volume has crossed it (the margin Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0 of §D.3). In this range the Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits; the tidal bulge raised on the Earth runs ahead of the Moon, pulls it forward and moves it outward in its orbit. This is the side of the watershed on which Deimos lies today (Figure 7).

How far beyond the line. The distance beyond the line is small compared with the release radius, but it sets the shape of the first orbit. On the line the Moon carries exactly the speed of a circular orbit; beyond it, a little more, so the release point becomes the nearest point of a slightly stretched orbit, with an eccentricity e0e_0 that grows with the distance. D.2The novelty of the work

The physical foundations of the model — the corotation formula, Maclaurin figures, conservation of angular momentum and the Roche limits — are existing physics. The novelty lies in their joint application to a connected-transfer condition, with a consistent computation of the change in mass, shape and spin, and with a presentation of the sensitivity domains. “Connected transfer” means that the material stays joined to the Earth and turns with it until it reaches the line, instead of being launched from the surface. Replacing the surface release condition with this condition is what makes the classical objection to fission — an angular-momentum budget several times larger than required — not arise (§A.6.5). A full claim of historical priority requires the prior-work check of Appendix F; the success of the numerical reproduction does not prove priority.

PART E

Conclusions

A body of the Moon’s mass can reach orbit from the Earth’s own material, without a giant impact from outside. The fast spin of the parent supplies the tangential speed; the mass bridge (the pear) connects the Earth to the corotation line; the lengthening pear and the sinking of iron into the core together set a day of 5.77 hours at release; the Moon, as a rigid and round body, is released when its centre of mass reaches the corotation line at a radius of about 16,400 km; the total angular-momentum budget closes at 1.007 times today’s budget, consistent with the loss to solar tides; and the Moon recedes from the moment of release.

The process comes together as a single chain: the Earth’s rotation carries the material outward; the line at which the carried tangential speed equals the orbital speed sets where the Moon separates; the balance between the pear and the core sets where that line lies; and the parent’s initial angular-momentum reservoir suffices, with a surplus of about 1% that the solar tide will remove later.

The calculations in this paper fix the inputs for further work that other research groups are equipped to undertake. Each item names a calculation this paper does not perform, and the paper of the series that will use its result.

  1. The stratified Maclaurin problem. The solution of the coupled end state (§A.6.2) uses a uniform figure combined with a varying moment-of-inertia coefficient. The Clairaut–Darwin–Radau method is the standard way of computing the shape of a rotating body whose density increases inward; it would replace the uniform shape used here with one that follows the sinking iron.

  2. The dissipation of the early Earth. The recession from about 2.6 Earth radii (16,400 km; 2.8 Earth radii in the earlier pear-alone state, with a 6.44-hour day) is set by k2/Qk_2/Q and by its history; the present value is anomalous. Here k2k_2 measures how much the Earth deforms under the tide, and QQ how much of the tidal energy it dissipates; their ratio sets the rate at which the Moon recedes. Ocean-resonance models run from the 5.77-hour day at release (rather than from the 6.4-hour day of the earlier, pear-alone state) would give the rate function that Paper 4 requires.

  3. The thermal expansion of the parent. The 120 km adopted here (98–520 admissible) is a first estimate from magma-ocean depth and melt expansion; a full equation-of-state integration for a hot, volatile-rich early Earth would fix it, and with it the rotation window.

  4. The onset of recession. A body released exactly on the corotation line is in a tidally neutral state: the Earth turns once in the time the body completes one orbit, and the tide neither pushes it out nor pulls it in. As soon as the body lies beyond the line, the tide pushes it outward, so the Moon recedes from the start (§B.8; Paper 4, §B.2). Separation leaves the centre of mass beyond the line by the release margin Δr≃2d0\Delta r\simeq2d_0, about 210 km (§D.3, §E.1). A tidal integration with a frequency-dependent rheology in both bodies, starting from the release state of Paper 3, would fix the range of the margin and the rate of the first recession. “Frequency-dependent rheology” means that the deformation response of each body to the tide depends on how fast the tidal field changes.

  5. Survival inside the fluid Roche limit. The order-of-magnitude strain estimate of the appendix “Estimate of acceleration and strain” is not a solution of the deformation field. A full visco-elastic calculation of the rigid body of 1,890 km, staying at about 16,400 km from the Earth over 10110^1–10310^3 years, would replace it. A visco-elastic calculation treats the body as elastic over short times and slowly flowing over long ones, which is how a hot body with a rigid outer shell behaves.

  6. The classical route. The factor 2.74 at the Jacobi bifurcation is computed; the pear-shaped point along the Jacobi sequence requires the tabulated values to be verified, and a modern computation of the sequence for the adopted mass and density would settle it. The Jacobi sequence is the family of elongated, three-axis equilibrium shapes into which a fast-spinning fluid body passes beyond the Maclaurin shapes (§A.6.5).

The short full derivation is in §B.1. Distinguishing the mass definitions. The right side, ℳ(e)\mathcal M(e), is the flattening that this balance requires, written through the eccentricity ee of the meridian section — e=0e=0 is a sphere, and ee grows as the body flattens. Given the spin and the density, the equation is solved for ee.

a=RV(1−e2)1/6,c=RV(1−e2)1/3.a=\frac{R_V}{(1-e^2)^{1/6}}, \qquad c=R_V(1-e^2)^{1/3}.

The two expressions keep the volume fixed: a2c=RV3a^2c=R_V^3, so the body flattens without gaining or losing material.

For a given spin below the maximum, the Maclaurin equation has two solutions: one slightly flattened and one strongly flattened. A body spun up gradually from a sphere follows the first. On the branch near the spherical shape one obtains e0≃0.4107e_0\simeq0.4107, a0≃6,721a_0\simeq6{,}721 km and c0≃6,128c_0\simeq6{,}128 km — an equatorial bulge in which the equator lies about 600 km further from the centre than the poles.

Uniform density is a calculation input. It represents a parent still at an early stage of sorting: iron may already have gathered in the core at the start, and the organisation of the core continued through the transfer and after release. APPENDIX C

The differentiation calculation and the sensitivity tests

This appendix describes how the two-layer calculation underlying §A.4 and §A.4.1 was set up, and how far its numbers can be trusted. It supports the table of final periods and corotation radii against the density ratio DD (for D=2D=2: 5.77 hours and 16,331 km), and the value k=0.360k=0.360 of §B.6. The physical idea is the skater drawing in her arms: when iron sinks toward the centre, the moment of inertia falls, and with the angular momentum conserved, the spin speeds up.

Characterising the two-layer model. The body is divided into two layers: an inner one and an outer one. qq is the ratio of the volumetric radius of the inner layer to that of the outer, and DD is the ratio of their densities. The layers are incompressible and in shared rotation, so they turn together as one body. The volume of the final body is fixed in all scenarios; the initial state used to set L0L_0 remains homogeneous, so every scenario starts from the same budget and differs only in how the final body has arranged its mass.

Solving the equipotential surfaces. For each scenario a constant effective potential is solved on both boundaries: each boundary is required to be a surface of equal combined gravitational and centrifugal potential, which is the condition for a fluid layer to be at rest in the rotating frame. II is then computed directly from the density and the shape, not taken from a formula for a uniform body. There is no differentiation path in time and no equation of state here: the calculation compares end states, and does not follow the sinking of iron step by step.

The reliability of the results is examined by two convergence checks. Freeing the shape of the surfaces: allowing the layer boundaries to depart from an exact spheroidal shape (instead of forcing that shape on them) changes the radius by up to about 2.5 km in the q=0.55q=0.55 scan — a small deviation against a corotation radius of more than 15,000 km. Numerical resolution: changing the numerical step size changes the radii by only a few metres. The field residuals do not converge monotonically in every check, so the model makes no claim of metre-level physical precision. The results are reliable to the kilometre level, which is the working scale of the paper.

The main calculation releases the Moon in the equatorial plane. The point is geometric: off the equator, the orbital angular-momentum vector of the Moon and the spin angular-momentum vector of the Earth do not coincide on a single axis, and angular momenta that point in different directions add as arrows in space, not as plain numbers.

In a frame in which zz is defined as the final spin axis, and λ\lambda is the latitude of the release point, measured relative to its equator, the total angular-momentum vector is defined as:

𝐋tot=(−Hsinλ,0,S+Hcosλ),H=μredωr2cosλ.\mathbf{L}_{\mathrm{tot}}=(-H\sin\lambda,0,S+H\cos\lambda),\quad H=\mu_{\mathrm{red}}\omega r^2\cos\lambda.

APPENDIX E

Roche calculations and the survival assumptions

This appendix gathers the background to §A.7 and §B.7: two Roche limits and a first estimate of how strongly the Earth’s tide loads the young Moon. The Roche limit is the distance inside which the difference between the Earth’s pull on the near and far sides of a satellite (the tidal stretch) exceeds the self-gravity holding it together. A fluid body that deforms freely develops an elongation that increases the stretch on itself, so its limit lies further out (coefficient 2.44); a body that keeps its shape has a limit further in (coefficient 1.26). The question this appendix answers is how large the tidal load is at the release distance, and what more is needed to turn that load into a survival result.

The coefficients 1.26 and 2.44 and the definitions of the limits were kept from the previous text. A focused verification of the source for the so-called rigid coefficient and of its domain of validity is defined as an open requirement to be completed before submission. The coefficient contains no strength law for a crust: the rigid limit describes a body that does not change shape, without saying what material strength makes this possible.

Estimate of acceleration and strain — a previous calculation text preserved

The estimate compares two accelerations. Δa\Delta a is the tidal stretch: the difference between the Earth’s pull at the centre of the body and its pull at the body’s surface, along the line to the Earth. gsg_s is the self-gravity acceleration at the body’s surface. For a body radius Rs=1,890R_s=1{,}890 km:

Δa≃2GM⊕Rsr3,gs=GmRs2.\Delta a\simeq\frac{2GM_\oplus R_s}{r^3}, \qquad g_s=\frac{Gm}{R_s^2}.

The stretch falls with the cube of the distance rr: halving the distance raises it eightfold. At a radius of 15,818 km one obtains Δa≃0.381\Delta a\simeq0.381, gs≃1.373g_s\simeq1.373 m/s² and a ratio of about 0.277. The ratio compares the tidal stretch with self-gravity: when it is below one, self-gravity wins, and at this distance the tidal load is a little over a quarter of the body’s own gravity. This condition is necessary, but does not settle the question for a deforming body; the deformation of a hot body over the years it spends inside the fluid limit is the survival condition detailed in §D.3.

The next step converts the acceleration into a stress and the stress into a time. The stress estimate and the time for the deformation to develop are defined by:

σtidal∼ρsΔaRs∼1.9×109Pa,tstrain∼ε*ησtidal.\sigma_{\mathrm{tidal}}\sim\rho_s\Delta aR_s\sim1.9\times10^9\ \mathrm{Pa}, \qquad t_{\mathrm{strain}}\sim\frac{\varepsilon_*\eta}{\sigma_{\mathrm{tidal}}}.

The first expression represents the weight-like load of a column of the body’s material, of density ρs\rho_s and height RsR_s, under the acceleration Δa\Delta a. The second describes how long a material of viscosity η\eta (its resistance to flow, in pascal-seconds) takes to reach a strain ε*\varepsilon_* — a fractional change of shape — under this stress: the stiffer the material, the longer the process.

Substituting ε*=0.5\varepsilon_*=0.5 (a change of shape by half) and η=1019Pas\eta=10^{19}\ \mathrm{Pa\,s} gives about 85 years (with ρs=2,600\rho_s=2{,}600 kg/m3^3, Δa≃0.381\Delta a\simeq0.381 m/s2^2 and Rs=1,890R_s=1{,}890 km the stress is 1.87×1091.87\times10^9 Pa; an earlier version of this estimate gave 1.4×1091.4\times10^9 Pa and about 116 years, a value corresponding to a body density of about 1,950 kg/m3^3). This is an estimate of strain under a constant representative stress, not a calculation of a disruption time. The rheology (how the material responds to load over time), the shape, the varying stress and the actual residence time must be examined.

This estimate is kept as a parametric dependence: it shows how the time changes with viscosity and with stress, and lets the reader insert other values. The residence time itself is derived from the orbital calculation and is not adopted here. The card to accompany every calculation

The table above becomes a binding working rule: a short card that every calculation in the series states before its result, so that the reader sees at once which state it describes, and so that data are not borrowed from the present or from another stage.

Before an equation or a table of results, state: (1) stage and time — the stage of the scenario (A, B or C) and the time t; (2) point of measurement — external, at the surface, internal or in the connection region; (3) frame and axis — inertial or rotating, and the position of the spin axis; (4) scenario version — for example a homogeneous or layered profile; (5) masses and radii valid at that stage; (6) heat and material inputs — thermal parameters, melt fractions and viscosity; (7) effects neglected — for example the solar perturbation; (8) result and sensitivity. If a datum is missing or not yet determined, the calculation is marked explicitly as conditional or deferred — a value must not be borrowed from the present or from another stage without justification.

The mass bookkeeping is written as one equation. Mass-management equation: Mtotal=ME+mbody+Mtransit+MescapedM_{\mathrm{total}}=M_E+m_{\mathrm{body}}+M_{\mathrm{transit}}+M_{\mathrm{escaped}}, according to the boundaries of the system and the reservoirs: the Earth, the developing body, the material on its way between them (in the pear), and any material lost from the system. If a closed system without loss is chosen, or the material of the connection is assigned wholly to one of the two bodies, this must be stated explicitly in the card and the terms of the equation reduced accordingly. The mass entering GM must match the reservoir definition in the same calculation.

The combined gravitational field must be derived from the actual mass distribution, at the stated moment and place. A two-point-mass approximation is permitted only in the domain where it was defined and at the precision defined for it; it is not a full description of the Earth’s interior, of the connection region or of a local anomaly. In a decomposition into a background component and a local anomaly component (such as Lobe A), only the difference mass is added, so that no mass is counted twice.
