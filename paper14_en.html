14. Research topics

What the model opens for other researchers

Zohar Duran · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 8 November 2026

Abstract

• The mechanism discussed in the series: The LEF model (Lunar Elevation and Fission) proposes that the Moon was built from the Earth’s own material while it was still attached to it. A hot, fast-spinning Earth raised a pear-shaped extension of its own material; deep, volatile-rich melt was fed up through it into a body on its tip; the Earth’s outer shell over the region rose with that body as the surface of a dome and became the Moon’s crust; and the body was released when its centre of mass reached the corotation line (the distance at which a body turning synchronously with the Earth moves at free orbital speed). Papers 1–13 of the series set out this process, its orbit, the maria, the craters, the poles, the South Pole–Aitken basin, the stripes of the Pacific floor, the moons of Mars and the requirements the Moon must meet.

• The role of Paper 14: This companion paper performs no new calculations. It collects, from all 12 previous papers, every topic that has been left open: requirements not yet tested, studies defined but not yet run, calculations marked “to be computed”, and rows that the summary table of Paper 1 marks as partial or awaiting. To these are added 15 questions from the working notes of the series (9 of them the author’s own proposals that the papers have not yet taken up). Recurring items have been merged into a single topic, with all sources kept.

• Structure of the list: The paper gathers 132 research topics, grouped by theme in 11 parts (A–K). Part L gives three lists for choosing: the two key observations of the model, the topics that can be done at once on public data, and the topics that need a large simulation, a space mission or laboratory samples.

What this paper proposes (and its limits)

• Distributed calibration of the model: The model is fully stated; its calibration requires broad computational and observational tools (three-dimensional simulations, equations of state at 50−150 GPa50 - 150\text{ GPa}, coupled thermal–orbital integrations, laboratory work, space missions and samples). Each requirement of Paper 13 is defined as a topic for a paper of its own, with its own test, the deciding observation and the expected numerical value.

• Kinds of topics and the key observations:

• Calibration topics: sharpening parameters that the series gives as a range (the expansion of the hot parent, the time of failure of the contact, and the dissipation history of the Earth).

• Topics of partial confirmation: extending predictions already partly supported (the order of sealing of the maria, the young craters of the poles, and the LLVP province beneath the Pacific).

• The two key observations of the model:

1. The geometry of the exposure zones (topics 121–122): whether the crust and the crater record are ordered in three concentric zones about the centre of the near side.

2. The source of the craters (topics 49 and 123): whether the material that made the craters is returned terrestrial material (as the model requires) or foreign material from outside the Earth–Moon system.

• What this paper does not do: The paper recomputes no results, changes no numerical value of the series and weakens no claim; every number quoted is given with a reference to the original paper and section. Two subjects named in the series as future work — the Earth–Moon axes system and the Moon’s magnetism — are noted at the end of the paper and are not counted among the 132 topics.

Introduction — how to use this paper

1. Definition of a research topic and its five sources

A research topic is defined as one open question of the model that merits a paper of its own, and it is drawn from one of five sources:

1. A requirement that has been stated but not yet tested (the requirements R1–R18 of Paper 13).

2. A study defined in a paper with thresholds fixed in advance, but not yet run.

3. A calculation marked in the papers as still to be made.

4. A row of the summary table of Paper 1 (§D.1), which marks each claim as shown, partial or awaiting (in traffic-light colours: green, orange and red).

5. A question from the working notes of the series, with the model’s expected answer, that no paper has yet taken up (15 topics at the end of Parts B, D, E, G, I and K).

2. How each topic is presented

Each topic opens with a short paragraph that explains what the question is about and why it matters to the model, followed by five fixed tagged lines:

• The question: the problem stated in one sentence.

• What the model predicts: the numerical value or the direction the series expects, with a reference to the relevant paper.

• Data: the data sets that exist or are needed.

• Method: the computational, observational or laboratory tool required.

• Where in the series: an exact reference to the original paper and section.

PART A

The Earth before and during the pear

Part A covers the parent body: the early Earth before the Moon separated from it. In the model the parent was hot, rich in volatiles, completed one rotation in about 5.5 hours, and was in the midst of differentiating and separating its iron toward the centre. From one region of it (near the island of Kiritimati in the central Pacific), a pear-shaped extension of the Earth’s own material rose toward the corotation line, and the Moon was built on its tip. Topics 1–11 set the dynamical and thermodynamic starting conditions of the system (ten of them need new modelling and one needs new laboratory data).

1The thermal expansion of the hot parent

A hot body saturated with dissolved volatiles is larger in volume than a cold one; at a given mass, the radius of the young Earth sets the window of rotation periods in which the transfer mechanism can work. Paper 2 adopts an expansion of 120 km and shows that the model works over a range of values; a full calculation would narrow that range.

• The question: How much larger in radius was the hot, volatile-rich early Earth than a cold one?

• What the model predicts: An expansion of 120 km is adopted (admissible range 98 - 520 km), which sets a window of rotation periods for the transfer of 4.90 - 5.54 hours at ΔR = 120 km (Paper 2).

• Data: Estimates of the depth of the early magma ocean; thermal expansion coefficients of silicate melts.

• Method: An integration of an equation of state (which relates pressure, temperature and density) from the centre outward for a hot, volatile-rich Earth.

• Where in the series: Paper 2, Further work item 3; §D.3.

2The stratified Maclaurin problem

A Maclaurin spheroid describes a uniform fluid body in steady rotation, whereas the early Earth was undergoing iron descent that made its interior denser than its mantle. The position of the corotation line (the Moon’s starting orbit) depends on the distribution of density inside.

• The question: Where does the corotation line lie for a parent whose iron is sinking, computed self-consistently?

• What the model predicts: A range of 15,382 - 17,569 km from D = 3 to D = 1 (where D is the ratio of the density of the inner layer to that of the outer layer; D = 1 is a uniform body). At D = 2 the line lies at 16,300 - 16,400 km, which is the starting orbit of Paper 4 (Paper 2).

• Data: The parent parameters of the series: volumetric radius 6,517 km, rotation period 5.5 hours, and moment-of-inertia factor k ≳ 0.397 (where k = 0.4 for a uniform sphere and less for a body with a dense core).

• Method: The Clairaut–Darwin–Radau theory of the figure of a rotating body, applied to a body with active internal differentiation.

• Where in the series: Paper 2, Further work item 1; §A.4.1, §A.6.2; Paper 1 §F.2.

3Core formation during the transfer, and the Hf–W reservoir history

The short-lived radioactive isotope ¹⁸²Hf (a lithophile that stays in the silicate) decays to ¹⁸²W (a siderophile that follows iron into the core), so an excess of ¹⁸²W in a reservoir records when its metal separated. The model requires that the Earth’s core was still forming during the transfer and that one chronology carry the tungsten signatures through the whole window of construction.

• The question: Was the Earth’s iron still descending during the transfer (centralisation D ≈ 2), and can one chronology carry the tungsten signatures through a 600-Myr window?

• What the model predicts: An essentially uniform parent at the start (k ≥ 0.397); the centralisation of the core during the transfer shortens the day at release from 6.44 to 5.77 hours (Paper 2), and the Hf–W excess was set early in the window and carried without reset (Paper 13).

• Data: ¹⁸²W anomalies in terrestrial and lunar samples; Hf/W ratios.

• Method: A reservoir model of metal–silicate separation over a 600-Myr window under partial equilibration of metal and silicate.

• Where in the series: Paper 2, requirements box of Part D, §D.3; Paper 13 §D.2; Paper 1 §F.2 and §D.1 (the orange row of Paper 2).

4The equation of state of the deep, volatile-rich melt at 50−150 GPa50 - 150\text{ GPa}

The pressure test of Paper 3 asks whether the deep source could push its melt up through the pear and into the body on its tip; the answer depends on the density of a melt rich in water and carbon dioxide at the pressures of the deep mantle (50−150 GPa50 - 150\text{ GPa}), since a lighter melt needs less pressure to be lifted.

• The question: Is a hydrous or carbonated silicate melt lighter than about 3,864 kg/m33,864\text{ kg/m}^3 along the ascent?

• What the model predicts: The pressure test passes below 3,864 kg/m33,864\text{ kg/m}^3; at 3,500 kg/m33,500\text{ kg/m}^3 a margin of 10.49 GPa10.49\text{ GPa} is left, while at 4,000 kg/m34,000\text{ kg/m}^3 it does not pass (Paper 3).

• Data: Shock-compression and diamond-anvil-cell data on hydrous and carbonated melts; first-principles molecular dynamics.

• Method: Experiments and ab-initio molecular dynamics (quantum simulations at the atomic scale) at the relevant compositions, giving the density threshold as a function of volatile content.

• Where in the series: Paper 3, Further work item 3; §A.9–A.10; §D.1 row 1; Paper 1 §F.2 and §E.1; Paper 13 §D.3 (row R6).

5The energy of the lift and the energy release of the parent

Lifting a lunar mass and a pear several times heavier out of the Earth’s gravity takes a great deal of energy, which in the model is supplied by the descent of iron to the core, releasing gravitational energy. The topic asks whether the supply of energy matches the cost and at what rate it was delivered.

• The question: Does the evolving Earth release the work needed to lift the body and the pear, and at what rate?

• What the model predicts: Lifting the Moon costs of order 1030 J10^{30}\text{ J} and lifting the pear about 8×1030 J8 \times 10^{30}\text{ J} (5.4 times the Moon’s), while the descent of the iron frees of order 1031 J10^{31}\text{ J} (Paper 3).

• Data: Parameters of core formation and of cooling under a steam atmosphere.

• Method: A thermal-evolution model of an Earth whose iron is descending, contracting and cooling, with the duration of the feed derived from its output.

• Where in the series: Paper 3, Further work item 4; §A.12; §C.9; §D.1 row 3; Paper 1 §F.2.

6The pressure test, repeated with the pear’s mass and the flattened parent

A parent completing one rotation in 5.5 hours5.5\text{ hours} is flattened (characterised by the gravity coefficient J2J_{2}, which describes the equatorial bulge), so its gravity field differs from that of a sphere. Paper 3 estimated the effect of the flattening; what remains is to complete the pressure test with the pear’s own mass included.

• The question: Does the source pressure still lift and hold the body and the pear in the field of a flattened parent?

• What the model predicts: With J2≃0.036J_2 \simeq 0.036 the inward pull rises by 3%3\% (5.4%5.4\% at the equatorial surface), and the cost of the lift rises by about 4 GPa4\text{ GPa} (within the margin of 10.49 GPa10.49\text{ GPa}); the test has still to be rerun with the pear’s own mass (Paper 3).

• Data: The geometry and mass of the pear (Paper 3 §C.7).

• Method: Running the joint test of Paper 3 §A.9–A.13 in a non-spherical gravity field.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §C.9; §A.13.

7The construction rate: feed flux over the window

The flow of a viscous melt through a passage grows as the fourth power of the passage radius (b4b^{4}), so a broad passage is required to deliver the lunar mass in the permitted time without freezing and closing.

• The question: Does the net feed deliver the internal mass in the time available (about 400+100+100 Myr400 + 100 + 100\text{ Myr}, with 100 Myr100\text{ Myr} as a sensitivity case)?

• What the model predicts: The flux is sensitive to aperture as b4b^{4}; a passage of about 1,000 km1,000\text{ km} radius is required (0.460.46 lunar masses; a lateral load of order 1022 N10^{22}\text{ N}) (Paper 3).

• Data: Viscosities of the melt; radii of the passage.

• Method: A model of pipe flow and freezing over the permitted ranges of viscosity and aperture.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §D.1 row 2; §A.11; §A.13; Paper 1 §E.1.

8The pear’s self-holding equilibrium figure and its budget of angular momentum (R9)

The pear is the Earth’s own material standing out toward the corotation line and holding itself up as a dynamic equilibrium figure (requirement R9 of Paper 13). A detailed estimate would replace the present approximation based on round cross-sections of uniform density and would close the budget of angular momentum.

• The question: Does a rotating, internally pressurised Earth admit an equilibrium figure with a body at its tip — a base of about 9,000 km9,000\text{ km} narrowing in one arc to a contact of 3,000 km3,000\text{ km}, wholly inside the corotation line — and does that figure close the angular-momentum budget?

• What the model predicts: The narrowest self-holding base grows from 7,8007,800 to 8,800 km8,800\text{ km} (at 3,000 kg/m33,000\text{ kg/m}^3) between the start and release; the pear’s mass is 8.8−12.18.8 - 12.1 lunar masses for bases of 9,000−11,000 km9,000 - 11,000\text{ km}; the pear passes to the Moon about a sixth of the system’s angular momentum, which is taken from the Earth’s spin and offset by the centralising core (Paper 3).

• Data: The parent state of the series (no observations are needed).

• Method: A calculation of an equilibrium figure with a free surface, including the pear’s own gravity along its axis and the internal flow.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §C.7, §C.8, §C.9; Paper 13 §C.6 (R9); Paper 1 §E.1 (R9, awaiting); Paper 2 §A.4.

9The northward lean of the pear: oblique feed from a source north of the equator

The base of the pear lay near the equator (1.9∘N1.9^{\circ }\text{N}), whereas the Moon was released from latitude 22.5∘N22.5^{\circ }\text{N}. Since rotation pushes a raised mass toward the equator and not away from it, the model attributes the northward lean to the oblique feed path from a deep source lying north of the equator.

• The question: Can a deep source north of the equator feed obliquely enough to take the tip from a base near 1.9∘N1.9^{\circ }\text{N} to about 22.5∘N22.5^{\circ }\text{N}, against the equatorward push of the rotation?

• What the model predicts: The lean comes from the feed path, not from the rotation (Paper 3).

• Data: The position of the base (Kiritimati) and of the low-velocity province (LLVP) beneath the Pacific, read with reservation as the returned pile.

• Method: A geometry of the deep source and of the feed path in a rotating, flattened parent.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §D.1; §A.4; §B.1.

10The Earth’s core during the pear

A pear of about nine lunar masses on one side of the Earth moves the Earth’s centre of mass toward it and draws the dense core after it; when the pear fell back, the core returned to its place. The topic asks whether this episode left a signature in the east–west asymmetry of the solid inner core.

• The question: Was the Earth’s core drawn 270−370 km270 - 370\text{ km} toward the pear while the pear stood, and did it return after the pear fell?

• What the model predicts: A displacement of 270 km270\text{ km} at the start (base 9,000 km9,000\text{ km}) growing to 370 km370\text{ km} at release (base 11,000 km11,000\text{ km}), and a return after compensation; a hemispherical structure of the inner core aligned with the pear’s side is the record to look for (Paper 3).

• Data: The east–west asymmetry of the inner core in seismic travel times; the age of the inner core (the test weakens if the inner core is much younger than the Moon).

• Method: A three-dimensional hydrostatic model of the Earth with the pear standing and after its fall, following the deficit left in the source.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §E.5; Further work item 7.

11The classical fission route, recomputed

The classical fission model (George Darwin onward) was rejected because it required far more angular momentum than the Earth–Moon system has, whereas the connected transfer of the LEF model needs almost exactly today’s angular momentum (Paper 2). A modern computation would make the comparison exact.

• The question: What does a modern computation of the Jacobi sequence and its pear-shaped branch give for this mass and density?

• What the model predicts: The classical route needs 2.742.74 times today’s angular momentum at the Jacobi bifurcation point, against 1.0071.007 for the connected transfer (Paper 2).

• Data: Tabulated Jacobi and Poincaré sequences.

• Method: A numerical sequence of rotating fluid equilibria, with the pear-shaped point verified.

• Where in the series: Paper 2, Further work item 6; §A.6.5.

13A stiff shell over a mobile base: the coupled thermal–rheological solution

The model requires at once a stiff outer shell that keeps the far-side craters during the ascent, and a soft interface at its base that lets it ride up on the lengthening dome.

• The question: Is there one rheological range in which the outer shell stays stiff enough to keep its craters while the interface beneath it stays mobile enough to ride up on the dome?

• What the model predicts: A shell about 18 km18\text{ km} thick with viscosity η≳1023 Pa⋅s\eta \gtrsim 10^{23}\text{ Pa}\cdot\text{s} through the ascent, over a weak interface between sial and sima (Papers 3, 13); the making of the record, its preservation and the rise must overlap in time (Paper 6).

• Method: A coupled thermal and rheological balance along the construction.

• Where in the series: Paper 3 §D.1; §A.7; §D.4 item 4; Paper 13 §D.1, §D.3; Paper 6 §O.1; Paper 1 §E.1.

16Stefan adhesion and the time of failure of the contact

The Moon’s contact with the pear was a thin, partly molten layer in which Stefan adhesion acted (resistance to the flow of fluid into the widening gap). The way this layer finally parts sets the release margin beyond the corotation line.

• The question: How does a partly molten layer between two bodies let go — by cavitation, by necking, by a passage from viscous to brittle failure — and when?

• What the model predicts: A slow stage, followed by an unrestrained burst when the gap reaches d=3d0d = 3d_0; the release margin is Δr≃2d0≈210 km\Delta r \simeq 2d_0 \approx 210\text{ km} beyond the corotation line (d0≈105 kmd_0 \approx 105\text{ km}), where the search for the release distance in Paper 3 §C.5a finds the present inclination and the present eccentricity meeting, with slightly more than half of the Moon across the line.

• Data: Laboratory separation of plates joined by partial melt.

• Method: Laboratory and numerical work on non-Newtonian Stefan adhesion, giving the range of the margin and the time of release.

• Where in the series: Paper 3, Further work item 5; §C.5; §C.5a; §D.1; Paper 1 §F.2 and §D.1 (the orange row of Paper 3).

19The layered far-side crust

In the model the far side was built layer upon layer (sial gathered from the Earth’s surface and returned material that landed on the rising shell), the later layers being lighter and richer in fluids; the central peaks of large craters bring up material from known depths and serve as natural drill cores.

• The question: Does the iron content of the far-side crust rise with depth, more strongly than on the near side?

• What the model predicts: The far side is sialic in the sense of the early Earth’s surface and built in layers whose iron rises with depth, and its thickness does not depend on the local history of basins (Paper 3).

• Data: Central-peak compositions (Kaguya, Moon Mineralogy Mapper M3, LRO) with their depth of origin; Chang’e-6 material; GRAIL residuals after removal of the basins.

• Method: A comparison of peak composition against depth of origin (far side against near side) and the building of a construction model that predicts the steps.

• Where in the series: Paper 3, Further work item 6; §B.7; §D.1; §D.4 item 8; Paper 1 §F.3 item 3.

20The near-side crust over the contact

As the body peeled away from the pear, the lower ring of the dome came away with its sial and became the marginal belt, while over the 3,000 km3,000\text{ km} contact the heat of attachment melted the sial and smoothed it.

PART D

The maria and the openings

92The gas push in a long conduit: the critical step

To reach speeds of 2.4 - 2.9 km/s (beyond the ~1 km/s limit of a free volcanic explosion), Paper 10 relies on trapped gas pushing a sealing plug along a confined conduit of 200 - 300 km at a mean pressure of 0.25 - 0.47 GPa (balanced by a surrounding confining pressure of 0.26 - 0.39 GPa) and with an energy of 1.3 × 10²² J for Deimos and 3.6 × 10²² J for Phobos.

• The question: Can gas trapped in a deep chamber push a plug of crust along a conduit of 200 - 300 km to 2.4 - 2.9 km/s without breaking the walls or bypassing the plug?

• What the model predicts: A mean driving pressure of 0.25 - 0.5 GPa balanced by the confining pressure along the path, and a gas mass of 1 to 3 times the plug mass (10¹⁶ - 3 × 10¹⁶ kg of steam; Paper 10).

• Data: The rheology and strength of the Martian crust and mantle at depth; the volatile contents of Martian magmas.

• Method: A one-dimensional and then two-dimensional model of a gas-driven plug in a deformable conduit, computing together gas expansion, wall stress and leakage past the plug.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §C.3, §C.6, §C.8; Part O item 3.

93The soft plug that seals: the cycle that builds it

The plug in Paper 10 is a hot, highly viscous body that seals the conduit a few kilometres below the exit vent and is built in a Vulcanian cycle (Clarke et al. 2015) until its complete break-out (as the inward-folded impact marks on both moons attest).

• The question: In what range of viscosity and temperature does a plug of Martian crust seal a conduit against 0.25 - 0.5 GPa and then leave as one body instead of breaking into fragments?

• What the model predicts: A soft, sealing plug that leaves whole; the first plug (Deimos) small, light and wet, and the second (Phobos) larger, drier and harder (Paper 10).

• Data: Viscosity and fracture data for crystal-rich, volatile-bearing magmas; terrestrial volcanic sequences on the Earth.

• Method: A model of the cooling, sealing and fracture of a viscous plug in a conduit over repeated cycles.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §C.4–C.5, §D.4.

94Drag in the conduit and the braking of Phobos

A plug moving up a conduit on a rotating planet is pressed sideways by the Coriolis effect (2ωv ≈ 1.8 - 2.0 m/s²) against the western wall, which accounts for the grooves of Phobos and its rapid braking.

• The question: How much speed and how much mass does a plug lose against the conduit wall, and is the grooving concentrated on the side that faced west?

• What the model predicts: Phobos was braked to 2.4 - 2.6 km/s and lost about 1% of its mass, and its grooves are concentrated on the side that faced west (while Deimos left without drag; Paper 10).

• Data: The groove maps of Phobos; the shape model.

• Method: A contact-and-friction model of a viscous plug in a rotating conduit, and an examination of the direction of the grooves against the predicted western side.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §C.7, §G.3, §K.1.

95The ring of condensed steam: mass, structure and coupling

A plug fired from the surface lacks the sideways speed needed for a Keplerian orbit and gains it by friction in a ring of condensed steam (a hot gap inside, ice crystals and dust in the middle, and clumps beyond the Roche limit for ice at ~13,000 km).

• The question: Can a young Mars build and keep such a ring, and does a body crossing it gain its orbit, round it and draw it into the equatorial plane before it is ground away?

• What the model predicts: A ring of ~10¹⁶ kg; Deimos was ground by 30% - 60% of its mass in the outer ring, while Phobos was ground by about 1% at the edge of a fading ring (Paper 10).

• Data: Models of circumplanetary discs and of the atmosphere and water of early Mars.

• Method: An N-body or hydrodynamic simulation of a plug crossing a ring of condensed steam, including drag, grinding and the damping of the orbit to the equator.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §E.2, §F.1–F.3, §H.1; Part O item 4.

96The tidal climb of Deimos and a soft young Mars

The tide pushed Deimos from the ring (~15,000 km) to its present orbit (23,463 km), beyond the present corotation line (20,428 km), before the line reached there — a process that requires dissipation 8 - 20 times higher in the young Mars.

• The question: What history of k₂/Q for a hot, soft, fast Mars carries Deimos to 23,463 km before the corotation line reaches 20,428 km, given its larger exit mass?

• What the model predicts: Tidal dissipation 8 - 20 times today’s over 0.5 - 1 Gyr (Paper 10).

• Data: The present orbit of Deimos; models of dissipation in a partly molten planet.

• Method: A coupled integration of the spin rate of Mars, its dissipation and the orbit of Deimos.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §G.2, §J.1; Part O item 2.

97The race of Phobos with the corotation line

Phobos was fired to a height of 10,000 - 11,000 km and pushed outward at first, but the corotation line, receding faster as Mars slowed, caught it near 19,000 km (at a day of ~22 hours), and Phobos has been falling inward since.

• The question: Does one history of the spin of Mars and of its dissipation give both the outward push of Phobos and the faster outward motion of the line, so that the line catches Phobos near 19,000 km?

• What the model predicts: The line catches Phobos near 19,000 km at a day of about 22 hours; running its fall backward at today’s dissipation (k2/Q=1.816×10−3k_2/Q=1.816\times10^{-3}) places it at 19,100 - 19,500 km 3.9 - 4.5 Gyr ago, in agreement with Cheng et al. (2022); the inclination fell from 1.48° to today’s 1.09° (Paper 10).

• Data: The present orbit and inclination of Phobos; the measured dissipation of Mars.

• Method: The same coupled integration as in topic 96, run for Phobos, including the damping of the inclination.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §G.3, §K.4–K.5; Part O item 5.

98The samples of MMX: Martian material, one source, vesicles

The MMX mission will return more than 10 g of regolith from the surface of Phobos in 2031; in the LEF model Phobos is a Martian crustal plug, hydrothermally altered by Martian water, from a single planetary source, never melted in a giant impact nor recondensed from a disc.

• The question: Is Phobos Martian crust from one source, or a mixture of Mars and an impactor, or primitive asteroidal material?

• What the model predicts: Oxygen, chromium and titanium isotopes falling on the Martian fractionation line; no component of an external impactor; zinc and potassium isotopes not shifted by evaporation; hydrated minerals present; and closed gas vesicles of a magmatic foam that cooled (Paper 10, N.1).

• Data: The regolith samples to be returned by the MMX mission (2031).

• Method: Multi-system isotopic analysis and textural microscopy of the returned grains.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §N, Appendix B.

In the model the grooves and the pit chains of Phobos are one structure formed in the conduit, and the grooves were cut by gas-borne dust, not smoothed by water.

• The question: Do pits ever cross grooves? Are the groove walls V-shaped and the edges sharp or only slightly degraded?

• What the model predicts: Pits never cross grooves; V-shaped groove walls; sharp or slightly degraded edges, unlike the rounded surface of Deimos (Paper 10, N.2).

• Data: Existing images and shape models of Phobos; MMX imaging from 2027.

• Method: Mapping the pit chains against the grooves; cutting profiles across the groove walls and edges.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §K.1–K.3, §N.

100The depth of the hollows on Deimos and Phobos

The shape models measured for Paper 10 (Ernst et al. 2023) give a median depth-to-width ratio of the hollows of 1.9% on Deimos against 5.5% on Phobos (Deimos is much more worn); both moons have a sharp end and a blunt end facing Mars.

• The question: Does a complete catalogue of the hollows on both moons confirm the contrast, and does it show that the rims of Deimos are worn rather than buried?

• What the model predicts: Shallow, worn hollows on Deimos, and deeper ones on Phobos; low ridges on Deimos that are the roots of its highest folds (Paper 10, N.3).

• Data: Public shape models of both moons; MMX passes near Deimos.

• Method: Automatic and manual measurement of the depth, width and rim profile of every hollow above a size threshold.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §J.1–J.2, §L.

101The centre of mass of Phobos

A plug that cooled from its surface and was turned by tidal forces so that its blunt end faces Mars need not be uniform in its internal density.

• The question: Is the centre of mass of Phobos offset from its centre of figure toward Mars, toward its blunt end?

• What the model predicts: An offset toward Mars and toward the blunt end (while the usual explanations give no such prediction; Paper 10, N.5).

• Data: Close MMX tracking of Phobos.

• Method: An inversion of the gravity field compared with the shape model.

• Where in the series: Paper 10 §N; Appendix B.

105The other bodies: Venus, Mercury and fast-spinning super-Earths

• The question: What does a parameter study in spin, mass and volatile content predict for the moons of other planets?

• What the model predicts: Nothing is raised on slowly spinning bodies (Venus, Mercury); more on a fast-spinning super-Earth (Paper 1).

• Data: The satellite inventory of the planets; the spins and masses of exoplanets.

• Method: Applying the corotation and pressure tests of the series across the parameter space.

106The height difference between the two poles and the spin at formation

On Mars the north pole is several kilometres lower than the south pole, as part of the Martian dichotomy; the author’s proposal ties this difference to the spin rate of the body at its formation, since the spin rate sets how close the corotation radius lies and how much a young body can raise (Papers 2, 10).

• The question: Does the height difference between the north pole and the south pole of a body grow with the rate at which the body spun at its formation?

• What the model predicts: The faster the spin at formation, the larger the difference between the poles (a hypothesis to be tested, not an existing result of the series).

• Data: Polar topography from orbital altimetry (MOLA for Mars — Smith et al. 1999; LOLA for the Moon; and corresponding data for further bodies); the spin of the young Mars from Paper 10 (§§B.1–B.2) and the spins of the series for the Earth and the Moon.

• Method: In a first stage, modelling the one-sided component that turns fast spin into a polar difference (since spin alone flattens both poles equally); in a second stage, measuring the difference on the ground beneath the polar ice caps relative to the equipotential surface against the spin at formation (taking into account possible polar wander due to the load of Tharsis).

• Where in the series: Raised in the author’s working notes (collection of 16.10.2026); related to topics 104, 105 and 129.

107The arc of births: moon production by distance from the Sun

The extent of moon production depends on the planet’s distance from the Sun: near the Sun the planet dries out fast and the window closes; farther out the planet is rich in water and ejects more moons; and beyond Saturn the surfaces freeze and produce fewer moons (the ratio of the mass of the regular moons to the parent rises from Jupiter, 2.1×10−42.1 \times 10^{-4}, to Saturn, 2.5×10−42.5 \times 10^{-4}, and falls at Uranus, 1.0×10−41.0 \times 10^{-4}, and at Neptune, about 5×10−75 \times 10^{-7}).

• The question: Do the mass of the regular moons relative to the parent, and their number, form an arc by distance from the Sun with a peak at Saturn, when all the regular moons are counted with measured masses?

• What the model predicts: An arc: no birth at Mercury and Venus; one built moon at the Earth; two small moons at Mars; a rise to a peak at Saturn (about 24 regular moons, 2.5×10−42.5 \times 10^{-4}); a fall at Uranus (19, 1.0×10−41.0 \times 10^{-4}) and at Neptune (7, about 5×10−75 \times 10^{-7}) (Paper 11).

• Data: The masses of the regular moons (Jacobson 2010, 2014); the masses of the inner moons of Neptune have no direct measurement and are estimated from size and an assumed density.

• Method: Building a uniform catalogue of regular moons after removing the captured ones by the capture rule (Paper 11 §L.3), and measuring the mass of Proteus and the inner moons of Neptune in a future mission.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §L.4, Figure L.3; §A.10 item 4.

108Mercury and Venus: no bulge, no birth

In the model Mercury and Venus dried out fast near the Sun, and the soft layer beneath the surface was sealed before a birth could take place (and today their corotation radius lies beyond the Hill radius: for Venus 1.5 million km1.5\text{ million km} against ≈1 million km\approx 1\text{ million km}, and for Mercury ≈243,000 km\approx 243,000\text{ km} against ≈220,000 km\approx 220,000\text{ km}), unlike the proposal of Alemi & Stevenson (2006) that Venus bore a moon and lost it.

• The question: Are the surfaces of Mercury and Venus free of signs of a past bulge or pear?

• What the model predicts: No signs of a bulge; Aphrodite Terra is not a remnant of a bulge (Paper 11).

• Data: Topography and gravity from Magellan (Venus) and MESSENGER (Mercury); in future VERITAS, EnVision and BepiColombo.

• Method: A joint analysis of topography and gravity in search of a long equatorial plateau with an excess or deficit of mass matching a bulge.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 Parts C and D, §L.4, Part N.

109How much water escaped through the line: the line at the time of birth in the giants

At Uranus and Neptune the corotation line today lies far outside the birth range (3.243.24 and 3.393.39 radii); the model holds that the line migrated outward as water escaped through it, carrying angular momentum with it (2%−3%2\% - 3\% of the mass of Uranus and Neptune as a lower bound).

• The question: How much water had to escape through the line at each giant to bring the line from its place at the time of birth to its place today?

• What the model predicts: At the time of birth the line stood within the birth range of the ejection mode (between 1.741.74 and 2.062.06 radii); at Uranus and Neptune an escape of at least 2%−3%2\% - 3\% of the mass is required (Paper 11).

• Data: The masses, radii and moment-of-inertia factors of the giants; the orbits of the moons.

• Method: A reverse angular-momentum balance with contraction inside and swelling outside (Paper 11 §A.5) along the history of differentiation, using the radius and the moment-of-inertia factor at the time of birth.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §A.3, §A.5, Parts I and J; Part M item 5.

110The inner body of the giants against their moons

The moons of the giants were born from the inner body of the parent and not from the hydrogen–helium envelope, so the rule “the parent is denser than its moons” compares the densities of the moons with the density of the inner body without the gas envelope. At the same time the envelope acts as a lid that sets how much water remained in the upper layer from which the moons were ejected.

• The question: What is the density of the inner body of each giant, with the hydrogen–helium envelope taken out of the account, and is it higher than that of each of its regular moons?

• What the model predicts: The inner body is denser than every regular moon: at Saturn at least 1,880 kg/m³ (the density of Titan), and at Uranus at least 1,711 kg/m³ (the density of Titania) (Paper 11).

• Data: The gravity fields of the Juno and Cassini missions; interior models of the giants; the densities of the moons.

• Method: Building interior models that separate the inner body from the gas envelope and comparing its density with the densities of the moons.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §A.9 (density without the gas envelope, Figure A.5); Parts G–J.

111The density order of the mid-sized moons of Saturn

In the Saturn system the mid-sized moons are not ordered by density as a function of distance from the planet: Mimas (1,149 kg/m³), Enceladus (1,609 kg/m³), Tethys (984 kg/m³), Dione (1,476 kg/m³) and Rhea (1,237 kg/m³). • The question: What sets the density order of the mid-sized moons of Saturn within stage 2?

• What the model predicts: Open; the model proposes checking whether Saturn’s thick lid caused intermittent feeding of the opening (Paper 11 Part M item 6).

• Data: The densities, frozen shapes and surfaces of the mid-sized moons from the Cassini mission.

• Method: Comparing the density with the frozen shape and the surface age of each moon, as evidence of the place and time of its birth.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 Part H; Part M item 6; Part N (Saturn).

112The density order of the moons of Uranus

At Uranus the density rises with distance (unlike Jupiter: Miranda 1,2001,200, Ariel 1,5921,592, Umbriel 1,3901,390, Titania 1,7111,711, Oberon 1,630 kg/m31,630\text{ kg/m}^3), which is explained by the stages of differentiation (Titania and Oberon left at its start and reflect the parent, and Miranda left at its peak, laden with water and of low density).

• What the model predicts: Titania and Oberon are less differentiated and reflect the parent; Miranda is rich in water (Paper 11).

• Data: Moment-of-inertia factors and gravity fields of the moons (requiring an orbiter at Uranus).

113The 98° tilt of Uranus

The origin of the axial tilt of Uranus (98∘98^{\circ }) remains open: water escape alone requires 117%117\% of the original angular momentum, and shifting the centre of mass alone does not tilt the axis, so a calculation of the effect of the distribution of mass is needed (moons and water on a distant orbit, or an early moon that returned onto the heavy side); the resonances in the literature have also been considered (Boué & Laskar 2010; Rogoszinski & Hamilton 2020; Saillenfest et al. 2022).

• The question: Can a change in the distribution of the mass of Uranus — moons and water on a distant orbit, or an early moon that left and returned — lead to a tilt of 98∘98^{\circ }?

• What the model predicts: Open; it was decided not to decide (Paper 11 §I.1).

• Data: The masses of the moons, the coefficient J2J_{2} of Uranus, the conditions of the early system.

• Method: An integration of the spin axis with an effective J2J_{2} of moons and water in orbit, and of a moon returning onto the heavy side.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §I.1, Figures I.1–I.3; Part M item 2.

114Grooves as the mark of a forced exit from a narrow opening

A moon ejected by force from a narrow opening under internal pressure leaves parallel grooves (a straight exit, as in the grooves of Phobos) or twisted grooves and rings at the end (a screwing exit, as in the belts of Miranda). In the literature the grooves of Phobos are explained as material thrown from craters on Mars (Murray & Heggie 2014), and the belts of Miranda as material that rose from within (Pappalardo et al. 1997).

• The question: Does the geometry of the grooves on Phobos and of the belts on Miranda match the mark of a forced exit from a narrow opening, straight or screwing?

• What the model predicts: Parallel grooves along a direction of exit, or twisted grooves and rings at the end (Paper 11).

• Data: The groove maps of Phobos (HRSC, HiRISE); Voyager 2 images of Miranda.

• Method: Mapping the geometry of the grooves and belts and comparing them with the two patterns of exit.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 Part I (grooves); Part F.

115The breaking of Pluto and Charon and its scars

Pluto split by the breaking of a frozen crust over a soft interior: on Charon the rough region in the north (Oz Terra) is the original crust and the smooth plain on the equator facing Pluto (Vulcan Planitia, Beyer et al. 2017) is the interior that was exposed and rounded; on Pluto the scar migrated before locking according to the sign of its mass.

• The question: Where is the scar of the break on Pluto — near the pole, or is Sputnik Planitia the filled hollow of the break — and what is the origin of the second smooth plain on the far side of Charon?

• What the model predicts: The surfaces of Pluto exposed in the break also smoothed in the rounding; the place of the scar is set by the sign of its mass (Paper 11).

• Data: New Horizons images (including the smooth plain about 400 km400\text{ km} across on the far side of Charon, Beyer et al. 2021, and imaging of the side of Pluto facing Charon).

• Method: Geological mapping of the side facing Charon and a calculation of the wander of Pluto’s spin axis with a scar of mass deficit or excess.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §K.1; Part M item 3; Part N (Pluto).

116The small moons of Pluto: before the split or at the moment of breaking

The four small moons of Pluto (Styx, Nix, Kerberos and Hydra) were ejected from the equator and the neck on circular orbits, and it remains to decide whether all of them left before the split or some were ejected at the moment of breaking.

• The question: Did all the small moons of Pluto leave before the split, or did some of them leave at the moment of breaking?

• What the model predicts: Ejection before the split, on circular orbits in the plane of Charon (Paper 11).

• Method: A simulation of ejection from the equator of a body on the verge of separation against ejection at the moment of breaking, and a comparison with the orbits and the ratios of orbital periods.

• Where in the series: Paper 11 §K.1; Part M item 11.

123No foreign material: the isotopic decision (R14)

• The question: Does the metal that was part of the old samples when they formed — the iron–nickel of the two dated norites of Chang’e-6, and the siderophile elements in the melts of every basin of the maria — carry a terrestrial or a non-terrestrial isotopic signature (Cr, Ti, Mo, Ru, Os)?

• What the model predicts: About 99% of the craters are made of returned terrestrial material (Papers 1, 5, 6, 8, 13); the norite metal is terrestrial (Paper 8, B14, partly fitting; the isotopes decide).

• Data: The Chang’e-6 norites (CE6C0100YJFM002-158 and CE6C0200YJFM001EGP07-16); melts from crater sites of Apollo and Chang’e; Pacific plume basalts as a terrestrial reference.

• Method: Nucleosynthetic isotope analyses and a comparison with a mixing model of a terrestrial returning body with a terrestrial target against asteroidal patterns.

• Where in the series: Paper 8 B14, §E.1–E.2, Part F row 1, §G.2 item 8; Paper 5 §J.7, Further work item 4, §M.3; Paper 6 §L.5, Further work item 6, §O.1; Paper 13 §C.6 (R14), §D.3; Paper 1 §E.2, §F.3 item 4, §D.1.

124A multi-system isotope balance (R1)

The Earth and the Moon are almost identical in oxygen isotopes (Δ¹⁷O = -1 ± 5 ppm), but the model requires that one initial composition (the mantle of the early Earth plus the deep upwelling that fed the pear) close at the same time the isotopic balance of titanium, chromium, tungsten and volatile elements after separation and evaporation.

• The question: Does one composition — the mantle of the early Earth plus the deep upwelling, after separation and evaporation — match the lunar data in O, Ti, Cr, W and the volatile elements together?

• What the model predicts: Terrestrial material throughout; oxygen: Δ¹⁷O = -1 ± 5 ppm between the Earth and the Moon (Paper 13).

• Data: Published lunar and terrestrial isotope data.

• Method: Running a multi-system mixing and fractionation model with one set of inputs for all the systems.

• Where in the series: Paper 13, Further work item 2; §A.1, §B.1, §D.3; Paper 1 §E.1 (R1).

125The metal budget: the core as a gauge (R2, R13)

The Moon’s core is small: about 1.4% of its mass (range 1% - 2%), with a radius of about 330 km. In the model the Moon’s material rose from a source next to the iron that descended to the Earth’s core, but not from that iron itself; a rising foamed melt dragged droplets of that iron with it — some sank on the way and some were carried up — so the size of the core gauges how deep the feed reached.

• The question: What fraction of the descending iron does a rising foamed melt drag along, and does it give a lunar core of about 1.4% (1% - 2%) from a source next to the descending iron, but not from that iron itself?

• What the model predicts: The small core gauges how deep the feed reached (Papers 3 and 13).

• Data: The seismic size of the core (about 1.4%); the feed rate of Paper 3.

• Method: The fluid dynamics of droplet entrainment and settling in a rising melt.

• Where in the series: Paper 13, Further work item 3; §A.2, §B.2, §C.6 (R13), §D.3; Paper 3 §C.10; Paper 1 §F.2.

126The joint crust–centre-of-mass solution (R3, R7)

The Moon’s centre of mass is offset by about 2 km2\text{ km} from its centre of figure toward the Earth; Paper 13 shows that a crust of the modelled shape offsets the centre of mass by 1.24−3.11 km1.24 - 3.11\text{ km} for density contrasts of 300−750 kg/m3300 - 750\text{ kg/m}^3, and that the mass of the dome shell (2.85×1021 kg2.85 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg}) matches the mass of the far-side crust (2.6×1021 kg2.6 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg}) to about 10%10\%. • The question: Does one supported density model give the size and the direction of the 2 km2\text{ km} offset of the centre of mass, together with the measured crustal thickness, on the geometry of the exposure zones?

• What the model predicts: A sensitivity range of 1.24−3.11 km1.24 - 3.11\text{ km} for density contrasts of 300−750 kg/m3300 - 750\text{ kg/m}^3 against the observed 2 km2\text{ km} or so, and a shell mass of 2.85×1021 kg2.85 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg} against the far-side crust of 2.6×1021 kg2.6 \times 10^{21}\text{ kg} (Paper 13).

• Method: A joint inversion of crustal thickness and centre of mass.

• Where in the series: Paper 13, Further work item 4; §B.3, §B.7, Appendix A, §D.3; Paper 3 §D.2; Paper 1 §F.2.

127Two crusts, two ages (R5)

Since in the model the surface of the far side was the outer part of the dome from the start and was preserved while the near side was attached to the Earth, the highland crust of the far side must be older than comparable highland crust on the near side (comparing crystallisation age with crystallisation age, outside impact basins).

• The question: Is the highland crust of the far side older than comparable highland crust on the near side, when the sampling is done outside the basins?

• What the model predicts: The far side closed and was preserved first (Paper 13).

• Data: Chang’e-6 material; future samples from the far side.

• Method: Comparing primary crystallisation ages (clock 1) of comparable highland units on both sides outside the basins.

• Where in the series: Paper 13, Further work item 5; §A.5, §B.5, §D.3.

128Volatiles in the amount and phase the feed needs (R6)

Volatiles play two roles: dissolved or as bubbles they lower the density of the feed so that it can stand up to the corotation radius, and in expanding they supply the driving pressure; what remains is to link the water measured in lunar melt inclusions (615−1,410 ppm615 - 1,410\text{ ppm}) with the amount and phase needed for density, rheology and pressure at the same time.

• The question: Does the water measured in lunar melt inclusions (615−1,410 ppm615 - 1,410\text{ ppm}) match the amount and phase that the density, rheology and pressure of the feed require at one and the same time?

• What the model predicts: Volatiles lower the density of the feed and supply its pressure (Papers 3 and 13).

• Data: Water measurements in melt inclusions and in apatite.

• Method: Cross-checking the transport requirement of topic 4 against the measured inventory.

• Where in the series: Paper 13 §A.6, §B.6, §D.3; Paper 3 §A.6.

129North–south asymmetry with South Pole–Aitken removed

Since the South Pole–Aitken (SPA) basin lies wholly in the southern hemisphere and distorts the hemispheric means, testing whether a global north–south asymmetry exists requires full removal of the near–far asymmetry and of SPA and the large basins.

• The question: Does any north–south asymmetry of the Moon remain once the near–far asymmetry and the large basins, SPA first among them, are removed?

• What the model predicts: This must be tested, not assumed in advance; SPA alone may not confirm a hemispheric asymmetry (Paper 13).

• Data: The GRAIL crustal models; crater catalogues.

• Method: An analysis of north–south asymmetry with SPA and the large basins masked identically in both hemispheres.

• Where in the series: Paper 13 §C.5; Paper 7 §D.4.

130The composition of the lunar core: the Earth’s iron, caught on its way down

Planetary cores contain light elements dissolved in the iron that record the conditions of their formation (silicon and oxygen at the high pressures of a deep core, against sulphur at low pressure as droplets settle through a silicate mantle); since in the model the lunar core is terrestrial iron caught in the ascent while the Earth’s iron was descending to its core (Paper 3, §C.10), its dominant light element should be sulphur and not silicon and oxygen.

• The question: Is the light element of the lunar core sulphur, and not silicon and oxygen?

• What the model predicts: The lunar core is terrestrial iron carried up with the feed while the Earth’s iron was still descending (Paper 3, §C.10), and its light element is mainly sulphur; a core whose light elements are silicon and oxygen would not support this reading.

• Data: The seismic core of Weber et al. (2011 — radius ~330 km, solid inner core ~240 km, density and wave speeds); seismic data from a future lunar geophysical network; laboratory measurements of density and sound speed in Fe–S and Fe–Si–O liquids at lunar-core pressures.

• Method: Comparing the measured density and wave speeds of the outer core with the candidate alloys under lunar conditions, and building a model of the light-element content of the terrestrial metal during its descent.

• Where in the series: Raised in the author’s working notes (collection of 16.10.2026); related to Paper 3 §C.10, Paper 13 §A.2, §B.2, §C.6 (R2, R13) and topic 125.

131The core offset as the residual of the centre-of-mass offset

While Paper 13 explains the offset of the lunar centre of mass (~2 km toward the Earth) by an asymmetric crust over a symmetric core (an offset of 1.24 - 3.11 km for a density contrast of 300 - 750 kg/m³), the author further proposes that the core itself (1% - 2% of the Moon’s mass) is offset by 100 - 200 km or more toward the Earth, following the push of the burner and the pull of the Earth before it froze.

• The question: How much of the centre-of-mass offset, in size and direction, remains once the crust and the topography are taken into account, and does this residual fit a core offset toward the Earth?

• What the model predicts: A residual toward the Earth matching a core offset of the order of 100 - 200 km or more (a zero residual, or a residual pointing away from the Earth, leaves no room for an offset core).

• Data: LOLA topography (centre of figure); GRAIL gravity and the GRAIL crustal-thickness models; the seismic core (~330 km, about 1.4% of the mass).

• Method: Computing the full offset vector from the crust and the topography under one density model (topic 126), converting the residual vector into a displacement of a core of the seismic size, and computing the forces (tide, attachment and the push of the burner) that could have displaced the core and fixed it in place.

• Where in the series: Paper 13 §A.7, §B.7, Further work item 4; Paper 3 §E.5; the author’s working notes (collection of 16.10.2026).

132An orphan minimum of crustal thickness

In the model the thickness of the lunar crust is thinned only by the broad order of the exposure zones, beneath the openings (a jump of ≥ 8 km at 29 of 36 openings, Paper 5) and beneath the large basins; the existence of an “orphan” local minimum that lies beneath no opening and no basin would be a difficulty for the model.

• The question: Does the crustal-thickness map contain a local minimum that lies neither beneath a basin nor beneath an opening?

• What the model predicts: No such minimum exists; every local minimum of the crust lies beneath an opening or beneath a basin (and the broad thinning of the contact region is the order of the exposure zones).

• Data: The four GRAIL crustal-thickness models (Wieczorek et al. 2013); the openings of Paper 5; the catalogue of lunar basins identified in GRAIL gravity (Neumann et al. 2015).

• Method: An automatic search for local minima in all four models under depth and size criteria fixed in advance (such as the rings and the 8 km threshold of Paper 5 §D.2: centre to 0.3R against a ring at 1.3 - 2.0R) and cross-checking them against the openings and the basins.

• Where in the series: Paper 5 §D.2; Paper 13 §B.8; Paper 3 §B.7; the author’s working notes (collection of 16.10.2026).

FURTHER WORKFurther work outside this paper

This section sets out the two complex fields of research named in the LEF series as future work, which have been left outside the list of 132 numbered topics in Paper 14.

• The Earth–Moon axes system (from release to the present): following the evolution of the four dynamical planes of the system — the Earth’s equator, the Moon’s orbit, the Moon’s equator and the plane of the ecliptic — which move in two kinds of motion at different rates, under the influence of the shape memory of a frozen bulge. The first step in this research is to examine the channel of axial tilt: how the coupling of the Sun, the Earth and the Moon acted on the initial tilt of 3.8∘3.8^{\circ } given to the Earth’s spin axis at release (whether it enlarged or damped it on the way to the present value of 23.44∘23.44^{\circ }), and whether a change in the Earth’s axis after separation caused the South Pole–Aitken basin to appear offset relative to the straight line facing the terrestrial burner.

• The magnetism of the Moon: the origin of the Moon's ancient magnetic field and its spatial pattern over the South Pole–Aitken basin (erased at the centre, strong on the trailing side, and including the magnetic swirls of Mare Ingenii), read in the series as a joint record of prolonged heating and of iron that came from the Earth. This study includes the test of the depth and direction of the magnetization, which must be strongest at the rim of the heated core region and come from a deep source.
