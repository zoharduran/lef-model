The Moon released at the corotation radius of a fast-spinning early Earth: a fission route that closes the angular-momentum budget

LPSC 2027 abstract

Z. Duran, Independent Researcher, Ramla, Israel · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 17 November 2026

Two-page conference abstract prepared for the 58th Lunar and Planetary Science Conference (LPSC 2027). It condenses the short version of Paper 2; the full Paper 2 gives every step.

1Introduction

Darwin’s fission hypothesis [1] and its subsequent formulations [2, 3] were set aside primarily because launching material from the Earth’s surface at orbital speed requires the Earth to spin so rapidly that the system would carry 2.74 times its present angular momentum. Here we examine an alternative dynamical route. Material co-rotating with a spinning planet moves faster with increasing distance from the rotation axis, whereas the speed of a free circular orbit decreases with distance. At the corotation (synchronous) radius, these two speeds are equal; material reaching this radius while still co-rotating with its parent body is therefore already at orbital speed upon release. We propose that the Moon was carried outward while still attached and co-rotating, and released at this radius rather than launched from the surface. This abstract tests whether such a route is permitted by the angular-momentum budget. We address the dynamics alone; the physical mechanism carrying the material outward is treated elsewhere [4].

2The parent

We consider a young, hot Earth that is thermally expanded and still nearly uniform in density (moment-of-inertia factor k = 0.4), with a radius of 6,517 km and a day of 5.5 h. For this parent body, the corotation radius is Rsync = (GM/ω²)1/3 = 15,818 km, where the circular orbital speed is 5,020 m/s. The total angular momentum of the parent is L0 = 3.466 × 1034 kg m² s−1, which is 0.7% above that of the present Earth–Moon system (3.441 × 1034 kg m² s−1) — a small surplus matching the amount later removed by solar tides [5]. The permissible window is 4.90–5.54 h for the initial day and 98–520 km for the thermal expansion.

3The budget during transfer

Two opposing processes act on the Earth’s spin while the lunar mass is carried outward. Moving mass outward slows the Earth’s rotation, whereas iron sinking toward the centre accelerates it. Solving both processes concurrently for a core twice as dense as the overlying mantle (centralisation D ≈ 2) yields a day of 5.77 h at release and a release radius of about 16,400 km, roughly 210 km beyond the instantaneous corotation line. At release, the lunar orbit holds about 17% of the angular-momentum budget (15.6% transferred during outward transport), while the Earth’s moment-of-inertia factor decreases to 0.355, consistent with an independent two-layer estimate of 0.360. Release off the equatorial plane alters the budget by a vector correction of only 1.46–2.51%.

4Release state

Figure 1 illustrates the release geometry to scale. Release at 16,400 km lies outside the rigid Roche limit (9,500–13,000 km for densities of 1,300–3,344 kg/m³) but inside the fluid Roche limit for 2,600 kg/m³ (about 20,000 km) [6]. Consequently, the Moon must separate as a body rigid enough to resist tidal disruption.

5What the mechanism must supply

This route closes both its velocity condition and its angular-momentum budget with a single set of parameters, placing four specific requirements on the transport mechanism:

  1. Early onset. Transfer begins while the Earth is still nearly uniform (k ≥ 0.397).
  2. Thermal expansion. Initial expansion lies within 98–520 km.
  3. Concurrent core sinking. Core sinking proceeds continuously during transfer; without it, outward mass transport alone would slow the Earth to a 6.44 h day.
  4. Lunar rigidity. The Moon is rigid enough at release to survive inside the fluid Roche limit.

None of these parameters enters the calculation of the corotation radius itself, which depends solely on the parent body’s mass and spin rate.

6Refutation criteria

The proposed route is refuted if it is shown that:

  1. the Earth’s internal differentiation was already advanced (k well below 0.397) before lunar material could separate;
  2. the total angular momentum of the early system differed from today’s by substantially more than the few per cent attributable to solar tides; or
  3. the young Moon could not have been rigid at the time and distance of release.

7Conclusions

CONCLUSION

The classical objection to rotational fission applies specifically to launch from the Earth’s surface. At the corotation radius, orbital speed is provided directly by planetary rotation, reducing the required angular-momentum budget from 2.74 to 1.007 times today’s value. A fission origin of the Moon is therefore dynamically permitted by the angular-momentum budget of the present Earth–Moon system, subject to the four physical requirements outlined above.
