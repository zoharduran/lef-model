A SERIES OF PAPERS ON THE ORIGIN OF THE MOON AND OF THE MOONS OF MARS

The LEF Model of Lunar Origin

Lunar Elevation and Fission: the Moon built from the Earth while attached to it, and released at the corotation radius

We propose that the Moon was built from terrestrial material while still attached to the Earth, and was released where the Earth's rotation had already given it orbital speed. The LEF model is a process model: it sets out a sequence of physical events, and for each event the papers give the number that sets it and the observation that tests it. Paper 1 presents the whole process; Papers 2–9 and 13 take its parts one at a time; Paper 10 applies a related account to the moons of Mars; Paper 14 collects the research topics the model opens.

  • 02
    LEF Mechanism in Principle
    Corotation and angular momentum

    Material that is part of a spinning planet turns with it, and its speed grows with distance, while the speed of a free circular orbit falls with distance. At the corotation radius the two are equal: material carried there while still turning with the parent, and released there, already moves at orbital speed. We examine whether transferring lunar material outward from a terrestrial parent, with corotation maintained, can reach a state whose orbital velocity and angular-momentum budget match the present Earth–Moon system. For an initial day of 5. 5 hours the corotation radius is 15,818 km and the orbital speed there is 5,020 m/s.

  • 03
    LEF Mechanism in Practice
    Pressure and the pear: building the Moon from within and from without

    In the LEF model the Moon was built from Earth material while still attached to it, by one process acting in two ways. From within, deep hot melt driven by internal pressure fed a rising body. From without, the Earth's light outer shell rose with a pear-shaped bulge and slid to its tip, where it became the body's skin and later the Moon's far-side crust. A one-dimensional pressure test gives a source pressure of 150. 26 GPa. Lifting melt of 3,500 kg/m³ to the corotation radius costs 139. 78 GPa, so the feed passes for melt lighter than about 3,864 kg/m³.

  • 05
    The Maria
    Openings over Feed Domes

    We propose that the lunar maria are openings in the shell of a Moon fed from within while attached to the Earth. Each stands over a feed dome, a dense body pushed up from a subsurface focus. Through most openings material rose, was thrown out and returned as the material that made the craters; Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, passed material and pressure into the Moon. The basalt plain is only the last frozen layer of such an opening. In the GRAIL Bouguer field a feed dome shows as a round high. Of 73 highs of 100 km and more, 25 lie under an open mare and 44 under highland, 36 of them on the far side.

  • 06
    The Craters
    Craters Without Asteroids

    In the LEF model deep material was ejected through openings in the young Earth's envelope, and part of it returned to the outer shell that later rose with the forming Moon. We ask whether such material could arrive fast enough to crater and shock the target. In a central-gravity approximation without drag, material ejected radially and returning to the same radius lands at a relative speed equal to its ejection speed. At 5 km/s the flight lasts about 28 minutes and lands about 1,032 km to the west; an ideal head-on basalt landing gives a contact pressure of 45. 6 GPa.

  • 07
    The Poles
    Why the Poles Are Crowded

    The Moon's poles carry more craters than its equator. The count is made in the Robbins catalogue with the maria and South Pole–Aitken removed on both sides. On that clean count, the density of craters of 20 km and more within 30° caps about each pole is 1. 20 ± 0. 05 times that of the equatorial belt. The ratio rises with crater size, from 1. 09 (20–30 km) to 1. 53 (60–100 km). Material falling from above, of any origin, should land uniformly; two crowded poles at the ends of one axis require that something ordered the craters by the axis. We propose that the record froze on a young Moon that changed shape.

  • 08
    South Pole–Aitken
    The Crater-Poor Centre

    South Pole–Aitken (SPA), the largest and oldest basin on the Moon, should hold one of its fullest crater records, yet its centre is crater-poor. The count is made in rings from the middle of the basin, on ground cleaned of the lava plains and of the 34 maria of this series. It rises steadily from centre to rim, and the central region holds about half the craters found just outside the basin. The minimum lies about 270 km north-north-east of the centre, on the meridian of the antipode of Serenitatis. Craters of 20–40 km are the most depleted.

    Mars: The Protrusion and the Ring
    How a fast, expanded Mars set Deimos and Phobos free from a volcano on its tapered protrusion

    Phobos and Deimos circle Mars on near-circular orbits of small inclination above its equatorial plane; Phobos lies inside the corotation radius and Deimos outside it. The surface of Deimos is smooth and rounded, while Phobos is scored by systems of long, parallel grooves. Both bodies are light and porous. Although Mars lost most of its water, a large reservoir of liquid water lies today 11.5–20 km beneath its crust.

  • 11
    Moon Births
    One mechanism for the births of moons, tested planet by planet across the Solar System

    Most moons of the Solar System orbit close to the equatorial plane of their parent, and many sit close to the corotation line, the height at which a body turning with the planet moves at orbital speed. In this paper we propose that every regular moon was born in the same way: from its parent, at the corotation line, in a window opened and closed by the parent’s differentiation. One parameter sets where the line stands in units of the parent’s radius — the ratio of the spin to the density — so one ruler serves all bodies.

    A ring breaks, grinds and returns

    The Requirements
    What the mechanism forces, what the Moon has, and what remains unresolved

    Suppose the Moon was built from Earth material while attached to it: deep material was fed into the developing body while the parent's outer shell rose with it and became its crust. What properties should the Moon then have? We state each such property as a requirement with three parts: the condition the model must meet, the quantity that tests it, and the result that would not fit it. Requirements are used rather than a total score, because several matches can be one fact seen several ways and a single clear mismatch must be counted on its own.

    Research Topics
    What the model opens for other researchers

    The LEF model proposes that the Moon was built from the Earth's own material while still attached to it. A fast-spinning, hot Earth raised a pear-shaped extension, and deep, volatile-rich melt fed a body on its tip. The Earth's outer shell rose with that body and became its crust, and the body was released when its centre of mass reached the corotation line. This companion to the series computes nothing new. It collects from Papers 1–13 what they leave open: untested requirements, studies defined but not yet run, calculations marked "to be computed", and results marked partial or awaiting.

    Based on Paper 04 — The Orbit (Parts A–E and Appendices A–B)

    For more than thirty years, the inclination of the Moon's orbit — an angle of 5.145° to the plane of the ecliptic — was considered one of the most stubborn riddles in planetary science. Why is this angle so hard to explain? Because in all the giant-impact theories, the Moon coalesces from a debris disc swirling around the Earth. Such a disc quickly settles into the Earth's equatorial plane, so a moon born from it should begin its life with almost zero tilt — and then late, complicated scenarios are needed (such as encounters with giant planetesimals, orbital resonances or an extreme tilt of the Earth's axis of more than 70°) to tilt the orbit up.
