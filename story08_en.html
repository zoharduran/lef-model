Chapter 8: What Is a Mare Really? — the Feeding Openings, the 73 Gravity Domes and the Floor That Sank Like a Frozen Lake

Based on Paper 05 — The Maria (Parts A–F: Studies 1–7 and 16)

01 · THE MYSTERY

The mystery: why does the Moon have two faces — and where are the maria of the far side hiding?

Anyone who looks at the Moon, even through simple binoculars, at once notices the sharp contrast that shaped the "man in the Moon": the near side, always facing the Earth, is covered with dark, round expanses ("maria" — Maria in Latin, because the ancients thought they were oceans), while the far side is bright, scarred and almost free of dark patches.

The accepted literature interprets each mare as two separate, unrelated events: first a giant asteroid struck the Moon and dug a deep basin in it; and hundreds of millions of years later dark basaltic lava broke out of the mantle and flooded the basin. By this definition, a mare is simply a surface of dark basalt.

But when the precise gravity maps of the GRAIL spacecraft are examined, a mystery appears that shakes this story to its foundations: beneath the bright surface of the far side hide dozens of round, dense bodies of excess gravity — identical in size and strength to those beneath the giant maria of the near side, but without any dark basalt on their surface! What are these hidden bodies, and does the far side look mare-free only because we are looking at its colour?

02 · AN IMAGE

An image: covered wells in a field and a frozen crust on a lava lake

To understand the turnaround that Paper 05 proposes in the definition of the maria, imagine two simple images:

  • A lava lake in a volcano's crater: looking down on an extinct volcanic crater, you see a dark, frozen rock surface. This dark crust is not the mountain itself — it is only the last layer that froze from above after the flow stopped. Beneath it lies the deep vent through which the lava rose. In exactly the same way, the dark basalt on the Moon's face is not the mare — the mare is the opening through which the Moon was built, and the basalt is only the last crust that froze at the top of the opening!
  • A field with active wells on two sides — when one side has been covered with earth: imagine a large field in which dozens of wells were dug. On one side of the field (the near side), the wells stayed open until the end of the building, so the dark water surfaces inside them can be seen. On the other side of the field (the far side), the wells closed earlier and were paved over with a thick layer of bright earth. Someone who counts only "dark water patches" will think there are wells on one side only — but an instrument that measures below the ground will find that the covered side has even more wells!
03 · SCIENCE AND MODEL

Science and model: separating the concepts and counting the 73 feeding domes

Parts A–D of Paper 05 break the word "mare" into four clear physical components, from the bottom up:

  1. The feed dome: a dense body rich in iron and titanium that was pushed upward from an underground focus, lifted its centre and widened its edges. When the feeding ended, the dome froze into a dense body that pulls the GRAIL spacecraft more strongly — and this is the mascon (mass concentration) measured in gravity.
  2. The opening: the fluid, low-viscosity zone in the shell through which material rose to the surface. Through most of the openings, material was fired outward and fell back onto the Moon to create the craters (exit openings); while through Mare Imbrium and Mare Serenitatis — which sat inside the contact disc with the Earth — the pressure and material passed inward into the Moon without firing outward (feeding mouths).
  3. The mare basalt: the last layer that froze at the top of the opening. By contrast, broad basalt surfaces with no feed dome beneath them — such as Oceanus Procellarum, Mare Tranquillitatis and Mare Fecunditatis — are not "maria" in the structural sense, but crustal plains melted in place by the heat of the nearby Earth!
  4. The basin: the raised rim surrounding the opening — built layer upon layer from the hot material that the opening itself fired around it.
The decisive discovery in gravity (Studies 1, 3 and 4)

When the GRAIL gravity map (Bouguer anomaly, degrees 6–660) is scanned automatically for every round dense body 100 km or more across and 50 mGal or more in strength, 73 feeding domes appear on the face of the Moon:
• 33 of the 34 maria identified on the gravity-gradient map sit exactly on one of the 73 domes (in 20,000 random runs not a single run reached 33!). The only one not sitting on a dome is Mare Ingenii, whose dome was "cooked" and erased by the enormous heat beneath the South Pole–Aitken basin.
• Of the 73 domes, 25 lie beneath an open mare, 4 are partly covered, and 44 domes are completely buried beneath the bright highlands — 36 of them on the far side! That is, the far side has more feeding domes than the near side (40 against 33).
• The crustal step (17 km on average): beneath the centre of the opening the crust is 8–29 km thinner (median 17 km) than in the ring around it (in 29 of 36 marked openings, compared with only 2% in ordinary impact craters) — proof that the dome pushed and lifted from below, not an external impact from above.
• The blanket over the buried domes: above the 44 buried domes, the iron content (−0.19 wt%) and thorium (+0.05 ppm) are completely identical to the surrounding ground, and the crust is thicker by 8–13 km — the thickness of the later layers that covered the ancient openings of the far side together with their craters!

04 · THE MECHANISM IN WORDS

The mechanism in words: the floor of a frozen lake, the sinking of the centre and the collar of fired material

What happens to the floor of a mare once the feeding through it stops, and why is it shaped like a sunken basin surrounded by raised edges? Parts E and F of Paper 05 reveal four measurable geometric phenomena:

  1. One level, like a frozen lake (Study 6): a fluid at rest under gravity cannot hold a slope — it settles to a single uniform level and freezes that way. In precise height measurements (SLDEM2015), 16 out of 16 large mare floors (and also 188 of the 189 mapped basalt units) show a single height level, lying deep below the rim of the mare (median 3.4 km, chance probability 1 in 65,000) and below the surrounding ground (median 1.6 km).
  2. The centre sank above the dome — except in the two contact maria (Study 5): when the feed dome freezes into a dense, iron-rich body, the centre above it sinks (mainly through thermal contraction on cooling, alongside the weight of the dense body and suction). Indeed, in 10 of the 12 open maria the centre is lower than the edges (median −267 m, for example −2.2 km at Orientale and −1.6 km at Nectaris), and the stronger the gravity dome, the deeper the sinking (ρ=−0.59,p=0.04\rho = -0.59, p = 0.04).
    The only two exceptions, which confirm the rule: at Mare Serenitatis (+60 m) and Mare Imbrium (+43 m) the centre is actually slightly higher than the edges! Why? Because these are the two maria that sat inside the contact disc and froze very close to the Earth, whose strong pull held the near side stretched outward while they froze!
  3. Stretched edges and a compressed interior (§F.2): like a sheet of ice on a pond whose water drops in the middle, the sinking of the centre stretched the edges of the mare and created rifts in them (grabens at 0.76–1.22 radii), and compressed the interior to form wrinkle ridges (at 0.71–0.77 radii).
  4. The collar of fired material around the far-side maria (Study 16): why do the edges around the far-side maria rise so high? Not because lava spilled over the rim (basalt covers only 0%–7% of the ring around them), but because the hot material fired from the opening landed around it and was smeared on layer upon layer! As a result, a raised "collar" was built around the far-side maria at 150–300 km (with a median height of +742 m around the buried maria), while around the contact maria — which fed inward and did not fire outward — there is no collar at all (−137 m).

06 · THE SIGNATURE

The signature in reality: clean floors that rank the order of freezing

If a mare floor begins to keep craters only from the moment it froze, then the number of craters on it serves as a direct measure of the order in which the maria froze (Studies 7 and 12):

  • Floors 12 times cleaner than their surroundings: in all 10 open maria measured (after removing the fields of young rays on both sides), the floor carries on average only 8% (1/12) of the density of 2–5-km craters in the surrounding ground!
  • A ratio of 83 times in large craters (10 km and more): the contact maria (Imbrium and Serenitatis) froze last and are therefore the cleanest (256 craters of 2–5 km per million km², and only 3.2 craters of 10+ km per million km²). By contrast, the maria on the hill carry 33 times more 10+-km craters (106 per million km²), and the maria beyond it (Orientale, Moscoviense and Poincaré) carry 83 times more (266 per million km²)!

The door ahead

We have discovered that the Moon's maria are not lava lakes that happened to break into asteroid craters, but the ancient feeding openings through which the Moon was built: 73 gravity domes (44 of them buried beneath the far-side highlands), each of which left a uniform floor like a frozen lake that sank at its centre after the flow stopped.
