The Stripes

The leading side of the pear on the floor of the Pacific, and the place where the Moon was formed

Zohar Duran · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 15 October 2026

Abstract

The floor of the North Pacific is crossed by long, straight stripes — Mendocino, Pioneer, Murray, Molokai, Clarion and Clipperton. The stripes are thousands of kilometres long, and they are parallel to one another. They are seen most clearly on maps of the vertical gravity gradient (VGG). These maps show where the pull of gravity changes sharply over a short distance, and so they pick out narrow structures in the floor and beneath it. In this series the Moon was built on the tip of the pear. The pear is made of the Earth’s own material; its base stood on the Pacific near Kiritimati, and it was gathered back into the Earth after the Moon was detached (Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice). The pear leaned back against the Earth’s rotation: its tip lagged to the west; its front — the side facing the direction of rotation, the leading side — faced east; and its back, the trailing side, faced west. This paper reads the stripes as the leading side of that pear: the side that faced the Earth’s rotation, that was stretched while the pear was attached, and that folded into stripes as the pear was gathered back.

The geometry fits this reading in four ways. The equator. The stripes are parallels of one equator, and the base of the pear lies on that equator (1.6° from it). “Parallels of one equator” means that every stripe keeps a constant distance from one great circle, as the lines of latitude keep a constant distance from the Earth’s equator — only here the equator is tilted. Lines of this kind are what a single turning motion about a single axis draws, and six such lines sharing one equator are a strong geometric constraint on how they were made. The direction of departure. The stripes run from the base eastward, toward America. The bends. West of the base there are two bends in the stripes: one in the north, bending down, and one in the south, bending up. The bends are 3,360 km apart — the width of the 3,000-km contact on which the Moon sat, and of the 3,650-km dome above it. Their centre lies about 3,760 km west of the base, on the trailing side, where a pear that leaned back would place its tip. The mirror imprint. Inside the tip lies the imprint of the Moon’s near side, as a mirror image. The two faces stood facing each other, so that, as with a seal pressed into wax, east and west are exchanged while north stays north. The imprint is enlarged by 1.37, which is the contraction of the Moon from the density of the body on the tip to its density today: the same mass, packed into a smaller volume, makes every distance on its surface shorter by that factor. With Imbrium placed at 13.2°N 179.0°E, Serenitatis falls on the Marshall chain and the contact centre beside Imbrium — all inside the footprint of the tip.

The stripes pass beneath the volcanic ridges that cross them, unbroken. It follows that they were made by folding from the sides and from below, not cut from above. The large ridge chains on either side — the Marshall–Gilbert–Tuvalu chains in the west and the Line Islands in the east — are read as the spatula ridges. These ridges lie within the tip of the pear itself, in its peeled part: they were drawn up from the still-soft material of the contact during the peeling of the Moon, as the contact narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km, as it was pulled outward with force — like the ridges plaster leaves when a spatula is pulled off it — and they were brought down to the floor as the pear was gathered back. The equator of the stripe system is inclined by at least 10° to today’s equator and crosses it near the base: the Earth’s axis may have changed after the separation, which the series leaves for future work.

This draft collects the observations, the measurements and one study made so far (C.5). The studies still to come are set out in Part G: the fit of the equator of the stripes, a test of chance for the base on the equator, the continuity of the stripes beneath the ridges, and a comparison of the spatula ridges with ridges built from below elsewhere in the world.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

The claim. The stripes of the North Pacific are the leading side of the pear on which the Moon was built: they were stretched in the direction of the Earth’s rotation while the pear stood, and folded into stripes as it was gathered back into the Earth. The pear’s tip — the place where the Moon was formed — lies west of the base, on the trailing side. The tip left its outline in two bends of the stripes, in the mirror imprint of the near side, and in two chains of ridges drawn up in the contact region during the peeling of the Moon, as it was pulled outward with force. In short, the floor of the North Pacific is read as the place where the pear stood and was gathered back, and the stripes as the record of its return.

The numbers. The stripes lie from 33.6° north of the equator of the stripe system (Mendocino) to 1.8° (Clipperton). The base of the pear (Kiritimati, 1.9°N 157.5°W) lies 1.6° from the equator of the system, which is inclined by at least 10° to today’s equator (up to about 20°; the positions in this paper are given for a fit of 11°, crossing at 159°W). The bends: 23.8°N 164.9°E and 5.3°S 173.2°E, 3,360 km apart (3,040–3,700); the centre at 9.3°N 169.3°E, about 3,760 km west of the base. The near side on the Earth, as a mirror image at ×\times1.37: Imbrium at 13.19°N 179.04°E; Serenitatis at ≈\approx11.5°N 166.3°E; the contact centre at ≈\approx8.2°N 179.3°W. The ridge chains are 2,800–3,500 km apart. Length of the pear’s own body, with its tip region, at release: 8,890–9,000 km; with the Moon as its tip, the pear reached the corotation line (10,570–10,690 km); the stripe system: 9,360 km (C.4). Traced on the GEBCO 2026 grid, the stripes of the north, the centre and the south of the Pacific lead to the tip: all ten traced paths reach the tip region, and six of them pass within 1,500 km of the centre of the final contact (166–1,274 km), against none of three reference lines (C.5). Read together. The bends and the ridge chains both measure a width of about three thousand kilometres — the width of the contact on which the Moon sat — and the base and the southern edge of the tip lie on the equator of the stripes.

What it requires. (1) A pear with its base on the Pacific near Kiritimati and its tip lagging west (Paper 3). (2) A leading side that was stretched and then folded, with no cut from above. (3) A Moon whose surface was larger by about 1.37 when it sat on the tip (density 1,300 against 3,344 kg m−3^{-3}). (4) A contact region on the tip whose material was still soft while the Moon was peeled away and pulled outward with force, so that it was drawn into ridges; the ridges came down to the Pacific floor with the tip region as the pear was gathered back. (5) A floor that gathered new layers on top while the layers beneath were renewed: the dated surface is the last layer, and the stripes are an older fold beneath it (Part F).

Tests. The requirements of the model, and what would test them, are collected in Paper 13 (The Requirements).

PART A

Introduction: the stripes

This Part introduces the subject of the paper — a set of straight lines on the floor of the North Pacific — and the reading the series gives them. Section A.1 describes what is seen on the maps and measures where each stripe lies; Section A.2 recalls the pear of Paper 3 and states how the stripes are read as its leading side. The observations that support this reading follow in Part B.

A.1What is seen

What the maps show. Across the floor of the North Pacific run long straight lines. They are best seen on maps of the gravity gradient, where they stand out as sharp, narrow lanes. Many of the stripes appear doubled or tripled — a single stripe appears as two or three close lanes side by side (Figure A.1). The gravity-gradient map is used because it responds to sharp contrasts of density over short distances: where a fold brings denser material beside lighter material along a narrow band, the map draws a line. This holds whether the band lies at the surface or beneath younger layers. On the topography of the same floor the stripes are faint (Part F). The stripes run from the coast of North America westward for thousands of kilometres — Clipperton alone for some 7,240 km, more than the radius of the Earth — and they are nearly parallel. All six are parallels of one equator, inclined by at least 10° to today’s equator (Part E).

The geometric analysis on the globe. What “parallels of one equator” means is best seen on a globe. Any great circle — a circle that divides the sphere into two equal halves — can serve as an equator. The circles that keep a constant distance from it, to the north or to the south, are its parallels, as the lines of latitude are the parallels of today’s equator. If that equator is tilted against today’s, its parallels run obliquely across today’s map, but they never meet one another. A set of long lines on a sphere need not behave this way: straight lines drawn independently would in general be parts of different great circles, and any two great circles cross. Six stripes that are all parallels of the same equator, spread over more than 30° from north to south, say something definite: every point of a body turning about an axis moves along a parallel of the equator of that axis, so lines of this kind are the record that one motion about one axis leaves behind. This is why the equator of the stripes is measured with care in this paper (Part E), and why it matters that the base of the pear lies on it (Part B).

A.2The reading of the series

The question this section answers is how a set of lines on the floor of the Pacific is connected with the origin of the Moon. The connection is the pear of Paper 3, recalled here in brief.

The link to the origin of the Moon. In this series the Moon was built from the Earth’s own material, on the tip of a pear that rose from the Earth while it spun fast; the pear was gathered back into the Earth after the Moon was detached (Paper 3). The structure of the pear. The pear was one bulge of the young Earth. The pressure inside it drew it out into a long extension of the Earth’s own material: broad at its base on the Earth’s surface, and narrowing in one smooth curve to its tip, which carried the body that became the Moon. The Moon and the contact on the tip. The Moon sat on the tip over a wide contact: the contact was 6,000 km across at first and narrowed to 3,000 km at release, and the Moon’s upper dome rose some 3,650 km above it.

Gravity and the corotation radius. The pear was part of the Earth and was held by the Earth’s gravity, like any other part of it. It was not thrown off, because its speed was not a throwing speed and it lay inside the corotation radius — about 16,400 km from the Earth’s centre on the equator of that time. This distance marks the limit at which a body turning once a day with the Earth already moves at the speed of a free orbit. Detachment and peeling. When the Moon’s centre reached that distance, the Moon began to separate: it peeled away with little resistance and was released about 210 km beyond the line, when a little more than half of it had crossed, pulled outward with force. Release of the pressure and gathering of the pear. We propose that detaching the Moon exposed the openings of the pear — the cork came out — and the pressure inside it was released; with nothing left to lengthen it, the pear was gathered back into the Earth by the sorting, over tens to hundreds of millions of years.

The place of the base and the deep slow province. The base of the pear stood on the Pacific near Kiritimati (Christmas Island, in the Line Islands), above the Pacific low-velocity province of the lower mantle (LLVP) (Paper 3, §E.4). A low-velocity province is a structure at the bottom of the mantle in which seismic waves travel more slowly than in the surrounding mantle, which shows that its material differs in temperature or composition. There are two such provinces of continental size: one beneath Africa and one beneath the Pacific. We propose that the Pacific one is in part where the heavy material of the pear came to rest as the pear was gathered back — with a light reservation, since the nature of the deep provinces is still open: the Pacific one may in part be core-related material that did not finish sorting.

The backward lean of the pear. The whole pear leaned back: its tip, and the Moon on it, lagged behind the rotation of the Earth. The everyday picture is a child walking outward on a turning roundabout: the farther out she goes from the centre, the faster she must move to keep up with the roundabout, and she feels herself pushed backward, against the direction of the turn. Material carried outward along the pear lagged in the same way, so the tip came to stand behind the base — to the west of it, since the Earth turns toward the east. The two sides of the pear. This gave the pear two sides. Its front, the side facing the direction of rotation — the leading side, on the east — was stretched. Its back, the side facing away from the rotation — the trailing side, on the west — lay beneath the leaning tip.

Absorption and the forming of the wrinkles. As the pear was gathered back, it was absorbed into the Earth; its heavy part went down to the bottom of the mantle beneath the base of the pear, and the absorption left wrinkles on the floor — the stripes on the leading side, and the crumpled arcs of the western Pacific on the back (B.8, E.3). The mass did not leave the Earth; it moved from high outside to deep inside. The folding of the leading side. In this return the leading side of the pear was smeared along the floor in the direction of the rotation: the far end of the wall lagged most and its middle less, so the wall folded back on itself and was laid down as the stripes. This paper reads the stripes of the North Pacific as that leading side.

Figure A.2 sets out the map that the rest of the paper fills in: the base in the middle, the tip to its west, the folded leading side to its east, and the ridge chains on either side of the place of the tip.

The mirror imprint in the floor. If this reading is right, the floor should keep more than the stripes. Where the tip came down, it should show the tip’s outline, about as wide as the contact; and inside that outline it may keep the imprint of the face of the Moon that rested on the tip — the near side, the side that still faces the Earth. Seal and wax. Two faces pressed together leave mirror images of each other, as a seal leaves a reversed print in wax: seen from the Earth, east on the Moon’s near side falls on west on the floor beneath it, while north stays north. The enlargement factor. Because the Moon was then far less dense than today, the imprint should be larger than today’s near side by 1.37 — the factor by which the Moon’s lengths have shrunk since (Part C).

The course of the paper. Part B sets out the observations: the shared equator, the base on it, the stripes running east, the stripes passing beneath the ridges, the deep mantle beneath the base, and the crumpled trailing side. Part C finds the tip from the two bends, tests the mirror imprint of the near side, and follows the stripes of the whole Pacific to the tip in the data (C.5). Part D reads the ridge chains within the tip as ridges drawn up in its peeled part during the peeling of the Moon, as it was pulled outward with force. Part E measures the tilt of the equator of the stripes against today’s equator. Part F takes up the challenges, first among them the young age of the ocean floor; Part G sets out the studies that will test the reading, and Part H concludes.

PART B

The observations

Part A set out the reading of this paper: the long straight stripes of the North Pacific are the leading side of the pear on which the Moon was built, laid down on the floor as the pear was gathered back into the Earth after the release. This Part goes through what is seen on the map, one observation at a time. Each section has the same two steps. Under Observed we state only what the map shows, in the terms of the map — the morphological description. Under The model we say why the reading of the series expects exactly that — the physical explanation. The reader can therefore judge each observation on its own before judging the reading built on it.

B.1 The stripes are parallels of one equator

Observed. Six stripes parallel to one equator, from 33.6° north of it to 1.8° (A.1), and two more south of it (B.4); together they form a belt about one equator. In plain terms: if a globe is tilted until one particular great circle becomes its equator, each stripe then follows a line of constant latitude on the tilted globe, as the parallels on an ordinary map follow lines of constant latitude. The six stripes sit at different distances from that equator, but none of them crosses another, and together they form a band on both sides of it.

The model. This is the direction of the stretching of the leading side of the pear: the stripes are parallel to the motion of the pear’s front relative to the floor, and the equator of the stripe system is the equator of the rotation that stretched them — the Earth’s equator of that time (Part E). The reason is simple. Every point of a spinning body moves along a parallel of its equator. A wall standing on the Earth and carried by its rotation was pulled along these parallels, and when it was laid down, the marks and wrinkles it left run along them too. A set of lines that all share one equator is what a rotation about a single axis leaves behind; lines made by unrelated local causes would have no reason to share one equator.

B.2 The base of the pear lies on the equator of the stripe system

Observed. Kiritimati, the base of the pear in Paper 3, lies on the equator of the stripes, 1.6° from it, next to Clipperton (1.8°). Independence of the data sources. The place of the base was set in Paper 3 from the deep mantle beneath it, before the stripes were considered; the equator of the stripes was fitted to the stripes alone. That the two meet is therefore a finding, not a choice. How often a point chosen at random would fall this close to the equator of the system is the subject of Study 2 (Part G).

The model. The pear rose from the equator of that time; its base is where the stretching was centred, and the stripe system is arranged about it. A body that rises from a spinning planet and is drawn outward by the rotation rises most readily where the outward pull of the rotation is greatest — that is, at the equator. If the stripes record the equator of that time (B.1), the base of the pear should lie on that same line — and it does.

B.3The stripes run east of the base

Observed. The stripes run from the base region eastward to America; west of the base they bend and converge (B.6). One-sidedness. The stripes are clearly one-sided: they begin near the base and do not continue as straight parallels on its western side.

The model. East is the direction of the Earth’s rotation: the leading side. Since the Earth turns from west to east, the side of the pear facing east was the wall pushing ahead into the direction of rotation, and the side facing west was its back.

The trailing side. The trailing side, to the west, was laid down differently — toward the arcs of the western Pacific and Japan, on the back of the pear (B.8). A wall stretched in the direction of rotation should leave its stripes on the eastern side of the base only, and produce marks of a different kind on the western side. Characterising the structures that lie west of the base is the subject of Study 3 (Part G).

B.4Stripes on both sides of the base

Observed. The stripes from Mendocino to Clipperton lie north of the base of the pear. Two more stripes lie south of it, about 6° and 14° south of the equator of the system. These southern stripes are still to be identified in full, and they are probably the stripes known as Galapagos and Marquesas; the names are used here only as names of places on the map.

The model. The front was stretched on both sides of the base; the base lies inside the system, not at its edge. The dimensions of the leading side. The front of the pear was a wide wall, whose base was 9,000–11,000 km wide, and the whole width of that wall faced the direction of rotation. Stripes are therefore expected both to the north and to the south of the base, with the base among them rather than at one end. The northern stripes are better seen and better measured; tracing the two southern stripes in full is part of Study 1.

B.5The stripes pass beneath the ridges

Observed. Where volcanic ridges cross the stripes — near 36°N 178°W, in the Line Islands near 6°N 159°W, near the Phoenix Islands at 2°S 175°W and near 13°S 152°W — the stripe continues beneath the ridge, and the ridge is not cut (Figure B.2). At four separate crossings, in different parts of the system, the stripe meets a volcanic ridge, disappears under it and reappears on the other side, while the ridge above it stands whole.

The model. The stripes were made by folding from the sides and from below, not by gouging from above: a groove cut from above would also cut the ridge it met (Figure B.3). The tablecloth analogy. The difference between the two is easy to picture. Draw a knife across a tablecloth on which a book is lying: the knife meets the book first, and either cuts it or is stopped by it — the line on the cloth is interrupted where the book stands. Now push the tablecloth from its two edges so that it rucks up into a fold: the fold forms in the cloth itself, beneath the book, and the book rides on it whole. A stripe cut from above, by something dragged over the floor, would have to cut every ridge in its path, or end against it. A stripe made by folding the floor itself, from the sides and from below, passes beneath the ridge and leaves it whole. The four crossings show the second.

The order of events and the shortening test. The observation by itself does not fix the order: the ridges may already have stood on the stretched surface while the folding formed beneath them. The test is whether the ridges themselves are shortened where a stripe passes beneath them (Part G). A fold that formed beneath a standing ridge would have squeezed the ground on which the ridge stood, and the ridge should show it, as the book on the rucked tablecloth comes to lie on a narrower strip of cloth. This is the subject of Study 6, and the question is also collected in F.2.

B.6 Continuity and bending at the ridges — first reading

Observed. Continuity without offset. On close views of two crossings (Figures B.4–B.5) each stripe reaches the ridge and continues on the other side from the same point, with no lateral jump that can be seen at this resolution. Change of direction. The direction of the stripe changes where it meets the ridge. West of the ridges the northern stripe turns down (about 14° in the image, and in an arc farther north), while the southern stripe turns up (about 8°). Westward convergence. The stripes converge westward — toward the tip (Part C). Parallels of one equator do not converge: west of the ridges the stripes leave that geometry. In other words, east of the ridges the stripes behave as lines of latitude about one equator (B.1); west of them, the outer stripes turn toward each other — the northern one southward and the southern one northward — as if drawn toward a common region between them.

The model. The convergence points to the region where the tip of the pear came down (Part C). The stripes are the trace of the leading side; where the tip lay, the wall was gathered around it, and so the stripes turn toward it. Tracing the stripes in the data across the whole Pacific shows that the stripes of the north, the centre and the south all lead to the same tip (C.5).

Status. This is a first reading on oblique views with vertical exaggeration (30 and 200); it is to be measured on a flat map without exaggeration (Part G). The view parameters. These views look at the globe at a slant, as from an aircraft window, and multiply all heights — by 30 in one view and by 200 in another — so that low relief on the floor can be seen at all. Angular distortions in the image. Both steps help the eye find a stripe, and both distort the angles read from the image: on a slanted view of the curved globe a straight line can appear bent, and on an exaggerated surface the side of a ridge can make a line seem as if it turns where it only climbs. The angles of 14° and 8° are therefore angles in the image, not on the Earth. Fixing the true direction of each stripe on both sides of a ridge requires reading the same places on a flat map seen from straight above, with heights at their true scale, using the coordinates of the cursor rather than the eye (Study 6).

B.7The deep mantle beneath the base

Observed. Beneath the base of the pear, the lowermost layer of the lower mantle is slow in all five models of the vote maps (Cottaar & Lekic 2016), and the place lies inside the −1%-1\% contour of two further models; the centre of the Pacific low-velocity province lies 1,480–1,660 km to the south-south-west of the base (Paper 3, §E.4).

The terms explained. Seismic waves from earthquakes cross the whole Earth, and from their travel times one builds a map of the speed of the waves at each depth — a “tomographic model”. The meaning of low velocity. Where the waves travel more slowly than the average for that depth, the material is hotter than its surroundings, or of a different composition — denser and richer in iron, for example. Above the core, beneath the Pacific and beneath Africa, lie two large regions of slow material, the large low-velocity provinces (LLVP — large low-velocity province of the lower mantle). The vote maps. Different research groups build different tomographic models, and their maps do not agree in every detail. The vote maps of Cottaar & Lekic (2016) deal with this by counting: for each place at the base of the mantle they check how many of five models call it slow. A place with all five votes is slow in every model. The contour. The −1%-1\% contour is another, stricter way to draw the same regions: it is the line inside which the waves are slower than the average for that depth by at least one per cent. Beneath Kiritimati the vote is unanimous, and two further models also place it inside their −1%-1\% contours. The base of the pear thus stands on the Pacific slow region, a little to the north-north-east of its centre.

The model. After the release the pear was gathered back into the Earth whole, and absorbed into it (E.3); its heavy, iron-rich part went down to the bottom of the mantle, beneath the place where the pear had stood. We propose, with a light reservation, since the nature of the deep provinces is still open, that a dense, iron-rich pile there is part of the Pacific low-velocity province (Paper 3, §E.4). Two traces of one event. The stripes on the floor and the slow region at the base of the mantle are then two complementary traces of the same event: the upper trace marks where the wall was laid down, and the deep trace where the heavy material came to rest.

B.8The trailing side — the back of the pear

Observed. West of the tip, in the belt between the western Pacific and the Indian Ocean, the floor and its margins are crumpled and peeled in every direction: the Philippine and Mariana arcs, the arcs of Indonesia, and the hooked Banda arc north of Australia (Figure B.6). Regional contrast. East of the base the marks are parallel and straight (B.1); west of the tip they are curved, crowded and turned in many directions.

The model. This is the back of the pear — the side that faced away from the direction of rotation. This side crumpled in no order as the pear was gathered back, whereas its leading side was laid down in parallel stripes. The difference between the two sides. The contrast between the two sides follows from what each side went through while the pear stood. The leading side had been stretched for a long time in one direction, the direction of the Earth’s rotation; it carried that one direction into its return, and so it was laid down in lines that all share it. The back had no such single pull. As the pear was gathered back and absorbed, the back came down without a direction of its own and crumpled, as a rug crumples when it is dropped in a heap rather than drawn out flat.

A match with the length of the pear. Measured geodesically from the base (Kiritimati), the arcs lie at the distances the length of the pear reaches: the Banda arc at 8,100 km, the Philippines and Sulawesi at 9,040–9,070 km, and Java at 10,300 km — against a pear whose own body, with its tip region, was 8,890–9,000 km long at release (C.4). C.3The maria on the Earth — a mirror image

The face-to-face contact. The question of this section is whether the face of the tip kept a mark of the Moon that sat on it. In Paper 3 the near side of the Moon — the side that faces the Earth — was the side in contact with the tip, and its maria are openings through which the Earth’s pressure and heat passed from the pear into the Moon (Imbrium the main passage, Serenitatis the secondary). If the floor of the Pacific holds a trace of that contact, it should hold the pattern of the near-side maria, in the right arrangement and at the right size. The arrangement is a mirror image, and the size is larger than the Moon today. We take these two points first.

Why a mirror. The Moon and the tip of the pear stood face to face. Between two bodies facing each other, east on the near side of the Moon falls on west on the Earth: an imprint of the contact must be a mirror image of the near side — north kept, east and west exchanged. A rubber stamp shows why. The letters on a stamp are cut in reverse so that the print on the paper reads the right way: the stamp and the paper face each other, and the print is the mirror image of the stamp. In the same way, press a hand flat on a window and look at it from outside: the thumb that was on the right as you looked at your own palm is now on the left. North and south are not exchanged, because the Moon and the Earth turned about the same axis and shared the same “up”; only east and west are exchanged.

The first reading. On the gravity-gradient map the author first marked, by eye, three circular features as Imbrium, Serenitatis and Nectaris (Figure C.1). Their Imbrium–Serenitatis distance, 1,420 km, matched the lunar distance enlarged by the contraction (below), but the three points formed a direct image, not a mirror image; Nectaris then fell 574 km from its place in a direct copy and 2,500 km from its place in a mirror image. Replacing the reading. A direct imprint cannot come from two faces standing in contact, and so the reading was replaced. We keep this step in the paper because it is part of the investigation: the first marks were made by eye, before the geometry of the contact was worked out, and it was the requirement of a mirror that showed they could not all be right. The corotation line. The length of the pear is set by the corotation line. C.5 The stripes of the whole Pacific lead to the tip of the pear

Extending to the whole ocean. Sections C.1–C.4 found the tip from the stripes of the North Pacific: their two bends, west of the ridges, mark its edges. But the stripes of the North Pacific are only part of the lines on the floor of the ocean. The central system and the southern system. Farther south a central line runs west from South America, continues along Sala y Gomez and Easter Island, and turns north-west along the Tuamotu; and still farther south long lines run north-west from the far south-east of the ocean. A shared routing. The author’s insight is that all these lines, followed from America westward, are routed to the tip: the southern lines bend north near Tonga–Kermadec, and the central line turns north-west toward the same region (Figure C.4). If the stripes are the trace of the pear, the lines of the north, the centre and the south should all lead to the one place where the tip came down. This section tests that on a global grid of the depth of the ocean floor.

The phenomenon. The northern stripes converge west of the ridges toward the tip (B.6, C.1); the author’s drawing carries the same convergence to the lines of the central system and the southern system (Figure C.4). The gap in knowledge. The convergence has so far been read only on views of the globe, and only for the northern stripes; it has not been traced in the data for the three systems together, toward a target fixed in advance. The research question: traced in the data from their American ends westward, do the stripes of the north (Mendocino, Murray, Molokai, Clarion, Clipperton), the centre (Sala y Gomez–Easter–Tuamotu–Line Islands) and the south (the Eltanin system with the Louisville chain; the Tuvalu–Gilbert–Ratak chain) lead to the tip of the pear?

Data sources. The lines used here have long been mapped on charts of the ocean floor, and are named after nearby places and features; as in the rest of this paper, the names are used only as names of places on the map. The data are the GEBCO 2026 grid (GEBCO Compilation Group 2026), the unified global compilation of the depth of the ocean floor, issued each year from ship soundings and satellite gravity data. Hillshading. When the grid is lit from one side — a “hillshade” — the lines on the floor appear as distinct bands of light and shadow, much as a low sun brings out the furrows of a ploughed field. What is new here: the lines of the three systems are examined together, by one rule written down and fixed before the tracing, toward two targets taken from this paper.

The research hypothesis. Followed westward, the stripes of the north, the centre and the south lead to the tip of the pear: their westward continuations pass within the final contact, 3,000 km across, or at least within the whole tip — the 6,000-km contact; and lines chosen without regard to the tip do not reach the final contact.

The tracing protocol. Each line is followed from its American end westward in steps of 0.5°. The leading side: the leading side of the pear stood across the whole width of its base, 9,000–11,000 km, north and south of Kiritimati (B.4); as the pear was gathered back, the wall was gathered around the tip (B.6), and its lines were laid on the floor converging on the place where the tip came down. The directions of convergence: the southern stripes run north-west, bend north near Tonga–Kermadec and continue toward the tip; the central line turns north-west along the Tuamotu and the Line Islands; the northern stripes run west toward it from America. The chains at the end of the paths: the central and the southern paths end on the two chains that Part D reads as the spatula ridges within the tip — the Line Islands in the east and the Tuvalu–Gilbert–Ratak chain in the west.

PART D

The spatula ridges

Parts B and C read the stripes and the place of the tip of the pear. This part turns to the large chains of volcanic ridges that stand within that place, in the peeled part of the tip. The question it answers is whether these chains can be read as a mark left in the contact region of the tip of the pear while the Moon was peeled away and pulled outward with force, and which observations already bear on that reading.

D.1The ridges on either side

Geographic layout. West and east of the maria imprints (C.3) stand the large ridge chains of the central Pacific: the Marshall–Gilbert–Tuvalu chains in the west (with the Mid-Pacific Mountains and Wake farther north-west) and the Line Islands in the east, with Palmyra and Kiritimati. Definition in the model. We read them as the spatula ridges: ridges drawn up from above in the contact region at the tip of the pear, on which the Moon sat and from which it was peeled and pulled off with force (Paper 3). They lie within the tip of the pear itself, in its peeled part. The narrowing of the contact: the contact narrowed from 6,000 km to the 3,000 km on which the Moon sat in its last stage (Paper 3), and the narrowing was a peeling: the band of the contact that was peeled between the 6,000 and the 3,000 km is itself the tip of the pear, and the ridges were drawn up in that band. The state of the material: the material of the contact was still soft; as the Moon was pulled away, this material was drawn up between the Moon and the tip, as the plaster between a spatula and a wall is drawn into ridges when the spatula is pulled off while the plaster is still soft (Figure D.1). The ridges stayed on the face of the tip, in its peeled band. As the pear was gathered back, the tip region came down to the floor of the Pacific, west of the base (C.2), and the ridges came down with it.

The plasterer’s image. The everyday picture is that of a plasterer. Press a flat spatula onto a wall covered with fresh, soft plaster and pull it straight off: the plaster clings to both the spatula and the wall, is stretched between them as the gap opens, and is left standing in raised ridges — highest along the edges of the spatula, where the plaster is the last to let go. Nothing pushed these ridges up from inside the wall; they were pulled up from the outside, as the spatula was drawn away. The parallel in the model: the tool is the Moon; the wall is the tip of the pear over its contact, narrowing from 6,000 to 3,000 km; and the soft plaster is the material of the contact, which was still soft — it flowed slowly, like thick honey, rather than breaking — when the Moon was peeled and pulled off. Release with force: the pull was not gentle: the Moon was released only when a little more than half of it had crossed the corotation line, pulled outward with force (A.2), and it is this pull that drew the ridges up. A contact of this kind is one of the requirements of the paper (requirement 4 in the box at the start).

The Hawaiian–Emperor chain. What happened with the Hawaiian–Emperor chain is not yet known. We propose, tentatively, that it too was part of the spatula; no requirement of the paper rests on it (D.3, F.3).

D.2Their spacing

The spacing constraint. If the chains were drawn up in the contact region of the tip, the span between them should be about the width of the contact. The measurements: the two chains are 2,800–3,500 km apart (Majuro to Kiritimati 3,490 km; Tarawa to Palmyra 2,810 km; middle to middle 3,290 km). The match with the width of the tip and the contact: these values are the width of the tip (3,360 km, as measured between the two bends of the stripes in C.1) and the width of the contact region (3,000 km, the width of the surface on which the Moon sat at release, Paper 3). The spread of 2,800–3,500 km comes from measuring between different pairs of islands, and all of it lies within the range of the tip width found in C.1 (3,040–3,700 km).

Asymmetry and the shift toward the base. The two chains do not sit symmetrically about the footprint of the tip found from the bends (C.1). The western chain passes through its centre (Kwajalein, in the Marshall Islands, lies 180 km from it); the eastern chain, the Line Islands, runs farther east and carries Kiritimati, at the centre of the base of the pear. Containment within the width of the base: this places the chains within the base, not apart from it: the base of the pear was 9,000–11,000 km wide (Paper 3), so the whole 3,000-km tip region, which came down about 3,760 km west of the centre of the base (C.2), lies within the width of the base, and so do both chains. The mechanism of the shift: the pair of chains has the width of the contact region, and it is shifted about half that width east of the footprint of the bends. The shift follows directly from where the ridges were drawn. The footprint of the bends marks the final 3,000-km contact, on which the Moon sat at the moment of release; the spatula ridges, by contrast, lie within the wider tip of the pear, in its peeled part — the band that was peeled as the contact narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km (D.1) — and so partly beyond the final contact, toward the base. For this reason the chains are shifted from the footprint of the bends toward the base, by about half the width of the contact. Two independent measurements: the bends of the stripes on the one hand and the two chains on the other thus give two independent measurements of one width, the width of the contact region.

D.3Their ages

The ages of the volcanic material along a chain give a second test, and to read it two terms need to be defined.

A plume track. A plume is a column of hot material rising from deep in the Earth to a fixed spot beneath the floor. If the floor moves slowly over that spot, the plume erupts through it again and again at new places and leaves a line of volcanoes — much as a candle held still beneath a sheet of paper that is drawn slowly across it leaves a line of scorch marks. The newest volcano stands over the plume; each older one has been carried farther away from it.

An age progression. A plume track therefore shows a steady change of age along its length: the farther a volcano lies from the active end, the older it is, in an orderly sequence. This steady change is called an age progression.

The age pattern expected in the spatula ridges. The spatula ridges were not drawn up in one moment: they were drawn during the peeling of the Moon, over its whole duration — millions of years (Paper 3). Their ages should therefore be spread over the period of the peeling, not concentrated in one instant. No plume pattern: nor should they follow the steady progression of a plume track: nothing in this picture is a fixed source beneath a moving floor, leaving volcanoes that grow older step by step along the chain. A chain drawn up in this way may show a few separate episodes, each found at once over great distances, or a long span of ages with no simple order along it, but not a clean sequence from one end to the other. The criterion of comparison: what is compared here is not the value of the ages but their pattern along the chain. The published ages are given in the table below. Ages are in Ma, mega-annum: millions of years before the present (81 Ma means 81 million years ago).

D.4The comparison to come

The ages are one test. To show that the chains of the Pacific were drawn up from above and not pushed up from below, they will be compared in a controlled way with volcanic ridges outside the Pacific that the scientific literature reads as built from below. The reference ridges: the Ninetyeast Ridge (Kerguelen; a single continuous rise 5,000 km long, with ages of 77 Ma in the north to 43 Ma in the south), the Walvis Ridge (Tristan–Gough) and the Chagos–Laccadive Ridge (Réunion). The five measures of comparison: ages, morphological shape, flexural moat, crustal root and orientation (Part G). These three ridges serve as the point of reference: if the Pacific chains are built differently, the difference should show in the same five measures.

Two of these measures need a definition.

Flexural moat. A large volcanic ridge is a heavy load standing on the floor. The outer shell of the Earth bends under such a load, as a board floating on water sinks where a weight is placed on it. Around the ridge the floor is pressed down into a trough, which slowly fills with sediment: this trough is the flexural moat. Its depth and width tell how heavy the load is and how stiff the shell was when the load was placed on it. The floor around Hawaii is the classic measured case (Watts & ten Brink 1989).

Crustal root. Beneath many large ridges the crust is thicker than in the surrounding floor: the ridge has a keel reaching downward, as an iceberg has most of its body below the water line. This thickened crust beneath the ridge is defined as its root. A ridge built from below, by melt rising through the shell, is expected to sit on such a root.

The status of the test. The comparison is meant to establish whether the Pacific chains have the flexural moats and roots typical of ridges built from below, or whether they differ from them in the way a ridge drawn up from the surface would. The values are to be measured in Study 7; the comparison itself has not yet been made.

What we now have. Two chains of ridges spaced as wide as the contact region, one passing through the centre of the tip and the other past Kiritimati; both lie within the width of the base, and are displaced about half a width from the footprint of the bends toward the base — within the tip itself, in the band that was peeled as the contact narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km; two of the three chains do not show the age progression of a plume track; and a set of measures by which a ridge drawn up from above can be told from one built from below.

PART E

The equator of then

Parts A and B used the fact that the stripes are parallels of one equator. This part shows how that equator is found, how far it is tilted to today’s equator, and what the tilt means: the stripes record the equator of the Earth at the time the pear was gathered back, which need not be today’s.

E.1 The stripes are tilted to today’s equator

The geometry of the tilt. Every stripe measured rises systematically northward as one moves along it toward the east, the southern stripe included: the stripes are not parallel to today’s equator. If they were, each stripe would keep one latitude along its whole length; instead, each of them lies farther north at its eastern end than at its western end.

How the angles were measured on the globe. The angle was measured directly on the globe, between points the author marked along each stripe. For a stripe with two points the measurement is simple: the rise is the difference in latitude between the eastern and the western point; the run is the east–west distance between them — the difference in longitude, shortened by the cosine of the latitude, because the meridians draw together toward the pole; and the angle is the slope of the rise over the run. For example, the two Murray points lie 4.04° apart in latitude and 20.95° apart in longitude; at about 31° of latitude that is a run of about 18 degrees of arc, and the slope is 12.7°. For the stripes on which more than two points were marked (Clarion, Clipperton), the angle is that of the straight line fitted through all the points.

E.2One equator, inclined by at least 10°

The geometry of a tilted equator. Tilted lines of this kind are exactly what the parallels of a tilted equator look like on today’s map. An equator inclined to today’s equator crosses it at two opposite points; a line parallel to it rises above today’s latitudes on one side of the crossing and falls below them on the other side. The change of slope: near the crossing such a line rises at about the angle of the tilt; farther from it, the rise gradually becomes gentler, and a quarter of the way round the globe the line runs level for a while before turning down.

Fixing the lower limit. Fitted together, the stripes are parallels of one equator inclined to today’s equator by at least 10°; below that threshold the fit fails. The reason is the steepness of the stripes that lie close to the equator of the system: a line near a tilted equator rises at most at about the angle of the tilt, so an equator inclined by less than 10° cannot produce stripes such as Clipperton (+11.0°) and the southern stripe (+11.7°).

The limits set by the length of the data. Above that threshold, inclinations up to about 20° fit almost equally well, because the points span a sector of only some 55° of longitude — about one seventh of the way round the globe. Geometric likeness over short arcs: over so short a piece, a gently tilted equator seen close to its crossing and a more steeply tilted equator seen farther from its crossing draw nearly the same lines; telling them apart requires following the stripes over a longer arc. This is why the measurements fix a lower limit but not the value above it. The effect of the western bend points: the exact inclination depends on whether the westernmost points, which lie beyond the ridges where the stripes bend (B.6), still belong to the straight stripes: west of the ridges the stripes converge, and parallels of one equator do not converge, so including those points or leaving them out changes the fit.

The base on the common equator. At 10°–14° the base of the pear lies on the equator of the system (the fit of 11° places the crossing with today’s equator at 159°W, 1.5° of longitude from the base, and the base 1.6° from the equator of the system). This finding ties the stripes to the pear: the pear rose from the equator of its time (B.2), and its base sits on the equator that the stripes define. The exact inclination is set as Study 1, which will refit the equator on traces of the full stripes and give its errors.

E.3 The equator at the time the pear was gathered back

When the wrinkles were made. Why should the equator of the stripes differ from today’s? The difference lies in when the stripes were made. The stripes were not laid on the floor while the pear stood, but as it was gathered back, after the release. Until then a mass of seven to twelve lunar masses stood high above the surface on one side of the Earth, near the equator. When the Moon was detached and the pressure inside the pear was released, the pear was gathered back into the Earth and absorbed into it — its heavy part down to the base of the mantle beneath the base (B.7) — and the absorption left the wrinkles: the stripes on the leading side, and the crumpled arcs on the back (B.8).

The change in the distribution of mass. The mass did not leave the Earth; it moved from high outside to deep inside, and the distribution of mass about the axis of rotation changed with it. A spinning body is balanced when its mass is arranged in a certain way about its axis; the quantity that measures this arrangement is the moment of inertia, which grows with the amount of mass and with its distance from the axis. Moving a large mass from far outside the surface to deep within the body changes that arrangement, and the balance with it. The ball and the putty: the everyday picture is a spinning ball with a heavy lump of putty stuck high on one side of it: the ball spins steadily as long as the lump is on its equator. Press the lump deep into the ball and that balance is lost; the ball slowly rolls over, relative to its spin, until it finds a new balance. In the same way, the Earth could then begin to turn toward a new orientation.

The record of the turn of the axis. The stripes therefore record the equator at the time the pear was gathered back, and perhaps also the beginning of that turn. An inclination of at least 10° between the equator of the stripes and today’s equator is what such a turn would leave behind. The series does not enter the study of the axes — how far the Earth turned, how fast, and about which line; the question is left for future work.

PART F

Challenges

A specialist reader will raise three objections to the reading of Parts A–E. This part states each of them and what the model answers and requires. The first concerns the age of the floor on which the stripes lie, the second the order of stripes and ridges, and the third the ages of the Hawaiian chain.

F.1The age of the sea floor

The geochronological objection. The floor between the stripes is dated, by its magnetic anomalies and by its basalts, to at most about 180 million years — far younger than the detachment of the Moon. (Magnetic anomalies are bands of the floor magnetised in one direction or the other, recording the Earth’s field at the time the material cooled; basalts are the dark volcanic material of which the floor is made, dated by their radioactive clocks.) By this objection, a stripe made when the Moon left cannot lie in a floor that formed long after.

The answer in the model. In the model the floor shows a young age because it has gathered layers: a new layer is laid on top while the layer beneath is taken back and renewed from below. Surface age apart from structure: what is actually dated is the material that now lies on the stripes — the last layer — and not the stripes themselves. A floor renewed in this way cannot be dated as a whole, and the age of its present surface is not the age of the stripes.

Snow and furrows. The everyday picture is snow falling on a ploughed field. The snow fell last night, while the furrows were ploughed months ago. Under a light snowfall the furrows still show through as long, faint lines, because the snow lies over the old relief and follows its shape. Dating the snow gives the date of the snowfall, not of the ploughing. In the same way, a layer laid over an older fold takes on the shape of the fold beneath it, and dating the layer gives the age of the layer itself, not of the fold beneath it. Buried remnants: the stripes themselves may be billions of years old — worn and partly erased over that time, and seen today as remnants beneath the new material.

What the maps show. On the topography of the floor the stripes appear faint; on the gravity gradient, by contrast, they stand out sharply (Figures A.1, B.4, B.5). The two maps measure different things. Topography draws the shape of the surface only: where new layers have smoothed and covered an older relief, the surface shows it only faintly. The gravity of a place depends on all the mass beneath it, and the gravity gradient — the rate at which gravity changes from point to point — picks out sharp changes of density at depth as well as at the surface. Density contrasts below the surface: a fold of denser material lying beneath lighter, younger layers changes the gravity above it even where it hardly changes the shape of the surface. Gravity sees the density contrasts beneath the surface, while topography is limited to the surface alone: the stripes are clearer where the map sees below the new material, as remnants buried beneath it would be. The global gravity model of the floor was itself described in the literature as revealing “buried tectonic structure” (Sandwell et al. 2014).

What this requires. The stripes must be a structure of the layer lying beneath the dated surface — that is, a fold that the younger layers have draped rather than created. The test of confirmation: where a stripe is crossed by drilling or by seismic profiles (sections of the floor made by sending sound waves down and recording their echoes from the layers below), the fold should continue below the youngest layers. The thickness of the layers: the layers above it should be thinner over its crests and thicker in its troughs — as the structure of a layer laid down over an old relief. This requirement is tested by Study 9.

F.2The order of stripes and ridges

The objection. If the stripes pass beneath the ridges (B.5), the stripes might seem to be older than the ridges, or the ridges older than the stripes; the observation must be read with care. That the stripes pass beneath the ridges does not by itself fix the order of events: the ridges may already have stood on the surface while the folding formed beneath them. A fold made from the sides and from below (Figure B.3) can pass under a ridge that already stands, much as a rug can be wrinkled beneath a table standing on it.

The test. The criterion of the test is whether the ridges are shortened at the point where the stripes pass beneath them. If a fold formed under a standing ridge, the ground under the ridge was drawn together, and the ridge above it should show it — shortened, crowded or bent where the fold passes. If the ridge shows no damage where a fold must have passed beneath it, the ridge was built later. This question is the subject of Study 6, and it is to be measured on a flat map without vertical exaggeration.

F.3The Hawaiian age progression

The objection. The Hawaiian–Emperor chain has a clean age progression that fits a plume track (D.3), from 0 Ma at Hawaii to about 81 Ma at the far end of the Emperor chain, and the spatula ridges are not expected to show such an ordered progression.

The answer. The reading of this paper does not rest on the Hawaiian–Emperor chain, and the paper sets no requirement on it. What happened in it is not yet known; we propose, tentatively, that it too was part of the spatula. The pillars of the reading: the reading rests on the Line and Marshall chains, whose ages show no plume progression (D.3). The Hawaiian–Emperor chain is set beside them, and beside the ridges built from below, in Study 7.

The stripe system and the place where the Moon was formed. The stripes of the North Pacific are ordered about the base of the pear: the base lies on their equator, the stripes run east of it, and west of it they bend and converge toward a region of the width of the contact region on which the Moon sat. Traced in the data, the stripes of the centre and the south of the Pacific also lead to the same region. Inside this region of convergence lies the mirror imprint of the near side, at the scale of a Moon not yet contracted. The ridge chains are spaced as wide as the contact region, and they lie within the tip itself, in the band that was peeled as the contact narrowed — partly beyond the final contact, toward the base. We read the stripes as the leading side of the pear, folded as the pear was gathered back, and the region of the bends as the place where the Moon was formed.

In one picture. A pear of the Earth’s own material stood on the Pacific. Its base lay near Kiritimati, and its tip about 3,760 km to the west of it, lagging behind the Earth’s rotation, with the Moon on that tip. The front of the pear, the leading side that faced the direction of rotation, was stretched. When the Moon was detached and the pressure was released, the pear was gathered back into the Earth. The leading side was smeared along the floor eastward toward America, in the direction of the rotation, and was laid down as the stripes — 9,360 km of them, against a pear body of 8,890–9,000 km. The back of the pear crumpled into the island arcs of the western Pacific. The Moon was peeled from the contact as the contact narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km, and was pulled off it with force; during the peeling it drew up the soft material of the contact and raised the ridge chains within the tip itself, in the peeled band, 2,800–3,500 km apart, and they came down to the floor together with the tip as the pear was gathered back. And the face of the Moon left its mirror image on the floor at the scale 1.37. The stripes record the equator of then, tilted by at least 10° to today’s equator. The study protocol of Part G will test this.
