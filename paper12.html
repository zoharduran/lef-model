The Rings

A ring breaks, grinds and returns: the source of the material, its dynamics, and the marks it leaves on moons and on the parent planet

Zohar Duran · Independent Researcher, Ramla, Israel · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 10 November 2026

Abstract

Paper 11 established that a ring does not build moons. This paper develops that principle into a full mechanism for rings. It covers where the material comes from, what keeps a ring alive, how the material moves, what it does to the moons that pass through it, and what traces it leaves on the parent planet. 1. All ring material comes from the parent, in two ways. It comes either directly or through moons: • Directly: material ejected from the ground (lava, water, vapour) that did not cross the corotation line. It orbits in the ring, is ground and disperses, and does not become a moon. • Through moons: moons that came out of the parent and go on releasing material. Saturn's rings – 95%–99% water ice, 1.54×10¹⁹ kg in a thin equatorial disc – are not space dust. A captured moon is not part of the rule. 2. A ring lives as long as it is fed. At Saturn a rain of 432–45,000 kg s⁻¹ from the rings into the planet has been measured. Without feeding the rings would vanish within 11 million to 1.1 billion years. They are therefore a steady flow fed from within, at a cost of less than one part in a hundred thousand of Saturn's mass. On Mars the feed stopped and the moons are dry, so the ring vanished. A ring with no moon and no feed is expected to vanish: so the moonless narrow rings of Uranus, and Neptune's Galle and Lassell rings. 3. Two conditions for a massive ring. First, the corotation line must lie before the Roche limit. When the two are compared the density cancels, so the condition holds, for any planet, when the day is shorter than about 13.4 hours. Second, the ground must leak. • Only Saturn today and young Mars meet both. • Jupiter is fast, but its thick cap hardly leaks. • Uranus and Neptune are slow and have frozen. The rings of the giant planets lie before the line or around it. Those of the small bodies lie above it, near the 1:3 resonance. 4. The angular momentum of Uranus and Neptune left with the water. Their lines moved outward. The moons account for only 1% and 0.002% of the momentum lost, and the water that left weighed 270 times all of Uranus's moons; it was therefore expelled into space. The far planets lost water because their caps are thin and their escape speeds low, not because of the cold. 5. A ring marks the moon. An ice ring adds material, as in the equatorial ridges of Pan, Atlas and Daphnis. A silicate ring grinds: Deimos stayed long in the heart of the Martian ring and was ground all over, while Phobos left through its lower edge and was ground only slightly. No ring has been found around any moon. Requirements, predictions and refutation criteria are collected in Part L.
