The Rings

A ring breaks, grinds and returns: the source of the material, its dynamics, and the marks it leaves on moons and on the parent planet

Abstract

The research series established that a planetary ring is not a place of accretion where moons are built, but a mechanism of grinding and disintegration [Paper 11]. This paper extends that principle into a unified physical and dynamical model of ring systems. It sets out the source of the material, the conditions for a ring to survive, the mechanisms of mass movement, and the morphological marks on the moons and on the parent planet.

1. All ring material comes from the parent body, by two mechanical routes: direct feeding — lava, water and vapour from the surface that did not cross the corotation line, trapped in orbit, ground and dispersed without condensing into a moon; and secondary feeding — material ejected by moons born from the parent (captured moons are excluded). Saturn’s rings, 95%–99% water ice with a total mass of 1.54×10¹⁹ kg in a thin equatorial disc, come from inside the planet and are not captured interplanetary dust.

2. A ring persists as long as it is fed. At Saturn a ring rain of 432–45,000 kg s⁻¹ into the planet has been measured; without feeding the rings would vanish within 11 million to 1.1 billion years. The ring is therefore a steady flow fed from within, at a cumulative cost of up to about one hundred-thousandth (1.1×10⁻⁵) of Saturn’s mass. On Mars the hydrodynamic pump stopped and the moons dried, and the ring vanished. A ring with neither direct nor moon feeding — the moonless narrow rings of Uranus, Neptune’s Galle and Lassell rings — is destined to fade.

3. A massive ring needs two conditions together. The corotation line must lie inside the Roche limit; comparing the two equations cancels the density and gives one critical day for all bodies, about 13.4 hours. And material must actively leak from the surface or envelope. Only Saturn today and early Mars meet both. Jupiter meets the speed condition but its thick gas envelope blocks the water; Uranus and Neptune are too slow and frozen. Giant-planet rings lie inside or around the line; small-body rings lie above it, near the 1:3 resonance.

4. On Uranus and Neptune the line moved outward. Today’s moons account for only 1% and 0.002% of the angular momentum lost. The water that escaped through the line weighed 270 times all of Uranus’s moons and was expelled into space — because of the thin atmospheric cap and low escape speed of the ice giants, not because of temperature.

5. An ice ring adds mass (the equatorial ridges of Pan, Atlas and Daphnis); a silicate ring grinds (Deimos stayed long in the heart of the Martian ring and was ground, while Phobos crossed its lower edge and was ground only in part). No ring has been found around any moon. Requirements, predictions and refutation criteria are collected in Part L.
