01 · THE MYSTERY

The mystery: where did 4.4–5.0 × 10¹⁹ kg of external metal disappear to — and why is the crater interior uniform?

In the previous chapter we saw that material fired from the feeding openings of the parent body and returning to the shell can produce shock pressures of tens of gigapascals, and that the morphology of the craters follows the asymmetry between the leading and trailing hemispheres. Now we put the model to the sharpest chemical test — a test that separates it from the asteroid theory at the level of the atom and the isotope:

  1. The foreign mass missing from the Moon's crust: chondritic asteroids that never separated a core are very rich in siderophile ("iron-loving") elements — iridium, osmium, platinum and ruthenium — while the Moon's crust is almost completely poor in them. If asteroids dug the 1.32 million craters seen on the Moon today, where is the foreign metal they brought with them?
  2. The crater interior — a hole dug through layers, or a cup filled with returning material? If a crater is a pit violently dug into a layered crust, its central peak should lift deep rocks that differ in composition from the walls, the rim and the surroundings — and in addition, a hard head-on impact on the leading hemisphere (90°W) should expose deeper layers than a soft impact on the trailing hemisphere (90°E). Is this really what is measured over thousands of craters?
  3. The riddle of the missing craters above the hidden domes (Study 3): in Chapter 8 we discovered 44 buried feeding domes (36 of them on the far side). If every dome fired craters around itself while it was active, why is there no unusual crater density around them today?
02 · AN IMAGE

An image: drops of ink in a glass of clear water, and a hot wax seal

Two simple images make clear why the chemistry of the crater interior and of the Moon's crust decides in favour of a returning Earth source:

  • Drops of ink in a glass of clear water (the siderophile elements and the ruthenium isotopes): the Moon's crust is like a glass of clear water almost free of iron-loving metals, while a chondritic asteroid is like dark ink. Even a small addition of asteroidal material would at once colour the upper crust — and in addition, the ruthenium atoms of asteroids have an isotopic "accent" different from the Earth's. In fact, there is almost no foreign metal in the crust, and the ruthenium isotopes measured in the impact-melt rocks of the craters are those of the Earth itself!
  • A hot wax seal pressed onto paper (the uniformity of the crater interior): a crater in the LEF model is not an excavation that brings up foreign layers from below, but a lump of hot returning material that landed and was smeared from edge to edge — so from the centre, through the floor and up to the rim, one uniform chemical composition prevails, identical to the composition of the nearby surroundings into which the material was fired from the same mare!
03 · SCIENCE AND MODEL

Science and model: the uniform crater interior (7,227 craters) and "the foreign mass that is not there" (1.32 million craters)

Parts D and L of Paper 06 present four large-scale quantitative studies that close the chemical and structural proof:

  1. The buried craters of the far side and the melted craters in the maria (Studies 3, 4 and 14):
    • Around the 44 buried domes (Study 3): the crater density above the buried domes is equal to or lower than at a distance of 3–5 radii (0.93 at 2–10 km, 1.00 at 10–50 km, 1.02 at 50–100 km) — because the craters fired by the ancient openings of the far side were buried together with them beneath the layers of sial and returning material that built the thick crust of the far side!
    • The mass without a rim beneath the melted craters in the maria (Studies 4 and 14): on the mare floors, 62% (49 of 79) of the craters 10 km or more across are melted into rings, and only one sharp crater 20+ km across exists on all 1.18 million km² of floors (0.8 per million km² against 207 in the highlands!). Beneath 16 melted craters in the maria (Study 14), the base of the crust is raised by +0.8 km above the floor of the mare itself (in 13 of 16, p = 0.04), but with no sharp rim at all in the Bouguer gravity (edge steepness 1.04 against 1.00) — because the returning material sank and mixed into the fluid magma of the mare rather than filling a solid crater from outside!
  2. The crater interior is one uniform material (Study 7, §L.4): examining 7,227 craters 10–60 km across in six radial rings (centre 0–0.3R, floor 0.3–0.7R, inner wall 0.7–0.95R, rim 0.95–1.1R, ejecta 1.2–2R, surroundings 3–4R) shows that the difference in iron and titanium between the centre and the inner wall is only +0.08 wt% FeO and +0.060 wt% TiO2+0.060\text{ wt\% TiO}_2 (8–10 times smaller than the calibration scatter of the maps, 0.81 and 0.43 wt%!), and the difference between the floor and the surroundings is 0.00 wt% FeO and 0.000 wt% TiO20.000\text{ wt\% TiO}_2. Moreover, in a paired comparison of 1,214–1,235 pairs of identical craters between the leading hemisphere (90°W) and the trailing hemisphere (90°E), the chemical difference is absolutely zero (0.00 wt%) — a hard head-on impact changed the shape, but exposed no foreign layers!
  3. The foreign mass that is not there and the terrestrial ruthenium isotopes (Study 8, §L.5): calculating the mass needed to create all 1,318,387 craters seen on the Moon (1 km and more, at 18 km/s) gives 4.4–5.0 × 10¹⁹ kg of impactor mass (68% for the SPA basin alone, 30% for the 32 other large basins, 2% for the 6,940 craters of 20–300 km, and 0.03% for the 1.31 million craters of 1–20 km). Had it been left in the upper crust (5 km) at the accepted retention rate (20%–60%), it would make up 2%–6% of the layer (0.9–3.0 × 10¹⁹ kg) — while all the siderophile elements in the whole lunar crust add up to only 0.9%–2% (0.45–1.0 × 10¹⁹ kg), that is, 6.7 times less than required (and 14–18 times less than the total bombardment of 3.7 × 10²⁰ kg)! And in the two samples in which ruthenium isotopes were measured in the impact-melt rocks of the craters, they are identical to the Earth's silicate!
An answer to the claim of the single meteorite grains in Chang'e-5 and Chang'e-6 (§L.5)

The discovery of a single chondrule grain in the Chang'e-5 samples or of microscopic meteorite remnants in Chang'e-6 does not contradict the model: every airless body in the Solar System collects a tiny amount of micrometeoritic space dust — and this is exactly the amount the model allows ("space dust on the side"), unlike the tens of billions of tonnes of foreign metal that would have been needed to dig the Moon's basins!

04 · THE MECHANISM IN WORDS

The mechanism in words: the Earth as "the last scatterer", the captured craters and the ring test

Parts L.6–M.1 of Paper 06 complete the chronological history of the craters from the moment the Moon detached from the Earth (3.873 Ga) to the final freezing of the surface:

  1. The Earth kept firing light material at the Moon after the detachment (Study 12, §L.6): at the moment of detachment the internal pressure in the Moon stopped and its feeding openings sealed; but the Earth itself did not fall silent — the pear slowly drew back inward (over tens to hundreds of millions of years), and its openings kept firing material into space. Since the heavy material sank back down into the Earth and the light material floated up, the material that managed to reach 8.8–10.75 km/s and get to the Moon was the light, thorium-rich (and iron-poor) part of the Earth's ejecta. Indeed, in all 3,478 craters on the near-side basalt, the ejecta around the craters is poorer in iron than the basalt around them (by −0.20 wt% on the mare floors and up to −3.8 wt% in large craters, and by −0.36 wt% on the plains; and the Apollo 12 samples linked to the rays of Copernicus carry 14.0–15.7 wt% FeO against 20.5 wt% in the local basalt, and 7.6–9.4 ppm thorium against 0.83 ppm).
  2. The near side kept more of the last small craters — the Earth is "the last scatterer" (Study 9, §L.7): since the Earth always stood in the sky of the near side (0°), the last material fired from it landed on the highlands of both sides but struck the near side at a higher rate. Counting the fresh craters 1–2 km across on the highlands alone (after removing all the maria, the SPA basin and the secondary fields of 16 young ray craters) shows an excess of +19% on the near side (14,103 against 11,814 per million km²; confidence interval 1.12–1.27, and more than 600 km from any mare even 1.51 times!), while in the larger, older craters the near side is much poorer (0.86 at 2–5 km, 0.63 at 5–10 km and 0.53 at 10–20 km), because it was erased by the heat of the contact and the maria!
  3. The captured ejecta on the maria and a Moon that never passed through a ring (§L.8, §M.1): the few young craters on the frozen mare floors are captured ejecta fired from the Earth near the escape ceiling (10.748 km/s) and captured by the Moon's gravity at a speed far lower than that of an asteroid. In addition, unlike Deimos of Mars (which spent a long time in a dense ring, so its craters are flat and smooth — median depth 1.9% against 5.5% on Phobos), the Moon never passed through a ring of ice and dust: the pear swept the zone out to the corotation line and the heat prevented ice from condensing, so the Moon kept highlands saturated with sharp craters from the start.

06 · THE SIGNATURE

The signature in reality: a prediction for the MMX mission at Phobos and a summary of the 14 studies

Paper 06 extends the mechanism beyond the Moon to any moon built from its parent planet and exposed to its ejecta (§O.1):

  • The direct prediction for the MMX mission (Martian Moons eXploration) at Phobos: since Phobos and Deimos were fired from Mars (the subject of Paper 10), the model predicts that the crater rocks on Phobos returned by the MMX mission will have the composition and isotopes of Mars itself — with no excess of external metal beyond micrometeoritic space dust!
  • The balance of the 14 studies of Paper 06 (§O.2): of the 14 numbered studies in the paper, 11 received a full green light and 3 received an amber light (partial confirmation with a limit of resolution or a small sample, and zero studies with a red light).

07 · NEXT

The door ahead

In Chapters 11–12 we completed the investigation of the Moon's craters (Craters Without Asteroids): we saw that the craters were dug by returning Earth material fired from the feeding openings at 2–8 km/s, which struck with a force of 13–46 GPa, created an excess of craters on the trailing hemisphere and hard head-on impacts on the leading hemisphere, filled every crater with a uniform composition, and left in the crust exactly the ruthenium isotopes of the Earth, with no external metal.

But after the Moon was flattened as a squashed sphere above the Earth and gathered all these craters on its shell, how did it round back into a whole sphere, and why are the Moon's poles (the north pole and the south pole) more crowded with large craters than any other region on its face?
