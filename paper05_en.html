Several rows need a word of explanation. The construction. In the LEF model the Moon was not thrown off finished: it was built as a body standing on the Earth, seated on the tip of a long extension of the Earth’s own material, the pear, and fed from within through openings in its shell. Paper 3 follows that body through three stages — it rounded outward from a squashed shape into a ball, was flattened by the spin, and rounded inward again as the flattening relaxed and it grew denser — and it was round, its outer shell rigid, when it was released. The whole construction, from the start of the bulge to release at 3.873 Ga, spans about 600 Myr: roughly 400 for the bulge and the pear, about 100 for the flattening and about 100 to release. The maria of this paper were active at different stages of it: most openings of the far side early, until they sealed as the pear grew and less of the Earth’s heat reached them (Moscoviense, fed from Serenitatis, is the exception); the openings of the limbs over a long time; and the near-side maria, through which the Earth’s pressure passed into the Moon, until release.

The hill and the contact. The part of the body that sat on the tip of the pear is recorded on today’s Moon as two nested regions of the near side. The hill is the base of the bulge, the 6,000 km of the body’s underside that rested on the tip when the rounding began. The contact is the last 3,000 km of it, the patch that still touched the Earth at release. Both are given by their centres on today’s Moon; 335.2°E is the same as 24.8°W, so the contact lies north-west of the centre of the near side. The contact disc, the circle of 1,500 km radius about the contact centre, is used throughout the paper as the region that touched the Earth last.

The exposure zones. As the body rounded, each part of its surface kept its share of the whole, as every patch of a slightly squashed ball keeps its share when the ball becomes round; this is the rule of proportional share. It carries the zones of the body on the pear onto the Moon of today as angles measured from the centre of the near side: within 31° lies the ground that was covered until the last stage, between 31° and 70° the ring that peeled from the Earth during the outward rounding, and beyond 70° the upper dome, exposed from the start. Which zone a mare lies in tells how long it stood open to space and how long it stayed covered (Part I).

Leading and trailing. The Moon keeps one face toward the Earth, so one half always faces forward along its orbit (the leading half) and the other faces back (the trailing half). The two halves meet the returning material differently, and this matters for the count of craters (Paper 6).

Comparisons. Every comparison of the ground in this paper is made like for like. The maria have far fewer craters than the land around them, so a comparison that kept them on one side and not on the other would measure the maria rather than the ground; they are therefore removed from both sides, and each comparison says so.

The data. The basalt map of the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) outlines every dark plain of basalt on the Moon; it is the literature’s map of the maria, and this paper reads it unit by unit. The gravity is from the GRAIL mission. A Bouguer map is the gravity field from which the pull of the visible relief has been removed, assuming a crust of 2,550 kg/m³; what remains shows where the material beneath the surface is denser or lighter than that crust. The field is built of components of increasing detail, called degrees; the lowest, which describe the shape of the whole Moon, are left out, and the highest kept, 660, sets the finest detail that can be seen. Gravity is measured in milligal (mGal), a hundred-thousandth of a metre per second squared. The chemistry is read from orbit: iron and titanium as the oxides FeO and TiO2_2, in per cent by weight (wt%), from the Kaguya Multiband Imager, and thorium, in parts per million (ppm), from the gamma rays counted by Lunar Prospector. The craters are from two global catalogues, one complete from 20 km and one from 1 km, and the elevation from the SLDEM2015 model. The sizes given with each map — 240 m, 60 km, 60 m — are the finest detail it resolves.

Abstract

What a mare is. In the LEF model the Moon was built on the Earth and fed from within through openings in its shell. This paper proposes that the maria are those openings. Each stands over a feed dome: a body pushed up from a subsurface focus, which raised the ground above it. Through most openings the material rose, was thrown out, and came back as the returning material that made the craters; Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, passed material and the Earth’s pressure into the Moon and did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). The dark basalt plain is only what remains on the surface of such an opening, its last frozen layer.

Counted as openings, the maria are not where the basalt is. A feed dome is dense and pulls harder than the ground around it, so in the GRAIL gravity field, with the pull of the relief removed (the Bouguer field), it shows as a round high. Of 73 such highs of 100 km and more, 25 lie under an open mare and 44 under bright highland, 36 of them on the far side. Over these buried domes iron and thorium equal those of the land around and the crust is thicker by 8–13 km: later layers covered them. And 33 of the 34 maria identified on the gravity gradients sit on a dome.

The floor. Every floor holds one level, as a frozen lake does, below its rim by a median of 3.4 km. The maria of the contact, the region that touched the Earth until release, lie at the level of the plains beside them; the far maria lie deep below the ground around them, inside a raised collar built of their own returning material. On the open maria the centre stands lower than the margin in 10 of 12, the more so the stronger the dome beneath (ρ=−0.59\rho=-0.59).

The source. The ground around six maria carries their iron, falling from 6.9 to 3.1 wt% FeO within 150 km and holding above the far highlands out to 600 km, and the craters in it are poorer than their ground; beside the plains, which spilled their melt over their edges, the excess falls to the far highlands. The floors of the open maria carry a twelfth of the craters of 2–5 km of the land around them, the cleanest being the maria of the contact. The contact disc, 17.5% of the surface, holds 95% of the thorium above 3 ppm, and its centre, fixed in advance, is third of 16,200 possible centres; within it the maria are the poorest ground, having let their thorium sink back with the last of the melt. Read as basins of external impacting bodies, the basins of the maria would leave a foreign mass their melts do not carry.

What the abstract means, in plain words. The literature and this paper look at the same dark plains and name different things. For the literature the mare is the dark plain. For this paper the mare is the structure beneath it: a hole through the Moon’s shell over a dense body, through which material came up from depth and, at most openings, was thrown out, and the dark plain is the last skin that froze over that hole when the flow stopped. A lava lake in a volcanic crater is a fair picture: what one sees is a frozen crust, but the crust is not the volcano; beneath it is the liquid region through which the pressure that fed it passed. Once the mare is read this way, a mare need not carry basalt at all, and a patch of basalt need not be a mare.

What the gravity shows. A body denser than its surroundings pulls a passing spacecraft a little harder, and GRAIL mapped that extra pull over the whole Moon. A feed dome, frozen into a dense, iron-rich body, appears as a round high. Of the 73 highs counted, 25 lie under a dark plain, 4 are partly covered, and 44 lie under ordinary bright ground — the far side holds most of these. They are the openings of the far side, buried by the layers that later built its thick crust. The surface over them carries the same iron and thorium as the land around, because it is the land around: the cover laid over everything. The crust over them is 8–13 km thicker than over an open dome of the same strength, which is the thickness of that cover. The gravity gradients — how fast the pull changes from place to place — are sharpest at the edges of dense bodies; on them the author identified 34 maria, and 33 of them sit on one of the 73 domes. The one exception, Ingenii, lies over South Pole–Aitken, where the heat beneath that basin cooked its dome away (§C.5).

What the floor shows. A floor that is one level throughout froze as a lake freezes: one liquid surface that froze slowly, from the outside inward, not a pile of separate flows. It lies a few kilometres below its rim, and its centre sank over the dense body beneath it: of 12 open maria, 10 have a centre lower than the margin. The rank correlation ρ\rho runs from −1-1 to +1+1 and measures how consistently two quantities rise or fall together; −0.59-0.59 says that a stronger dome goes, fairly consistently, with a lower centre. Of the maria of the contact, Serenitatis froze first, very close to the Earth, and Imbrium after detachment, as the Moon receded; both lie level with the plains beside them; the maria farther out lie deep inside a collar built of what they themselves threw.

What the chemistry shows. If the maria threw the material that made the craters, the land around each mare must carry its iron, the most close by and less farther out — and it does, from 6.9 wt% of iron oxide near the edge to 3.1 at 150 km, still above the far highlands at 600 km. A crater made later in that ground threw the iron-rich veneer aside and opened the poorer base, so it is poorer than its own ground. The dark plains without a feed dome behave differently: their melt spilled over their edges and did not reach far. Thorium, a radioactive element the literature attributes to the last residue of a magma ocean, is gathered almost wholly in the contact disc: a sixth of the surface holds 95% of it. The contact centre was fixed in Paper 3 before the thorium was read; when a disc of the same size is centred in turn on 16,200 points over the whole Moon, the fixed centre catches more thorium than all but two of them (§J.3).

What the craters on the floors show. An open mare holds or melts what lands on it and keeps no record while it is active; its floor collects craters only after it froze. The floors carry a twelfth of the small craters of the land around them, and the maria of the contact, which froze late, carry the fewest. Finally, if the basins of the maria had been dug by bodies from outside, those bodies would have left their mass in the crust; the melts of the basins do not carry it (§J.7).

Introduction — the literature and the model

The literature reads a mare as two events. First an external impacting body digs a basin; hundreds of millions of years later basalt rises from the mantle and floods it. The dark plains are the basalt, the basins are the holes, and the two are counted apart: a basin without basalt is not a mare, and a patch of basalt on the floor of a crater is. The heat that made the basalt is placed in the Procellarum region, where the radioactive KREEP material is concentrated. KREEP is named for its potassium (K), rare-earth elements (REE) and phosphorus (P); it is rich in thorium and uranium, whose decay releases heat, and the literature reads it as the last liquid left when a global ocean of magma crystallised.

The table sets the two readings side by side, question by question. Each row is a place where the same observation is given a different meaning; the rest of the paper takes the rows in turn and measures which meaning the data carry. The mascon of the third row, a “mass concentration”, is the excess of gravity found under the large near-side maria.

The LEF model reads the same plains as one structure. The Moon was built on the Earth, fed from within through openings, and its maria are those openings. Each stands over a feed dome — the body through which the material rose — and the basalt is the last of what passed through it. The question of this paper is therefore not why there is basalt in the basins, but what a mare is, how many there are, and what each records of the feed system that built the Moon.

This changes what counts as evidence. If the maria are openings, a mare is recognised by the dense body beneath it, not by the colour of its surface; the land around it must carry the material it threw; its floor must have frozen as one surface after the craters around it were made; and its age is the time it cooled, not the time a separate flood arrived. Each of these is a measurement on public maps, and each is made in this paper.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

A mare is an opening over a feed dome. Material pushed upward from a subsurface focus raised a centre and widened its margins, and so built a dome beneath the opening; through it the material rose into the mare; at most openings it was thrown out, and part of it came back to the surface as returning material (Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, did not eject; Serenitatis only slight popping). What the literature counts as a mare — a dark plain of basalt — is what is left on the surface of such an opening. Counted as openings, the maria are not where the basalt is: 44 of the 73 feed domes seen in gravity lie under bright highland, 36 of them on the far side, buried by its layers together with the craters they made (Part D).

The floor froze like a lake, and sank over its dome. Every floor holds one level, about 3 km below its rim; the maria of the contact lie at the level of their plains, the far maria deep inside a collar of their own returning material; the centre of a mare stands lower than its margin, the more so the stronger the dome; the candidate causes are the weight of the dense, iron-rich body beneath it, suction, and mainly thermal expansion and contraction with heat and cold, stronger the larger the mass — still to be tested (Parts E, F).

The maria are the source of the returning material. The ground around every mare carries its iron and titanium, falling off with distance, and the large craters near it are poorer than that ground, having thrown aside the veneer the mare laid down (Part C). Thorium is concentrated where the Moon last touched the Earth: the contact disc, 17.5% of the surface, holds 95% of the thorium above 3 ppm (Part J).

The maria sealed in order. Most of the old openings of the far side sealed early, as the pear grew and less of the Earth’s heat reached them (Moscoviense, fed from Serenitatis, is the exception); the openings of the limbs threw over a long time. The near-side maria were open passages through which the Earth’s pressure passed into the Moon; at release it stopped reaching the Moon, and the ejections stopped. The near-side maria were then hot and of low viscosity and froze slowly, from the outside inward, which is why their floors are smooth — Mare Imbrium holds 11 craters of 20 km and more per million km² against 205 on the highlands. After release the small craters were made by drops thrown by the Earth, the last scatterer (Part I, §§F.5–F.6).

The requirements of the model, and what would test it, are collected in Paper 11 (The Requirements).

How the box reads. The first paragraph is the definition on which the whole paper rests, and it has three parts. The feed dome is the body beneath: material pushed up from a focus below the surface lifted the ground over it in the middle and spread it at the sides, as a dome. The opening is where that flow broke through the shell: material rose through it and, at most openings, was thrown out, and part of it came back down as the returning material that made the craters (Paper 6); Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). The basalt is what was left: the last melt in the opening, frozen into the dark plain we see. Read in this way, a mare is recognised by the dome beneath it, and most domes turn out to carry no basalt at all.

The second paragraph says what the floor records. A lake freezes to one flat surface; a floor that is one level everywhere froze in the same way. When the flow stopped, the dome beneath froze into a dense, iron-rich body, and the centre of the floor stands lower over it — the stronger the dome, the deeper the sag. Three causes may lower it: the weight of that body, suction, and mainly thermal expansion and contraction with heat and cold, which is stronger the larger the mass in diameter and depth; the measurements do not yet choose between them. The third paragraph says where the thrown material went: into the ground around each mare, as a veneer rich in iron and titanium, thickest at the edge. The thorium marks something else, the place where the Moon last touched the Earth.

The fourth paragraph gives the order in which the maria stopped. Most openings of the far side sealed first, as the pear grew and less of the Earth’s heat reached them; Moscoviense, fed from Serenitatis by a straight internal passage, is the exception. The openings of the limbs threw over a long time. The near-side maria were open passages through which the Earth’s pressure passed, through the pear, into the Moon while the body was attached; at release the pressure stopped reaching the Moon, and the ejections stopped. The near-side maria were then hot and of low viscosity, and they froze slowly, from the outside inward, after almost all the returning material had already landed. That is why their floors are so clean: Imbrium carries 11 large craters per million km² where the highlands carry 205. After release only drops thrown by the Earth still reached the Moon, and they scattered the small craters (§F.6).

A.1Opening, mare, basin and feed dome

Four structures carry the model, and they are best pictured from the bottom up: a dense body beneath (the feed dome), the place where its material reached the surface (the opening), the surface that opening left behind (the mare), and the margin around it (the basin).

The feed dome. A feed dome forms where material is pushed upward from a subsurface focus. The push raises the centre and widens the margins, and so builds a dome beneath the mare. From it the material rises into the mare; at most openings it was thrown out, and part of it returned to the surface of the Moon as returning material (Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, did not eject; Serenitatis only slight popping). The dome is not an intrusion and not a plug: it forms from the flow that passes through it and out. The difference matters. An intrusion is a body of melt that enters the crust and stops there; a plug is what blocks a channel. A feed dome is neither a body that came to rest nor an obstacle; it is the shape the flow itself builds as it passes upward, as the water of a strong spring raises a mound over its outlet while it runs through it. Once the flow stopped, the dome froze into a dense, iron-rich body, and that body is what the gravity field sees today (Part E).

The opening. The opening is the place where the feed reached the surface. An exit opening threw material outward; a mouth passed material while still covered, toward the exit openings, and was exposed only as the contact retreated. The same structure can begin as a mouth and end as an exit (§I.2). An opening is not a pipe with walls: it is a region of liquid within the parent material, bounded by a contrast of viscosity, through which pressure passes. Viscosity is a material’s resistance to flow — honey has more than water, and a cold, stiff shell vastly more than either. The opening is therefore defined by how easily its material flows compared with the material around it, not by a wall; the pressure from below passes through the runny region as through a gap, and the stiff material around it does not let it pass.

A.2The source is not the landing place

An exit opening throws material that lands 50 to 1,000 km away, and while it is active its own floor is remelted and covers its record. Two consequences follow, and the equation below states them. A surface near an active opening gains craters made by what the opening throws; the opening’s own floor loses the craters that land on it, because it is still liquid or is melted again. The number of craters seen on a surface is therefore Nobs=Nbg+Nreturn−Nerased,N_{\mathrm obs}=N_{\mathrm bg}+N_{\mathrm return}-N_{\mathrm erased},\tag{1} where NobsN_{\mathrm obs} is the number of craters observed on the surface today, NbgN_{\mathrm bg} the background — the craters made there by material from all other sources — NreturnN_{\mathrm return} the craters made by material thrown from the nearby opening, and NerasedN_{\mathrm erased} the craters later removed by melting or covering. The equation is a bookkeeping rule, not a model to be fitted: it says only that a crater count is the result of three processes at once and cannot be read as a simple clock.

A mask that removes the maria does not restore what they erased: leaving the floor of a mare out of a count removes its area from the comparison, but the craters it melted while it was active are gone, and nothing in the remaining ground shows where they were. A crater cannot be assigned to the mare that threw it — the returning material carries no label of its source, and an opening throws to every distance between 50 and 1,000 km. The test is statistical, by distance and by side: if the maria are the sources, the ground and the craters around them must, on average, carry their material more strongly near them than far away (§C.7), and must differ between the leading and the trailing halves (Paper 6).

What we now have: separate names for the dense body beneath (the feed dome), the place where it reached the surface (the opening), the surface left behind (the mare’s basalt) and the margin around it (the basin); and a rule that a crater count records arrival, return and erasure together.

B.1From Paper 3: the body on the pear

In the model the Earth, spinning fast, raised a bulge that was drawn out into a pear shape, and the body that became the Moon was built on its end (Paper 3). The body sat on the curved tip of the Earth’s pear, a flattened ball with a lower dome on the tip and an upper dome above it — a squashed ball resting on a rounded support, its underside hugging the support and its top standing high over it. The tip was 6,000 km across when the rounding began and 3,000 km at release: as the body became rounder, the area by which it touched the Earth narrowed to half its width (Paper 3, §B.4).

The construction ran in three stages: the outward rounding, as the body seated on the tip became a ball; the flattening, in which most craters formed and the largest, the differentiation having advanced and the ejected material being light and rich in water; and the inward rounding, as the flattening relaxed and the body densified. Differentiation is the separation of a melt into heavier and lighter parts; by the flattening it had gone far enough that what was thrown out was light and rich in water. Each of the three stages is derived in Paper 3; here they set the order in which the openings were exposed.

Three zones are carried onto the Moon of today: the hill, the base of the bulge, 6,000 km across and holding 99% of the near-side maria; the contact, the last 3,000 km, holding 66% of them; and Mare Imbrium within the contact, the opening that stayed connected to the Earth until detachment; through it and through Serenitatis, which peeled away first, heat and material entered the body from the Earth. The two shares are shares of area: of the area of the dark basalt of the near side on the LROC map, not of the number of maria (by number, 13 of the 14 near-side maria of Appendix A lie in the hill and 4 in the contact). The 66% is that of the contact as outlined on the map in Paper 3; on the circles of Appendix A, 3,000 km and 1,500 km in radius, the shares come to 99.8% and 72%. The hill is the region that sat over the wide tip when the rounding began, the contact the region still attached at release; on today’s Moon they are two nested discs on the near side, and their centres are given in §B.3. The source of each is Paper 3, §B.4.

How the contact let go. The corotation line is the distance at which a body turning with the Earth already moves at the speed of a free orbit. The contact peeled from the tip of the pear as the body’s centre of mass reached that line, and the Moon was detached a little beyond it. Detachment exactly on the line would leave the Moon on a circular orbit, which the tide cannot make eccentric; the eccentric orbit of the Moon therefore requires detachment slightly after the centre of mass crossed. The series adopts a release about 210 km beyond the line, when a little more than half the Moon, about 58%, had crossed it, because two independent tests both point to it: the present inclination (release at 22.5°) and the present eccentricity (for a hot young Moon). The full tables are in Paper 3.

The openings are of three kinds by when they were exposed: those of the flat shell at the first stage; those on the upper dome, exposed as it rose, which threw material outward; and those under the lower dome, covered until release, through which material only passed. These are the exit openings and the mouths of §A.1, now placed in time; their zones on today’s Moon are given for each mare in Appendix A.

The near side was opened by heat: the region that touched the tip of the Earth’s pear, the 3,000 km of the contact, melted away its sial — the light upper layer of crust, rich in silicon and aluminium — and the thin crust beneath the near-side maria is thin because they are openings (Paper 3, §B.7). The order of cause is the point: the crust there is not thin first and then broken through; it is thin because the openings and the heat beneath them removed its upper layer.

B.2From Paper 6: the returning material

Material thrown from the openings returned to the shell at the speed it left, and made the craters: some 99% of them are of returned terrestrial material (Paper 6, The Craters). A stone thrown upward falls back at the speed with which it was thrown; in the same way the material thrown from the openings landed back on the shell fast enough to make craters. The body’s own ejecta landed toward its trailing half, 90°E, and met its leading half, 90°W, head-on — the leading half moved into the material as a runner moves into rain, and was struck harder. The maria are the source of that material, and the foreign mass an external impacting body would leave is not found in the crust (Paper 6, §C.1, §L.5, Parts F–I). This is the link between the two papers: Paper 6 measures the craters, and this paper names the openings that threw the material that made them.

C.1The mare in the literature

In the literature a mare is a surface: a dark plain of basalt, recognised by its low albedo and mapped by its outline. The basin is a separate thing, the structure made by an external impacting body, and the mare is its filling. A basin without basalt is therefore not a mare, and a patch of basalt on the floor of a crater is. Basalt hidden under later bright material is a cryptomare, a mare that cannot be seen. The International Astronomical Union names the dark plains by kind: Oceanus, Mare, Lacus, Sinus, Palus — ocean, sea, lake, bay and marsh, names given when the dark plains were thought to be water. Under this definition a mare is counted by looking: whatever is dark and flat and made of basalt is a mare, whatever its size and whatever lies beneath it.

C.2The mare in the model

In the model a mare is an opening over a feed dome, and the basalt is only what remains on its surface. Under this definition a mare is counted by what lies beneath it, not by what lies on top. Two rules follow. Every opening with a feed dome beneath it is counted, also when it is buried and carries no basalt. A patch of basalt that a later crater exposed on its floor, with no dome beneath it, is not counted: it is a crater that landed on a lava area and exposed a lava floor. The first rule adds maria the literature does not see; the second removes some it does.

The maria surveyed in this paper. The maria were identified by the author on the gradients of the GRAIL Bouguer gravity field, which show the edges of dense bodies beneath the surface: 34 openings, listed in Appendix A with their positions, zones and the feed dome beneath each. The identification was made by eye: the author marked, on the gradient map in QuickMap, the round outlines that enclose a dense body, whether or not basalt lies on top. These are the maria of this paper, and every study in it is carried out on them.

34 maria and 36 marked openings. Two counts recur in this paper and must be kept apart. The author first marked 36 openings by eye on QuickMap, numbered 1 to 36; these 36 marks are the sample of the crust test (§D.2) and of Study 3. Six of the marks (numbers 2, 8, 14, 15, 22 and 31) did not hold as maria, and four openings were added later (numbers 37–40); the catalogue of Appendix A therefore holds 34 maria, among them Copernicus H (35) and Sinus Aestuum (36). Where the text speaks of the maria, it means these 34; where it speaks of the marked openings, it means the 36 original marks.

Each mare is given the area of the core of the feed dome beneath it. Three of them — Ingenii, Apollo and Bailly — lie over South Pole–Aitken, the largest and oldest basin of the far side; the feed domes beneath them were cooked away by the heat under that basin, and each is given a radius of 150 km. The dark plains of basalt that carry no feed dome — Oceanus Procellarum, Mare Insularum, Mare Nubium, Mare Cognitum, Mare Frigoris, Mare Vaporum, Mare Tranquillitatis and others — are not maria: they are the ordinary crust of the section of the pear, melted in place by the general heat of the Earth while the body was attached, with no channel to depth. They are dark for the same reason a mare floor is dark — melt froze on them — but the melt came from heat applied from outside, not from a feed rising from below, and so no dense body lies beneath them. In this paper they are called plains. Mare Fecunditatis, Mare Australe and Lacus Mortis, though they lie over gravity highs, are not among the maria of this paper.

C.6 What the difference in definition explains

The two definitions do not only give different numbers; they give a different picture of the Moon. Read as basalt, the Moon is two-faced: a dark near side and a bright far side. Read as openings, it is not: the far side holds more feed domes than the near side, 40 against 33, buried by the layers that built it (Paper 3, §B.7), while on the near side, which faced the hot Earth, the openings were active passages of the Earth’s pressure — Serenitatis until it peeled away, Imbrium until detachment. The smooth near side and the layered far side are one feed system under two covers — as two halves of a field with the same wells, one half later covered with soil and the other left bare. And a crater with a lava floor is not an opening: it landed on lava, and the model counts it among the craters, not among the maria.

What we now have: a definition of a mare by what lies beneath it; a count of the openings that includes the 44 the basalt does not show; and a test showing that the maria and the feed domes are one record. The rest of this Part asks whether these openings threw the material that lies around them.

C.8The veneer and the crater

The source test leaves one result to explain: a crater near a mare is slightly poorer in iron than the ground around it. The explanation is a thin surface layer. The ground around a mare is the mare’s own material: many small fragments thrown over the land around it laid a veneer rich in iron and titanium, thick at the mare’s edge and thinning outward. The ground beside a plain carries a veneer of the same kind, of the plain’s own melt spilled over its edge. A crater made later in this ground threw the veneer aside and opened the poorer base, and so it is poorer than its own ground at every distance; in the controls, where there is no veneer, the difference is zero — as a spade pushed into a lawn sprinkled with dark sand throws the sand aside and shows the paler soil beneath.

Dionysius, a crater of 17.4 km at 17.6 km from the plain of Tranquillitatis, shows the three places at once: the plain holds 17.2/8.1 wt% FeO/TiO2_2, the ground around the crater 12.7/3.4, its interior 9.1/0.7, and its ejecta 8.5/1.5 — the crater threw out the poor base, and the rich veneer lies beyond it. The numbers fall in the order the picture requires: the plain richest, the ground beside it next, and the crater’s inside and the material it threw out the poorest, because both come from beneath the veneer.

The halo is the zone around a mare or a plain in which the ground carries its iron. The halo grows with the basalt beside it. Its width is measured by the half-distance: the distance at which the excess of iron over the far ground falls by half. The distance at which the excess of iron falls by half is about 23 km around Moscoviense and about 119 km around the plain of Tranquillitatis; for titanium 17 and 104 km. PART D

The openings, visible and buried

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

if a mare is an opening, the openings must be found where no basalt lies. The approach: the feed domes of the gravity field are read with the surface above them, the crust over them and the chemistry on them.

Part C defined a mare as an opening over a feed dome and showed that the maria marked on the gravity map and the dense bodies measured in the gravity field are one record. This Part takes the next step. If the opening, not the basalt, is the mare, then some openings should exist where no basalt is seen at all: openings that worked, were later covered by the layers that built the land around them, and are now hidden under bright highland. We call such an opening a buried opening, and the dense body beneath it a buried feed dome. The question of this Part is how such an opening can be recognised when nothing on the surface shows it, and how it can be told apart from an ordinary crater.

The answer rests on gravity. A spacecraft pair such as GRAIL measures how strongly each part of the Moon pulls. The Bouguer gravity field is that pull after the attraction of the visible relief — the hills and hollows of the surface — has been computed and removed; what remains shows where the material beneath the surface is denser or lighter than average. A feed dome froze into a dense body, so it pulls harder than the ground around it and appears in the Bouguer field as a round patch of stronger gravity, whether basalt lies on top or not. Gravity therefore sees the opening through its cover. Three further readings then tell whether a gravity high is a buried opening: the crust under it (§D.2), the cover on top of it, and the chemistry and craters of that cover (§D.5).

D.1The map of the openings

The two catalogues of Part C are one record seen twice: 34 maria identified on the gradients of the gravity field, and 73 feed domes measured in it. The first catalogue was made by eye: the gradients of the field show where the pull changes sharply, that is, the edges of dense bodies. The second was made by code: every round gravity high of 100 km and more, standing 50 mGal and more above the ring around it, was taken as a feed dome (§C.3). (The mGal, or milligal, is a small unit of gravitational acceleration used for such local differences.) Thirty-three of the 34 sit on a dome (§C.5). The table of all maria, with name, position, zone, generation and the dome beneath, is Appendix A.

D.2Basin or crater, by what lies beneath

A large round dark plain may be one of two things. It may be an opening, the surface of a feed dome; or it may be an ordinary crater whose floor happens to be dark, because the returning material landed on a lava area and exposed lava on its floor. On the surface the two can look alike. This section separates them by what lies beneath.

What lies beneath decides. The reason is simple: a feed dome was pushed up from a subsurface focus, so it raised the dense material beneath the opening and thinned the light crust above it; a crater was made from above, by material that landed on the surface, and leaves the crust beneath it as it was. So under the centre of an opening the crust is thinner than in a ring at 1.3–2.0 radii around it; under a crater it is not. We call the difference — the crust thickness in the ring minus that under the centre — the crust jump.

Of the 36 marked openings (the points marked on the gravity map; their relation to the 34 maria is given in Part C), 26 show a jump of 8 km and more in the crustal models of GRAIL — Moscoviense +29 km, Orientale +28, Nectaris +24, Humboldtianum +24, Apollo +23, Smythii +23, Crisium +22, Serenitatis +21 among them; 4 are unclear; and 6 show less than 4 km, all of radius 43–92 km, Tsiolkovskiy among them. Every opening larger than about 120 km shows the jump. Under Moscoviense, for example, the crust is 29 km thinner at the centre than in the ring around it. Tsiolkovskiy, its floor dark with lava, is a crater: it landed on a lava area. The six small structures without a jump are therefore not openings that failed the test; they are craters that the eye, reading the dark floor, had taken for openings, and the crust beneath them sorts them out.

The phenomenon. Some round dark plains are large basins of the maria, others are craters whose floor is dark with basalt. The gap in knowledge. By their surface the two look alike; what lies beneath has not been used to sort the marked openings one by one. The research question: is the crust thinner under the centre of each marked opening than in a ring around it, and does that separate the openings from ordinary craters of the same size?

The GRAIL crustal models show the crust thinned under the large basins, read as the mantle lifted by the basin-forming event [S19]; the mascons above them are read as that uplift with basalt on top [S20]. (A crustal-thickness model converts the measured gravity into the depth of the boundary between the light crust and the denser mantle beneath it, given an assumed density for each; a mascon, short for “mass concentration”, is a region whose gravity is stronger than its surroundings because denser material lies beneath it.) What is new here: the thinning is measured for each opening of the catalogue, and the same measure is taken on ordinary craters away from any opening.

An opening sits over a feed dome that thinned the crust beneath it; a crater landed on the surface and did not. So the large openings show a crust jump between the ring and the centre, and the ordinary craters do not.

Type of research: quantitative, with a control group. The sample: the 36 marked openings; the control: 169 craters of 40–300 km radius from the Wang & Wu catalogue [S6], latitude within 70°, none closer to an opening than twice its radius. The control is the heart of the test: it asks what an ordinary crater of comparable size looks like beneath, measured in exactly the same way, so that a jump under the openings cannot be put down to the method or to size alone. The tools: the four crustal-thickness models of GRAIL, averaged, and code that reads the crust under the centre (to 0.3 of the radius) and in the ring of 1.3–2.0 radii. The four models differ in the densities and porosity they assume; averaging them keeps the result from resting on one set of assumptions.

The crust was read under each opening and each control crater with the same rings. A jump of 8 km and more counts as a jump; less than 4 km as none. The gap between the two thresholds keeps the borderline cases, the “unclear”, apart from both groups.

The openings. In the first reading (the text above), 26 of the 36 show a jump of 8 km and more, 4 are unclear and 6 show less than 4 km, all of radius 43–92 km. Read again with exactly the rings used for the control, 29 of the 36 show the jump and 5 show less than 4 km; the median jump is 17 km. Every opening larger than about 120 km shows it. The control. Of the 169 ordinary craters, 4 show a jump of 8 km and more, about 2%; their median jump is 1 km. What chance would give. The control fixes the chance rate: if the marked openings were ordinary craters, about 2% of them — less than one of the 36 — would show the jump. The observed number is 29. The typical opening has its crust thinned by 17 km; the typical crater by 1 km, within the noise of the models. By size. Among the structures of 40–120 km radius neither the small openings nor the craters show the jump; above 120 km almost every structure is an opening, and almost every opening shows it.

Result. The hypothesis is supported: a jump in the crust marks an opening, and an ordinary crater lacks it. The small openings without the jump, Tsiolkovskiy among them, are craters that landed on a lava area. Limitations. Size and kind are tied together: there are few ordinary craters as large as the large openings, so the comparison is strongest below 120 km; the crustal models assume a density for the crust, and the jump depends on it. A specialist may object that the large basins are expected to thin the crust whatever made them. The answer of this study is the control: the craters of 40–300 km radius, measured the same way, show the jump in only 4 of 169, so the jump belongs to the openings and not to any large round structure. Directions for further research. The jump read on the field of degrees 6–900, and against the strength of the dome above it.

D.3Procellarum, a basalt plain

Oceanus Procellarum, 1.8 million km² of basalt, is not one mare but a basalt plain. PART E

The feed dome

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

an opening rose through a dome, and the dome must be seen. The approach: each mare was measured over the core of its own feed dome: the strength of the dome, and the height of the centre against the margin.

Part D found the openings, open and buried, through the dense bodies beneath them. This Part looks at the dense body itself, the feed dome, and asks whether the surface of each mare carries its mark.

It helps first to fix the physical picture. A feed dome forms where material is pushed upward from a subsurface focus — a place at depth from which the feed rises. The push raises the centre and widens the margins, as a hand pressing up under a sheet of soft dough lifts its middle and stretches it outward. Through the dome the material rose into the opening, and at most openings it was thrown out (§A.1); Imbrium and Serenitatis did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). While the feed is active, the melt stands at the height of the rim. When the feed stops, the material left in the dome freezes in place into a body richer in iron and titanium, and therefore denser, than the crust around it. That dense body is what the gravity field sees today: it pulls harder than its surroundings, and so stands out in the Bouguer field as a round high, strongest over its centre (Figure 5). Its strength, the excess of gravity at its centre over the ring around it, measures how much dense material it holds. Over it the surface, the floor of the mare, stands lower at the centre; Three causes may lower the centre — the weight of the dense body, suction, and, in our view mainly, thermal expansion and contraction with heat and cold, which is stronger the larger the mass in diameter and depth (§E.3). This Part measures the sag.

(Strictly, Ingenii alone has no dome under it; Apollo and Bailly fall within the core of a large neighbouring dome, §C.5, but none of the three has a dome of its own.) The domes differ widely in strength, the excess of gravity at their centre over the ring around them. Centre against margin. Of the 12 open maria, 10 have a centre lower than the margin; the median difference is −267-267 m. Strength against height. Result. The hypothesis is supported: the centre of a mare sank over its dense, iron-rich dome, and sank the more the stronger the dome. The two maria of the contact. Serenitatis and Imbrium froze close to the Earth. The Earth’s pull drew the near side outward; a mare that froze under that pull froze with its centre held up, and the centre did not sink afterwards. Serenitatis, which peeled away first (Paper 3), froze first, very close to the Earth, and was drawn outward the most (+60+60 m); Imbrium froze after detachment, as the Moon receded (+43+43 m). The maria farther from the contact froze when that pull no longer held them, and their centres sank over their domes. The two exceptions are thus not noise in the test: they are the two maria the model places where the Earth’s pull was strongest, and they are the two that did not sink. Limitations. The test rests on 12 open maria; the height at the centre also carries the later landings and the shape of the floor before it froze. E.3 Why the floor sinks

The feed dome froze into a dense body rich in iron and titanium. The centre stands over the deepest part of that body, and sank the most. Three causes may lower the centre — the weight of the dense body, suction, and, in our view mainly, thermal expansion and contraction with heat and cold, which is stronger the larger the mass in diameter and depth. The weight of the dense body presses on what lies beneath it; suction draws the floor down as the melt beneath withdraws; and a mass that swelled with heat shrinks as it cools, the more so the larger and deeper it is. The picture of the last is a pot of thick soup left to cool: the surface ends lowest where the soup beneath it was deepest. All three are to be tested; the measurements do not choose between them, and the paper does not. What the measurements do fix is the link: the depth of the sag follows the strength of the dense body beneath each mare (§E.2).

A reader trained on the literature will note that the load of a mascon is also expected to bend the floor down at the centre (§E.2, Study 5, step 2). The two readings agree on the shape, a centre sunk over a dense body with its margins stretched and its interior squeezed. They differ on what the dense body is: in the literature, basalt fill over mantle lifted by an external event; in the model, the frozen feed dome itself, through which the opening was fed. The shape of the floor is common to both; what the model adds is the body beneath, and Part D has shown that body also under 44 openings that carry no basalt at all.

The floor, the floors and the last scatterer

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

if a mare froze as a lake, its floor must be one surface. The approach: the 646 units were measured for the levels, the rims and the small craters of their floors, and the floors compared with each other.

Part E went beneath the mare, to the feed dome, and found that the centre of a mare sank over its dense, iron-rich dome — by its weight, by suction or, mainly in our view, by thermal contraction, still to be tested — except at Serenitatis and Imbrium, the two maria of the contact, whose centres stand slightly raised. This Part comes back up to the surface the dome left behind: the floor. It asks three questions in turn. First, what shape is the floor — one surface or many, and how does it stand against its rim, the land around it and the basalt plains beside it (§§F.1–F.4)? Second, how many craters does it carry against the land around it (§F.5)? Third, which source made the craters it did gather, and what do they tell of the order in which the floors froze (§F.6)? The thread joining the three is simple: an opening that is still active melts or swallows whatever lands on it, so its floor begins to keep a record only when it freezes. The shape of the floor tells how it froze; the craters on it tell when.

Two words are used throughout. The floor of a mare is the basalt that lies within the core of the feed dome beneath it — the frozen surface of the opening itself. A buried mare is an opening that sealed and whose surface was later covered by the layers that built the far side, sial and returning material (Part D); it has no exposed floor of its own. An open mare is one whose floor is still exposed.

F.1One floor

Why a frozen lake is level. A liquid at rest under gravity cannot hold a slope: any part that stands higher flows down until the whole surface lies at one level, as the surface of a lake does. If it then freezes while still at rest, it keeps that one level. A floor built instead of many flows, laid at different times and frozen one by one, would show several levels — terraces or steps where one flow ends against another. The number of levels on a floor is therefore a direct test of how it formed. The model proposes that each mare floor is the top of one body of melt in the opening, which froze in place; it should show one level. And because the material that came out of the opening built the ground around it (§F.3), and the centre afterwards sank over the dome (Part E; by its weight, by suction or, mainly, by thermal contraction), the frozen level should lie below the rim and below the land around it, with its lowest part inward of the wall rather than against it.

How the floors were measured. The floor of each mare — the basalt within the core of the feed dome beneath it — was read on the global elevation model for the 16 maria whose floor covers 5,000 km2^2 and more: the 12 open maria, Humboldtianum (partial), Ingenii and Apollo over South Pole–Aitken, and Schiller–Zucchius, whose buried dome, 909 km across, still holds about 11,000 km2^2 of basalt; the other buried maria carry no floor to read. The elevation model is SLDEM2015 [S7], which joins the heights measured by the laser altimeter of the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LOLA) — an instrument that times a laser pulse down to the surface and back — with the stereo images of the Japanese SELENE Terrain Camera, giving one height map of the whole Moon. For this survey it was read on a grid of about 7.6 km (the limitation of Study 6 below), which is fine enough for these floors: a floor of 5,000 km2^2, the smallest admitted, is about 80 km across, some ten cells. The outline of the basalt is taken from the LROC mare map [S1], and the outline of the core from the feed domes of Appendix A. On each floor the overall tilt was removed and the heights of all its cells were gathered into one distribution; a floor of one level gives one peak, a floor of several flows at several heights gives several. F.2Margins stretched, interior squeezed

Two kinds of feature cross the mare floors. A wrinkle ridge is a low, winding rise where the surface was pushed together and buckled; a graben is a long, narrow trough between two parallel faults, where the surface was pulled apart and a strip dropped down. Their positions tell where the floor was squeezed and where it was stretched. Positions are given as fractions of the radius of the core of the dome: 0 at the centre, 1 at the edge of the core, more than 1 outside it.

On the mapped units of the maria the wrinkle ridges lie at 0.71–0.77 of the radius and the graben at 0.76–1.22: the margin was stretched as the centre sank, and the interior squeezed into ridges. The picture is a sheet of ice on a pond whose water drops beneath its middle: as the middle sags, the ice is pulled apart near the shore and pressed together toward the centre. No moat runs along the walls: the lowest part of every floor lies inward of 0.9 of the radius. A moat — a trough along the foot of the wall — would mean that the floor sank most at its edge; the floor sank most near its middle, over the dome.

The measurements of §§F.1–F.2 form one study, set out below in the seven steps used for every study of this paper.

The research question: is each mare floor one level; how does it stand against its rim, the land around it and the plains beside it; and where do its lowest part, its graben and its ridges lie?

F.3The margins: layers of craters

§F.1 left one question open: why does the ground around a far mare stand so high above its floor? In the literature the high ground around a basin is its rim, raised by the event that dug it. The model reads it otherwise, as ground built up from outside by what the mare itself threw out.

Every mare overflowed, at one time or another, but not as lava over its rim: the basalt reaches 0–7% of the ring around the far maria (Moscoviense 1%, Orientale 1%, Smythii 3%, Nectaris 7%). If melt had spilled over the rim, the ground around would be basalt; it is almost none. What overflowed was the material that came out of the mare and landed around it. Near the mare it was hot and of low viscosity — viscosity is a liquid’s resistance to flow: honey is more viscous than water, and a hot melt flows more easily than a cool one. A landing smeared and did not keep the form of a crater, but it left its layer, as a spoonful of hot porridge dropped on a plate spreads out instead of holding a crater. Layer on layer, the returning material of the mare built its margins, and the ground around a far mare stands high. A crater builds its rim otherwise: the energy at its centre pushes material outward, and the push piles the rim. The maria of the contact were fed to the end and did not overflow; they lie at the level of their plains (§F.1). Serenitatis, though in the contact, lies below the ground beside it; it did not eject material of substance, and around it lies a ring of spray from its slight popping during the peeling.

F.5Craters on the floors

Until now the floors were read for their shape. From here they are read for the craters on them, which tell when each floor froze.

The floors are clean. “Clean” here has a precise meaning: a floor carries fewer craters per area than the land around it, at every crater size measured. The reason to expect it is the core of this paper’s picture. An open mare melts or holds what lands on it and keeps no craters on itself — as a pot of boiling soup keeps no mark of what is dropped into it; a floor gathers craters only after it froze. The land around, by contrast, kept every crater made on it from the time it was laid down. If the floors froze after most of the returning material had already landed, they must be far cleaner than their land; if they froze early, they would be as cratered.

F.6 The order of freezing, and the last scatterer

§F.5 showed that every floor is clean; this section asks why some are cleaner than others, and who made the few craters they do carry. The last scatterer is the last source that threw the pieces which made the craters now on a surface. Knowing it matters, because the pattern of craters on the floors can be read as a clock only if the source that made them did not favour one mare over another.

The floors are clean, but not equally. The craters they carry were made after each floor froze, and the last scatterer that made them was the Earth: after release it went on throwing material, and part of it reached the Moon from all sides (Part G; Paper 6, Study 9). The sequence is this. At release the Moon left the Earth already round, its outer shell rigid; its flattening and rounding had taken place while it was still attached (Paper 7). The pear on which it had sat was not carried away with it: the sorting gathered it slowly back into the Earth over tens to hundreds of millions of years, and as it returned it left wrinkles in the Earth’s surface, the stripes of Paper 9. At release the pressure from the Earth stopped reaching the Moon, and with it the ejections from all the Moon’s openings; most openings of the far side had sealed long before, as the pear grew. The near-side maria, hot and of low viscosity, then froze slowly from the outside inward. The Earth’s own openings, however, did not stop. They went on throwing material, large pieces first while the Moon was close, small ones later as it receded, all of them hot (§§G.4–G.5). The Earth was therefore the last scatterer, and above all of the small craters on the frozen floors.

This is why the source matters for the reading. Material thrown by a mare of the Moon lands thickest near that mare, so it would leave more craters on the floors close to it than on the floors far away — a gradient toward one mare. Material from the Earth, arriving from all sides, has no such favourite on the Moon. No gradient toward one mare is then expected. Each floor gathered craters from the moment it froze, and the number of craters orders the floors by when they froze — as snow falling evenly on a set of tables, set out one after another, leaves the deepest layer on the table set out first. The maria of the contact, which froze last, are the cleanest. They froze last because they were the last to be fed: the contact was the last region of the Moon to let go of the Earth, Serenitatis, which peeled away first, froze first, very close to the Earth, and Imbrium, detached last, froze after detachment, as the Moon receded (Part E). The smallest pieces, of 1–2 km, are light and float far: they were scattered to every side, and the maria still active scattered small pieces of their own onto their neighbours, so that their spread over the floors is wide and their order by zone weak.

PART G

Size and state: the maria and the craters in time

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

the craters on the floors of the maria are not all in one state: some are melted, some smeared, some sharp, and the state follows the size. The approach: the state of every crater on the near-side mare floors is measured against its size, and read through the temperature and viscosity of the maria and of the arriving material, and through time. This part is shared by Papers 5 and 6: it belongs to the maria and to the craters alike. Its main study is numbered jointly with Paper 6: it is Study 8 in this paper and Study 5 in Paper 6.

The previous parts treated each mare as an opening over a feed dome, and its floor as the frozen surface of the melt that stood in that opening. A frozen floor is also a surface that receives craters, and the craters it carries tell how soft it was when they arrived. This part reads that record. By size and state we mean two properties of each crater set side by side: its diameter, and how well it has kept its form. A crater is sharp when it is as deep as, or deeper than, a fresh highland crater of the same diameter, with a standing rim and an open bowl; smeared when its rim still stands but its floor is partly filled and its walls are softened, so that it is noticeably shallower than a highland crater of its size; and melted when little more than its ring is left, the floor filled up to nearly the level of the surrounding plain. The question is simple: does the state of a crater depend on its size, and if so, what does that say about when the floors of the maria were still soft?

The part runs in seven steps. Section G.1 shows the three states side by side on one mare. Section G.2 measures the state of every crater of 3 km and more on the near-side basalt against its size (Study 8). Sections G.3 and G.4 give the two causes the model reads in the result: the cooling of the maria, and the change of the material that kept arriving from the Earth after release. Section G.5 joins the two into one sequence in time. Section G.6 turns to the large craters of the plains of the contact, whose rims were worn from below (Study 15), and G.7 states what the part shows.

Throughout, the contact is the last 3,000 km of the Moon’s base on the Earth, the disc where the Moon last touched the Earth, centred today at 24.1°N 335.2°E (Paper 3, §B.4); beyond the contact is the rest of the near-side basalt. A floor of a mare is the basalt over the core of a feed dome (Appendix A); a plain is basalt without a feed dome beneath it, such as Procellarum or Tranquillitatis.

G.1Three states on one floor

Before measuring thousands of craters it helps to see the three states on one surface. The yardstick is the depth-to-diameter ratio, the depth of a crater divided by its width. Fresh craters of a given size have a typical ratio, so a crater much shallower than its highland neighbours of the same size has been filled or worn, and one as deep or deeper has kept its form.

Within Mare Imbrium three craters show three states. Sinus Iridum, 246 km, is melted in half: its northern rim stands as the Jura Mountains, its southern half is gone, and its floor is the floor of the mare; its depth is a quarter of that of a highland crater of its size (depth-to-diameter 0.008 against 0.034). Seen from above it is a bay: an arc of mountains open toward the mare, with the mare’s own smooth surface running in where the southern rim would have been. Plato, 101 km, on the northern shore, kept its rim, but its floor is filled and smooth: it is smeared, at 0.020 against 0.034, a little more than half the depth of a highland crater of its size. Its ring of walls is complete, but inside it lies a flat, dark floor instead of a bowl. Kirch, 11 km, on the floor of the mare, is sharp, deeper than the highland craters of its size (0.166 against 0.078): a clean bowl with a crisp rim. The large crater is melted, the middle one smeared, the small one sharp.

The three stand on or beside the same mare, so differences of place, latitude or of the ground beneath cannot explain them. What differs is the size, and with it, as the following sections show, the time at which each arrived.

G.2The state of the craters by size

Two kinds of melting. A crater that landed on an active mare could be melted in two ways. Melted and vanished: it landed, melted, spread and disappeared while the mare was active, merging with the material of the mare; no body is left beneath the surface, at most remnants, and no ring; it is absent from every catalogue, and is seen only as the deficit of craters on the floors. Melted to a ring: it lost depth, its floor filled, and only its ring is seen; it is in the catalogue as a crater far shallower than a highland crater of its size. In this part melted alone means a crater melted to a ring, still seen; vanished means a crater melted and vanished. The distinction matters because the two are counted in different ways: a melted crater is counted by its state, a vanished one only by its absence, as a shortfall against a surface that kept its craters.

The measurement. Every crater of 3 km and more on the near side whose centre lies on basalt was taken from the catalogue of Wang & Wu [S6], with its depth from the global elevation models, and assigned to the floor of a mare — within the core of the feed dome beneath one of the maria of Appendix A — or to a plain. Its depth-to-diameter ratio was compared with that of the highland craters of the same size, latitude within 60°, basalt removed from the highland sample. Shallower than 95% of the highland craters of its size, a crater is melted: only its ring is seen. Shallower than 75% of them, it is smeared. The rest are sharp. Of the 3,478 craters, 324 lie on the floors of the maria and 3,154 on the plains.

The comparison is made size by size for a reason. Large craters are naturally shallower for their width than small ones, so a single depth threshold would call every large crater filled. Comparing each crater only with highland craters of its own size removes that effect: a crater is called melted only if it is shallower than almost every highland crater of the same diameter. The highland craters are the control because the highland was solid when they formed and has no basalt in it.

In that reading, the state of a crater follows the thickness of the later flows, and the maria and the plains are one kind of surface. What is new here: the floors over the feed domes are set apart from the plains, and all the craters of 3 km and more are classified by one rule against highland craters of their own size.

The large pieces arrived in the time when the maria were active; on an active mare they melted, many of them spreading and merging with the material of the mare until they vanished, others left as rings. The contact. Result. The hypothesis is supported: on the floors of the maria craters melted and vanished, merging with the material of the active mare, while the plains kept theirs; the state of the craters that remain follows the size, melted for the large and sharp for the small; and on the maria of the contact, the last to freeze, the large craters are gone and the craters that remain are sharp. Limitations. The depth of a small crater is less well measured than that of a large one; the classes rest on a rule that sets the lines at 95 and 75 of every 100 highland craters; the large craters on the floors are few; and the state alone does not tell whether a crater was melted or covered by a later flow. Directions for further research. Remnants of the vanished craters in the gravity field beneath the floors of the maria of the contact; the large craters of the plains of the contact (Study 15).

G.3 The maria: temperature, viscosity and time

Study 8 established the pattern. This section and the next give its causes: here, the floor that receives the crater; in §G.4, the material that makes it. The key property of the floor is its viscosity, its resistance to flow: water has a low viscosity, honey a higher one, cold tar a very high one. A floor of low viscosity closes over a crater; a floor of very high viscosity keeps it.

The viscosity. A mare basalt melt is fluid: the lavas returned by Apollo have a viscosity of the order of 1 Pa s near their liquidus [S14], about 1,200 °C. The liquidus is the temperature above which the melt is wholly liquid; 1 Pa s is about a thousand times the viscosity of water, close to that of glycerine. As the melt cools below the liquidus, crystals grow in it; as they approach half of its volume the melt stops flowing, and its viscosity rises by many orders of magnitude before it freezes at the solidus, about 1,050 °C. The solidus is the temperature below which no liquid is left. A mare passes through three states as it cools — liquid, mush, solid — and the material that lands on it meets each in turn. The mush is the middle state, a crowd of crystals with melt between them, like wet sand or setting plaster: it holds a shape for a time but slowly yields.

What a landing does in each state. On a liquid mare the returning material sinks and mixes, the crater closes behind it, and at most a part of the rim that stood on firm ground remains: the crater is melted. On a mush the crater holds its shape for a while; its walls slump, its floor fills, its rim survives: the crater is smeared. On a frozen floor the crater keeps its full form: it is sharp. The picture is that of a spoon pressed into a pot of cooling porridge: in the hot pot the hollow closes at once, in the thickening pot it slowly fills and its edges sag, and in the cold, set pot it stays. Sinus Iridum is the first case — its southern rim, standing on the liquid mare, is gone, its northern rim, on the firm shore, remains as the Jura Mountains; Plato is the second; Kirch the third.

Time. A mare freezes from above. The maria of the contact were fed the longest and are the deepest. Deepest here is the depth of the body of the mare itself, measured inward, not the height of its floor: the centres of their floors stand slightly raised, Serenitatis by +60+60 m and Imbrium by +43+43 m. While they were liquid, the large craters that landed on them while they were liquid melted and vanished into the mare: not one of 20 km and more remains on their floors. The maria of the limbs, cut off earlier and shallower, froze first, and some of the large craters on them survive as rings.

G.4 The arriving material: temperature, size and distance

The second clock is in the material that lands. In the model about 99% of the craters are made by returning material from the Earth (Paper 6), and the Earth’s output did not stay the same after release.

The Earth went on throwing. At release the Earth did not stop. Its openings went on throwing material; most of it fell back to the Earth, and part reached the Moon and landed on it. To reach the Moon at a distance rr from the Earth’s centre, the material must leave the Earth’s surface at vmin=2GM⊕(1R⊕−1r),v_{\min}\ =\ \sqrt{2GM_\oplus\left(\frac{1}{R_\oplus}-\frac{1}{r}\right)},\tag{3} 8.8 km s−1^{-1} for a Moon at 17,000 km, 9.9 at 30,000 km and 10.6 at 60,000 km (R⊕=6,371R_\oplus=6{,}371 km). The equation is the balance of energy: a piece leaving the surface must carry enough energy of motion to climb out of the Earth’s gravity from the surface to the Moon’s distance. As the Moon receded, the speed needed rose toward the Earth’s escape speed, the speed at which a piece leaves the Earth altogether. At 17,000 km, close to the distance of release, a piece that the Earth could throw at 8.8 km s−1^{-1} reached the Moon; once the Moon stood at 60,000 km, the same piece fell back, and only those thrown at 10.6 km s−1^{-1} or more arrived.

The large first, the small later. As the Moon receded, a piece needed a higher speed to reach it (equation 3). The Earth’s gravity brought the large pieces back, while the small ones, which the Moon draws easily, still arrived. The large pieces came out first; as the storm of magma on the Earth calmed, smaller pieces, still hot, were thrown. While the Moon was close, large pieces reached it; as it receded, only smaller ones did. The size of the largest crater made on the maria falls with time.

All hot; the floors cooled. The material left a hot Earth with a low viscosity, in the state of the returning material that built the craters of the shell (Paper 6, Part J), and it stayed hot to the end: the large pieces first, then, as the storm of magma calmed, smaller pieces, still hot. What changed was the floor that received them. A small piece landing on a floor that had already frozen makes the sharpest crater: Kirch. Its ratio of 0.166, more than twice that of a highland crater of its size, is what a small piece on a frozen floor leaves.

G.6 The plains of the contact: heat from below, and the rim

The floors of the maria of the contact carry no large crater. The large craters of the contact are not missing, however: they lie on its plains, and their rims carry a second kind of wear. This section asks what wore them.

The large craters of the contact are on its plains. Of the 41 craters of 40 km and more on the basalt within the contact, not one lies on the floor of a mare: 37 lie on the plains, 22 of them on Procellarum, and 4 on the basalt of Imbrium and Serenitatis outside the cores of their domes. None is sharp, 33 are melted and 8 smeared. Beyond the contact, on the plains, 27 of 85 are sharp and 25 melted (an earlier version gave 27 of 86, counting with them a crater of 64 km on the basalt of Serenitatis that lies 1,507 km from the contact centre, just outside the disc). Most are of middle size: half are of 40–60 km, and four exceed 120 km.

The literature. The craters on the maria were made by bodies from outside; the basalt that came after them flooded the land around them. Flooding raises the ground outside a crater, so the rim that remains exposed stands lower above it, though the crest itself is not lowered [S16, S25]; a crater deeper than the flow keeps its rim as a ring above it. In that reading a low rim on a mare plain measures the thickness of the basalt laid around it. The picture is of a sandcastle with water rising around it: the water hides the lower slopes and the castle looks lower, but its top is untouched.

The model. The plains of the contact lay over the Earth: the heat that reached them came from below, from the Earth beneath them. A crater that landed on them after the middle of the storm, a piece of middle size thrown from the Earth, met a hot plain; the plain wore the rim away where it touched it, on the side that faces it. The crest itself was lowered and blunted. In the picture above, this is not water rising around the castle but warm ground beneath it softening one side, so that the top itself slumps. On Imbrium the heat came from below the crater, from the Moon, through the opening: the crater was melted from its floor upward, and its rim, standing on firmer ground, was kept.

G.7What this shows

The craters on the maria record the Earth after release. Material kept arriving from it — large at first, smaller with time, hot throughout — while the maria cooled beneath it. The large craters that landed on liquid floors vanished or were melted to rings; the middle ones that met a mush were smeared; the small ones that came last, onto frozen floors, stayed sharp; and on the plains of the contact, heated from the Earth beneath, the rims of the large craters were worn on the side of the hot plain. The activity of the Earth waned with time, but it went on after release, and the size and state of the craters on the maria are its record.

PART H

The engine

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

an opening lives as long as its melt is fed and can flow. The approach: the conditions of pressure, buoyancy, viscosity, volatiles and heat are stated, with what is computed and what is left to compute.

Parts C–G described what a mare is: an opening over a feed dome, with a floor that froze and sank, and a record of craters that tells when it froze. This Part asks what made such an opening work — what drove material up through it, and what decided when it stopped. We call this the engine of the opening. The engine has four parts, taken in turn: the force that pushes the material up, and why it must be carried by a liquid (§H.1); the volatiles that lighten the melt and help it flow (§H.2); the flux, the amount passing through the opening, and how its fall seals the opening (§H.3); and the heat of the Earth, which kept the openings of the near side open while the body was attached (§H.4). Some of these conditions are computed here; the one that needs a full thermal calculation, the critical flux, is stated as a requirement and collected in Part M.

H.1Pressure, buoyancy and viscosity

The force is carried by liquid. A solid would have to carry a stress of 13–16 GPa against a strength of about 0.2 GPa, sixty to eighty times too much; a liquid has no tensile strength and flows. A stress is a force divided by the area that carries it, and the strength of a material is the largest stress it can bear for a long time without breaking or creeping; one gigapascal (GPa) is about ten thousand times the air pressure at sea level. No solid of the shell could therefore carry the load; it would break and flow long before. A liquid does not need strength to pass on a push. It passes pressure from one end to the other as the fluid in a car’s brake line passes the push of the pedal to the wheels: the fluid itself resists nothing, it only transmits. This is why the engine of an opening is a column of melt and not a solid piston.

An opening is therefore alive while its melt is fed by pressure and buoyancy and its viscosity is low enough to flow beneath a strong shell. Two forces push the melt up. The pressure is the push from the source beneath, passed up through the liquid. The buoyancy is the lift a lighter fluid feels inside a denser surrounding, as a cork rises in water. The viscosity is the resistance of a fluid to flowing: honey has a high viscosity and water a low one. A melt of low viscosity can move through the opening while the strong shell around it holds its shape; a melt of high viscosity stalls.

The viscosity is bounded from both sides: water lowers it from above, and the need to carry iron bounds it from below. Water dissolved in a melt loosens its structure and makes it flow more easily, so a melt rich in water cannot be too thick. But a melt that is too thin lets the dense iron settle out of it instead of carrying it up, as a thin liquid lets heavy grains sink while a thick one holds them. The working melt of an opening lies between these two limits.

H.2 Volatiles

Volatiles are substances that pass easily into gas, water first among them. The early system was rich in volatiles. They lighten the melt, expand it and lower its viscosity, and they drive the material out of the maria that built their margins (§F.3). A melt carrying dissolved gas is lighter than the same melt without it, and as it rises and the pressure on it falls, the gas comes out of solution and swells it, as a bottle of soda foams when it is opened. This both lifts the melt and throws it out.

The maria were not alike in this: the volatile-rich maria sprayed more, and the maria rich in light material threw it far early, before the viscosity rose (§J.2). This difference between the maria returns in the chemistry of the ground around them, which Part J reads ring by ring.

A specialist will object that the interior of the Moon is known to be dry. The interior of the Moon is called dry today; that describes the Moon after the volatiles left, not during the construction. The samples measure the state that remained once the gas had escaped through the openings; the engine worked before that, while the volatiles were still present.

H.3Flux, decay and the critical flux

The flux QQ is the mass that passes through an opening in a unit of time. What an opening passed in its life is M=∫QdtM=\int Q\,dt: the flux added up over the whole time the opening was active, from its first flow to its seal. Part of it stayed in the body and part was thrown, Qfeed=Qretained+Qerupted,Q_{\mathrm feed}=Q_{\mathrm retained}+Q_{\mathrm erupted}, that is, the flux that fed the opening equals the part retained in the body, which built its interior, plus the part erupted out of the opening. Only the thrown part made returning craters. This matters for reading the craters: the number of craters around a mare measures what it threw, not everything that passed through it.

An opening seals when the flux falls below the level at which the heat brought in no longer balances the cooling. A tap left running on a frosty night is a fair picture: while the water flows strongly, the warmth it carries keeps the pipe open; when the flow falls to a trickle, the cold wins and the pipe freezes shut. The level of flow at which the two balance is the critical flux. The critical flux and the times of sealing require the time of transport, the diameter of the opening, the rate of rise, the viscosity of the melt, the rate at which the contact narrowed and the thermal history of the dome; they are listed as a requirement in Part M.

H.4The heat of the Earth

While the body was attached, the near side faced the hot Earth. Radiation from an Earth of about 2,000 K, with a view factor of 0.205, holds the near-side surface near 1,345 K, above the solidus: the near side could not freeze while attached, and its openings stayed open.

Three terms carry this result. The view factor is the share of the radiation leaving a surface that reaches a given body — in plain words, how much of the sky that body fills as seen from the surface. A view factor of 0.205 means that, from the near side, the Earth filled about a fifth of the sky. A surface that receives this radiation and sends it out again settles where what it emits equals what it receives; for a fifth of the sky filled by a body at about 2,000 K this is about 2,000K×0.2051/4≈1,3452{,}000\ {\mathrm K}\times0.205^{1/4}\approx1{,}345 K. The solidus is the temperature below which a material is wholly solid; for mare basalt it is about 1,050 °C (§G.3), close to 1,320 K. The near-side surface was held above it. It could not form a lasting solid lid, and the openings beneath it could not seal.

The far side faced away from the Earth. As the pear grew, less and less of the Earth’s heat reached it; it radiated to space and froze. Most of its openings sealed one after another and formed a closed cap that the pressure pushed; Moscoviense, fed from Serenitatis by a straight internal passage, stayed active long after them. One asymmetry of heat makes the mouth, the seal, the dark maria and the difference of ages: the near side, kept hot by the Earth, kept its openings as mouths and exits, passages of the Earth’s pressure, until release; most of the far side sealed its openings early; the near-side maria stayed liquid to the end and, after release, froze slowly from the outside inward into the large dark plains seen today; and the two sides froze at different times, so their surfaces carry different ages.

What we now have. An opening worked while a liquid melt, lightened by volatiles and of the right viscosity, was fed through it by pressure and buoyancy, and while the heat it brought in balanced its cooling. On the near side the Earth’s radiation removed the cooling from the balance while the body was attached. Part I adds the second condition: an opening could throw outward only once it was exposed.

Above both stood the upper dome, turned away from the Earth and open to space from the start. An opening under the contact was covered by the Earth and could only pass material into the body; an opening open to space could throw material outward. As the contact shrank, openings passed from the first state to the second. When the body rounded, each part kept its share of the surface, and the three parts became three zones about the centre of the near side: the contact a near-side cap of about 31°, the annulus a belt from 31° to 70°, and the upper dome the far side beyond (§B.3; Paper 3, §B.4). In the catalogue of Appendix A the same order is carried by the zones of Paper 3: the contact, the hill around it reaching toward the limbs, and beyond, on the far side.

I.1The moving front of exposure

As the tip of the pear narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km, the contact retreated, and the shell was exposed from the outside in. The outer regions were exposed first; the last contact stayed covered until release. The edge of the contact is therefore a front that moved inward across the shell, and every opening was exposed at the moment the front passed over it.

The geometry itself makes a clock. An opening far from the centre of the contact was exposed early; one near the centre, late. Its distance from the centre is a reading of when it was exposed, without years. The whole construction, from the start of the bulge to release at 3.873 Ga, took about 600 Myr (roughly 400 for the bulge and the pear, 100 for the flattening and 100 to release); the clock of exposure orders the openings within that time, without fixing their dates.

An image makes it plain: a deflated football, its half resting on the floor, is inflated and rounds, and the number of its faces that touch the floor falls. The faces do not change; what falls is how many of them touch the Earth at once, and only a face in contact can be a mouth. In the picture the faces are the openings and the floor is the Earth. The faces that lift off the floor first are those at the edge of the patch in contact; the face at the centre of the patch is the last to leave it.

I.2 From a covered mouth to an exposed exit

An opening under the contact fed the body inward while covered. When the front passed over it, the same path was exposed and began to throw outward. In the terms of §A.1, it began as a mouth, through which material passed while still covered, and ended as an exit, which threw material out. The mare seen today is the last stage of a structure whose life began long before. The structure is the same in both states; what differs is the dome around it, open to space or sealed. The dome seals above, and the opening below goes on passing material beneath it. This is why the same opening can carry the marks of both lives: a deep feed from its covered time, and the craters and margins of its exposed time.

I.4 Three generations

The openings fall into three generations by their history of exposure, not by the age of their basalt. The difference matters: the basalt is only the last surface of an opening and can be young over an opening that worked for a long time, so its age dates the end of an opening, not its life. Generation II: openings in the belt, exposed during the rounding, first mouths and then exits. Generation III: openings in the contact disc, exposed only toward release, the last mouths. An opening that fed for long under cover, in heat and under pressure, can be wider than one that was exposed and cooled early: a hot flow widens its path, as running water widens a channel in ice. J.2The chronology of iron

The rings of §J.1 also carry a clock. The iron of the ground records when each mare sealed. A mare sealed at its state of about 17% FeO; the ground far from it sealed low, and the ground near it high. In plain words: what a mare threw early landed far and was poor in iron; what it threw late landed near and was rich; and what it held when it sealed, about 17% FeO, is the iron of the mare itself. The maria rich in iron sealed late, and the maria poor in iron early.

The light material, poor in iron and rich in volatiles, flowed far at first; as the mare concentrated, its viscosity rose and its material flowed shorter distances, which is why the iron is highest at the rim. This is the same engine as in Part H, read from its products: at first the volatiles kept the melt thin and light and threw it far (§H.2); as they left, the melt became heavier, richer in iron and thicker, and it landed close. The fall of iron with distance is the record of that change.

How the model reads it. Thorium does not enter the crystals that grow as a melt cools; it stays in the liquid and is gathered in the last of it. Sea water freezing is a fair picture: the ice that forms is nearly fresh, and the salt gathers in the brine that remains. In a mare the melt cooled from its surface inward, and the liquid that remained, carrying the thorium, sank back down the channel toward the interiors of the Moon and the Earth. The mare was left poorer than the ground around it. A plain had no channel: its melted crust froze where it lay and kept its thorium, as the highland did. So the contact as a whole holds the thorium, and within it the channels hold the least.

Titanium went down with the last liquid too, but less: much of it was held in ilmenite and stayed. Within the contact the plains are rich in titanium, and the floors that lost part of theirs equal them. Outside the contact the maria froze fast, and there was little time for the liquid to sink: they lost less thorium, an eighth against a third. The contact lay over the middle of the passage that the pear formed between the Earth’s interior and the Moon, rich in heavy material; the far maria lay at the end of that passage, where the lightest material of the differentiating Earth arrived, and the plains beyond the contact, fed with it, are poor in titanium, so the maria there carry more titanium than their plains. Differentiation is the separation of a body into layers of different composition, the heavy sinking and the light rising; the Earth was differentiating while it fed the body, so the material reaching the far end of the pipe was the lightest.

The phenomenon. The contact holds almost all the Moon’s thorium (Study 10), and within it some dark ground is rich in thorium and some poorer. The gap in knowledge. The basalt of the contact has been read as one kind. In the model it is of two kinds: the maria, channels to depth over a feed dome, and the plains, ordinary crust melted in place. Whether the two carry the same thorium has not been measured. PART K

Dating

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

a basalt age is not the age of an opening. The approach: the clocks of an opening are named apart, the radiometric ages read as cooling, and the relative order of the maria taken from what lies on their floors.

The previous Parts placed the openings in space and read their floors, their chemistry and their craters. This Part asks when. The question matters because the usual answer, the age of the basalt brought back by the missions, is often taken as the age of the mare itself; if it were, a basalt of 2.0 Gyr would mean an opening formed 2.0 Gyr ago, long after the Moon left the Earth. The model separates the two, and this Part states how.

K.1Five clocks

Every opening carries five times that need not agree: tstructuret_{\mathrm structure}, when the opening formed; texpt_{\mathrm exp}, when it was exposed to space; tventt_{\mathrm vent}, when it began to throw; tshutdownt_{\mathrm shutdown}, when it sealed; and tsurfacet_{\mathrm surface}, the age of the surface seen today. An old opening can carry a young surface, and a basalt age is not the age of the opening.

The five are easiest to keep apart with a picture of a well. A well is dug at one time (tstructuret_{\mathrm structure}); its cover is lifted later (texpt_{\mathrm exp}); water first rises in it later still (tventt_{\mathrm vent}); it is capped when it runs dry (tshutdownt_{\mathrm shutdown}); and the puddle that dries at its mouth last of all is what a visitor finds today (tsurfacet_{\mathrm surface}). A sample taken from that puddle dates the puddle, not the digging. In the model the order is fixed by the construction: an opening forms under the cover, is exposed when the contact retreats past it (Part I), throws while it is fed, and seals when the feed stops; the condition that ties two of these clocks together is eq. (4), tshutdown≥texpt_{\mathrm shutdown}\ge t_{\mathrm exp} (§H.3).

What the radiometric ages measure. The basalts of the Apollo sites date to 3.1–3.9 Gyr, those of Chang’e-5 to about 2.0 Gyr [S8] and those of Chang’e-6, on the far side, to about 2.8 Gyr [S9]. The model reads them as cooling. A radiometric clock counts the daughter atoms made by the decay of a parent atom; it starts only when the material has cooled enough to hold those daughter atoms in place. Before that, they escape or are mixed, and the clock stays at zero. This is what is meant when a sample is said to record when its system “closed”. The heat of the Moon’s interior and of its core took a long time to leave, and a sample records when its system closed, not when its opening formed. An opening formed during the construction, which took about 600 Myr and ended with release at 3.873 Ga, can therefore carry on its surface a basalt that closed hundreds of millions of years or more after release, as the heat below it slowly left. The oldest Apollo ages, near 3.9 Gyr, lie close to release; the younger ones, and the 2.8 and 2.0 Gyr of the Chinese missions, record later cooling of material that stayed hot. The samples are shallow; the identity of the deep material is erased with depth, but the geometry remains. A sample tells what the top of the opening was when it last cooled; the shape of the opening and the dome beneath it, read in gravity (Parts C–E), tell what the opening was.

A specialist may object that a basalt is an erupted lava and that its age is the age of the eruption. In the model the two coincide only for the last liquid that reached the surface and froze there; that is the age of tsurfacet_{\mathrm surface}, or at most of tshutdownt_{\mathrm shutdown}. It is not the age of tstructuret_{\mathrm structure}, which is the subject of this paper.

Dating without isotopes. Two relative clocks are kept: superposition — what lies on what — which gives order without years; and the density of craters, compared between surfaces as long as it is not converted to years. Superposition is the rule that a layer lying on another is the younger of the two, as the top sheet of a pile of papers was put down last. The density of craters is the number of craters per unit area: a surface that has been exposed longer has gathered more, so two surfaces can be ranked by it. The conversion to years assumes a constant flux of bodies from outside, which is exactly what Paper 6 replaces, and it is not used. In Paper 6 almost all craters are made by returning material, whose rate rose and fell with the activity of the openings and of the Earth; a count of craters therefore orders surfaces, but a fixed rate of arrival cannot turn it into years.

K.2The relative order of the maria

The order in which the maria sealed is read from what lies on them, not from the age of their basalt. Three records give it, each from an earlier Part.

The floors of the maria of the contact are the cleanest of small craters, together: they sealed last (§F.5). A floor starts to gather craters only once it has frozen, so the floor with the fewest craters froze last. The large maria of the near side, Imbrium and Serenitatis, carry thirteen to eighteen times fewer craters of 20 km and more than the highlands (Procellarum, a plain, is not counted): they froze after most of the returning material had landed (§I.5). The buried openings of the far side are older than the cover above them, and the cover carries the craters of its time (§D.5): here superposition orders the opening before the layer that buried it.

Together these give an order without years: the buried far-side openings first, then the open maria of the belt, and the maria of the contact last. The test of a radiometric order is stated in §M.3: the youngest basalt of each mare against its distance from a centre fixed before the data, with thorium held fixed. Thorium is held fixed because it is a heat-producing element (§J.3): a mare rich in it could stay hot longer for that reason alone, and its youngest basalt would then be young for a reason unrelated to its place. Only once that effect is removed can the distance from the centre be judged.

What we now have is a way to order the openings that does not depend on the ages of their surface basalts: those ages date cooling, and the order of sealing is read from the floors.

L.1Data and masks

The products are those of the environment table. Every comparison of the ground is like for like: the maria are removed from both sides, and the comparison says so. This rule matters because the maria are themselves poor in craters and rich in iron. If a measurement around a mare included part of a mare floor while its control did not, the mare would push the result one way for a reason that has nothing to do with the question; removing the maria from both sides, the sample and its control, makes the two sides comparable. A “mask” is this list of areas left out of a count.

Areas are computed on the sphere; craters are assigned by their centres; a unit’s floor is the area inside its polygon. Computing on the sphere avoids the stretching of flat map projections, in which an area near the poles looks larger than it is. A crater that straddles the edge of a floor is counted where its centre lies, so no crater is counted twice. The feed domes are taken from the Bouguer field at degrees 6–660, with an independent check at 6–900. The Bouguer field is the gravity field after the pull of the visible topography has been removed, so that what is left shows the dense and light bodies beneath the surface; the “degrees” set the finest detail kept, and a higher degree keeps smaller features. A dome found at both settings is not an artefact of one choice of filter.

PART N

Discussion

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

the records of this paper must tell one process. The approach: they are drawn together, set against the explanations of the literature, and the contribution stated.

The Parts before this one each read one record: gravity, the height of the floors, the craters, the chemistry. This Part asks whether they tell one story, how that story compares with the explanations already in the literature, and what in it is new.

N.1 The unified picture

One feed system, seen in five records: the gravity of the domes, the shape of the floors, the craters on and around them, the iron and titanium of the ground, and the thorium. The openings were exposed in an order set by the narrowing contact; each stood over a feed dome through which the material rose. While open, most openings threw the material that made the craters around them and laid the veneer of their iron over the land, their floors standing at the height of their rims and spraying; Imbrium and Serenitatis did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). When the feed stopped, the floor froze as one level, and the centre sank over the dense, iron-rich dome; the candidate causes are its weight, suction and, mainly, thermal contraction, stronger the larger the mass, and they are still to be tested. Most of the far side’s openings worked early and sealed as the pear grew and less of the Earth’s heat reached them; they were then covered by the layers of sial and returning material that built the far side. On the near side the openings were open passages through which the Earth’s pressure passed, and the Earth’s heat kept them hot while the body was attached; at release the ejections stopped, and the floors froze slowly from the outside inward; the contact, the last to let go, holds the thorium.

The picture can be followed as one sequence in time. As the tip of the pear narrowed, the region still joined to the Earth shrank, and the openings outside it were exposed one band after another, the far side first and the contact last (Part I). Each was a channel over a dome: material rose and, at most openings, was thrown out and landed as the returning material. When an opening stopped being fed, its liquid froze flat, as a lake freezes, and the centre sank over the dense dome beneath, by its weight, by suction or, mainly, by thermal contraction. On the far side most openings threw early and sealed as the pear grew and less of the Earth’s heat reached them; afterwards the layers built on the far side covered them, together with the craters of their time. On the near side the openings passed the Earth’s pressure, and the Earth’s heat kept them hot until release; then they froze slowly from the outside inward, over hundreds of millions of years to 1–2 billion.

The concentration of the maria on the near side and its thin crust are explained by both readings, and a test that shows only that the maria lie on the near side decides nothing. A test decides between two readings only where they predict different results. The tests that decide are the buried domes, the floors over them, the iron rings and the foreign mass: an external body leaves no buried dome without a basin, does not make a floor one level that sinks only over a strong dome, does not lay a ring of iron that falls with distance from the mare, and does leave its metal in the melt.

N.3What exists and what is new

Secondary craters, the transfer of material and the asymmetry of the crust are known. Secondary craters are those made by material thrown out of a larger crater and landing again; the transfer of material across the Moon’s surface, and the thinner crust of the near side, are long established. New here: the mare defined as an opening over a feed dome, and counted so; the buried openings of the far side found in gravity and read with the cover above them; the floor that sank over its dome; the mare as the measured source of the returning material; and the thorium of the contact.

PART O

Conclusions

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

what the paper has shown, in order. The approach: one line for each result, with the part that carries it. Each conclusion below states first what was found and then what it means for the picture of the maria; the part or section named in brackets is where the measurement and its test are given in full.

The paper set out to answer one question: what is a mare, if not a basin dug by a body from outside and flooded later by lava? Its answer is that a mare is an opening over a feed dome, and the parts of the paper have tested that answer in five independent records — gravity, the height of the floors, the craters on and around the floors, the iron and titanium of the ground, and the thorium. The eight conclusions below follow that order, from what a mare is to where its material went and what the competing reading would require.

O.1What the paper has shown

  1. A mare is an opening over a feed dome; the basalt is what remains on its surface (Part A, C). A feed dome is the body pushed up from a subsurface focus beneath the opening; through it material rose into the mare and, at most openings, was thrown out and partly came back to the surface as returning material (Imbrium and Serenitatis did not eject; Serenitatis only slight popping). What it means: the dark basalt plain that the literature counts as “the mare” is only the last layer left on the top of the opening, not the structure itself. A mare is therefore found by what lies beneath it, not by the colour of its surface.

  2. Counted as openings, the maria are not where the basalt is: 73 feed domes in gravity, 44 of them buried, 36 on the far side; 33 of the 34 maria identified on the gravity gradients sit on a dome (Part C, D). The feed domes are round highs of the Bouguer gravity field — the field left after the pull of the visible topography is removed, so that it shows dense material below the surface. The 34 maria are those of Appendix A; the one that does not sit on a dome is Ingenii, whose dome was cooked away under South Pole–Aitken. In 20,000 sets of 34 points placed at random, not one reached 33 (Study 1). The crustal test of §D.2 was made on the 36 points the author marked by eye; Appendix A explains how these marks relate to the 34 maria. Under the centre of an opening the crust is thinner than in the ring around it, in 29 of the 36 marked openings against 4 of 169 ordinary craters (Study 3). What it means: the basalt is a poor guide to where the openings are. Most feed domes carry no basalt, and most of these lie on the far side; the difference between the near side and the far side is a difference of what covers the openings, not of how many there are.

  3. Over the buried domes iron and thorium equal the land around them and the crust is thicker by 8–13 km: the layers covered them, with their craters (§D.5). What it means: a buried dome carries on its surface the same layer as the land around it, and the extra crust above it is the thickness of that layer. These openings were active early, were then covered, and the craters made in their time were covered with them. The far side, rich in buried domes, records strong activity of openings in the early stages.

  4. Every floor is one level, about 3 km below its rim (median 3.4 km) and a median 1.6 km below the land around it; the centre of an open mare sank over its dome in 10 of 12, the more the stronger the dome; the two that did not are the large maria of the contact, which froze under the Earth’s pull (Parts E, F). The floor of each mare is one surface, frozen like a lake; in all 16 floors measured it lies below its rim and below its land, which by chance would happen once in 65,000. On the open maria the centre stands lower than the margin (median −267-267 m), and the rank correlation between the strength of the dome and the height of the centre is ρ=−0.59\rho=-0.59. What it means: the floor did not build up flow by flow from outside; it froze as one body over its dome, and the centre sank over the dense, iron-rich dome beneath it; the candidate causes, its weight, suction and, mainly, thermal contraction, are still to be tested. Serenitatis and Imbrium, the exceptions, lie in the contact, where the Earth’s pull held the near side outward while they froze.

  5. The maria are the source of the returning material: the ground around each carries its iron and titanium, from 6.9 to 3.1 wt% FeO within 150 km, with an excess out to 600 km; beside the plains the excess is their spilled melt and does not reach as far (Part C, J). The ground far from any mare, the control, holds about 2.3 wt% FeO; around the six maria the iron stays above that level all the way to 600 km. Around the plain of Procellarum the iron falls back to the level of the controls by 300–600 km. What it means: material thrown out of each mare came down on the ground around it, thickest near the mare and thinning with distance — the mark of a source, not of a target. A plain, which had no channel to depth, carries only the melt that spilled from its edge, and that does not travel far.

  6. The floors of the open maria are clean: a twelfth of the craters of 2–5 km of the land around them; the cleanest are the maria of the contact (§F.5). The comparison is like for like: the basalt is removed from the land, and the ray fields of young craters are removed from both sides. What it means: an open mare melts or holds what lands on it and keeps no craters; a floor gathers craters only after it froze. The number of craters on a floor therefore orders the floors by when they froze, and the maria of the contact, Imbrium and Serenitatis, froze last. The craters they carry were made afterwards by the last scatterer, the Earth, which after release went on throwing material toward the Moon from all sides.

  7. The contact disc, 17.5% of the surface, holds 95% of the thorium above 3 ppm; its centre, fixed in advance, is third of 16,200 possible centres, 47 km from the best. Within it the plains and the highland carry nearly the same thorium, and the maria the least (Part J). The contact is the region where the Moon last touched the Earth; its centre was set by the geometry of Paper 3 before the thorium was read. A cap of the same size was moved over the whole Moon, and almost no other centre catches more. What it means: the thorium belongs to the region of the last contact, not to the basalt: plain and highland inside the disc carry nearly the same amount. The maria of the contact, the channels to depth, are the poorest, because the last thorium-bearing liquid sank back down them.

  8. The basins of the maria, read as external impacting bodies, require a foreign mass their melts do not carry (§J.7). Read in the literature’s own terms, the basins of the maria require about 1×10191\times10^{19} kg of foreign material; yet the melts of Imbrium match no known group of chondrites, and the bulk of the crust’s siderophile elements was in place before Imbrium and Serenitatis. What it means: the reading of the maria as basins dug by bodies from outside leaves a mass that has to be found; in the model there is no such mass to find, because the basins are openings and no foreign body was involved.

Taken together, the eight results describe one feed system seen in five records. The openings were exposed in an order set by the narrowing contact, each over its own feed dome; each floor froze as one level and sank over its dome; most far openings sealed early and were later covered by the layers of the far side; the near-side openings passed the Earth’s pressure while the Moon was attached, their ejections stopped at release, and their floors froze slowly afterwards; and the thorium marks the place of the last contact. The deciding tests are the buried domes, the floors over them, the iron rings and the foreign mass.

Further work — openings for research

The seven items below are where the next measurement will strengthen or test the picture. Each is stated as what must be measured; the engine, the rim ring and the order of sealing are also set out as a requirement or a prediction in Part M.

  1. The engine. The critical flux and the times of sealing of the openings, against the times of exposure. An opening seals when the flow of melt through it falls below the level at which the heat it brings no longer balances its cooling (§H.3). Computing that level, and setting the time each opening sealed against the time it was exposed, turns the condition of §I.6 into a number for each mare.

  2. The rim ring. Small craters on the rims against a measured volatile content, for every mare. Volatiles lightened the melt and drove out of the maria the material that built their margins (§H.2). The measurements showed no ring of small craters on every rim; the ring is expected only on the volatile-rich maria (§M.1). The test is the small craters on each rim, maria removed on both sides, against a measured volatile content (§F.3, §M.3).

  3. The last scatterer. Small craters on the smooth floors, rays and secondary fields removed, against distance from each candidate. The model names the Earth as the last scatterer (§F.6); a count by distance from each candidate source would show whether any other source left a gradient on the floors.

  4. The isotopes of the basins. Ruthenium and osmium isotopes in the melts of each basin of the maria. These isotopes tell a foreign body from material of the Earth–Moon system; measured basin by basin, they test directly whether any foreign mass is present (§J.7).

  5. The thorium at high resolution. The reconstructed thorium map at 26 km over the feed domes. The orbital map used here has cells of half a degree; a sharper map would show whether the thorium is lowest exactly over the channels, as §J.6 requires.

  6. The order of sealing. The youngest basalt of each mare against a centre fixed in advance, with thorium held fixed. If the contact maria sealed last, the youngest basalt should grow younger toward the contact centre (§K.2, §M.3).

  7. Rays and the cooling of the near-side floors (the author’s proposal). The near-side maria stayed liquid for a long time, warmed by the Moon’s internal heat and by the Earth’s heat; the basalt plains, such as Oceanus Procellarum, where Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus lie, were exposed to the same great heat of the Earth and are in this very similar to the mare floors. Material that landed on these floors while they were hot lost its record. The author proposes that rays form only when material lands on a floor that has already cooled; on a hot floor they do not form at all. The rays are a record within the ground beneath the surface, not material lying on it. As the floors cooled they came to carry rays: when they had cooled, Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus landed hard and left the spider-like rays; Diophantus and Delisle landed while the floor was not yet fully frozen and left no rays; many others landed on a hot floor and left no mark. The study: count the small and medium craters on the near side, on the maria and on the basalt plains, with and without rays. The author expects those without rays to outnumber those with rays, because the amount of material landing on the Moon fell as the Moon receded.

The author marked the openings by eye, as points on the gradients of the GRAIL Bouguer gravity field viewed in QuickMap, and numbered them 1 to 36; point 35 is Copernicus H and point 36 is Sinus Aestuum. These 36 marked points are the openings on which the statistics of the paper are made, as in the crustal test of §D.2 (Study 3). The catalogue lists the 34 maria. It keeps the numbers of the original marking, so the gaps in the sequence (2, 8, 14, 15, 22, 31) are marks found not to be maria and removed; the four maria numbered 37–40 were added later, after a review of the strong feed domes that had been left unmarked.

The columns. #: the number of the original marking. Opening: the name of the mare or basin; where there is no name, its position. Lat, Lon E: latitude and east longitude of the marked point, in degrees (south latitudes are negative; longitude is counted eastward from 0° to 360°). Sub-Earth: the angular distance from the sub-Earth point, the point of today’s Moon that faces the Earth; below 90° the opening lies on the near side, above it on the far side. Zone (gen.): the contact, the hill or beyond, with the generation of exposure, III, II or I (§I.4). Feed dome beneath: the identifier of the feed dome in the GRAIL field at degrees 6–660 and its diameter; “cooked under SPA” marks the maria over South Pole–Aitken whose dome was erased by the heat under that basin. Cover: what lies over the dome today, by colour — blue, under a mare (the dome is open, with basalt on it); amber, partial; red, buried (bright highland covers the dome); grey, over SPA.

What to look at. The blue rows gather in the contact and the hill; the red rows, the buried domes, lie mostly beyond, at sub-Earth distances above 90°. This is the result of §D.4 in one table: the far side holds openings as the near side does, but covered.
