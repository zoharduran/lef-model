11. Moon Births

One mechanism for the births of moons, tested planet by planet across the Solar System

Zohar Duran · ORCID 0009-0005-3194-9387 · 9 November 2026

Most moons of the Solar System orbit close to the equatorial plane of their parent, and many sit close to the corotation line, the height at which a body turning with the planet moves at orbital speed.

Abstract

The Moon was built from Earth's material, on the tip of a pear that the young, fast-spinning Earth raised out of itself, and it was released when its centre crossed the corotation line – the height at which a body rotating together with Earth moves at orbital velocity [Papers 2–3]. On Mars, a sharpening bulge raised volcanoes, and Phobos and Deimos were ejected from them [Paper 10]. This paper defines what the two cases have in common and formulates a uniform mechanism for the birth of moons: the conditions required for the process to take place on a planet, and the traces it leaves on the parent and on the moon.

The mechanism rests on two laws and two physical principles:

• The birth law: a moon is born when the parent's corotation line stands at a position that the mode of birth allows to be reached. The required distance is about 2 radii for a pear and a bulge, close to the surface for a volcano without a bulge, and about 1 for a split. The position of the line in radius units depends solely on the ratio between spin rate and density, and is therefore applied uniformly to all bodies. The birth day follows from this and depends only on the density of the parent.

• The birth window: the process is possible only at a defined stage in the life of the parent. The window is set by the stage of differentiation, not by a calendar measure of years.

• The pump principle: the differentiation process pushes water and light materials from the interior of the body toward the exit opening. The window stays open as long as the pump operates and has an outlet.

• The cap (lid) principle: the distance from the Sun set the amount of hydrogen and helium gathered before the disc dispersed. A thick gas envelope closes over the water and leaves the corotation line in place. A thin envelope allows the water to escape through the line, carry angular momentum and push the line outward.

Moons are born in three modes, set by the position of the parent in the solar system:

1. The inner edge: near the Sun, the parent dries quickly and holds no water for ejection. Earth's Moon is the only one built on the tip of a pear, and it is therefore dry and large relative to its parent (1.2%).

2. The central zone: from Mars to Neptune, the bodies are large and wet. The moons were ejected with large amounts of water, and they are very small relative to the parent (0.01%–0.02% in the gas and ice giants).

3. The outer edge: Pluto and Charon are one body that split. The united body spun faster than the elongation threshold, was stretched into a peanut shape, and its differentiation accelerated it beyond the separation threshold until it was cut through at its neck.

The differentiation process brings the corotation line closer to the surface: the parent contracts in its interior, spins up, and swells in its outer part. The composition of the material ejected in each birth is set by the stage of differentiation:

• At the start of the process: material is ejected that directly represents the composition of the parent.

• At the peak of the pump's activity: water-laden material is ejected that consolidates into a spongy body.

• At the end of the process: a dense, dry residue is ejected.

The mechanism is applied as a uniform protocol of 13 checks to all the planets and to five dwarf planets. Mercury and Venus lack several matching conditions, and are therefore moonless. Jupiter and Saturn rotate today within the birth range, and their thick gas envelope closes over the water. On Saturn, water that escapes is trapped in the rings and returns as ring rain, the ring being a continuous flow. Uranus and Neptune left the birth range because of water escaping through the line; their inner moons are located to this day within the birth range and are falling back.

In five bodies a fixed pattern recurs in which small moons are located on both sides of the corotation line. Moon density decreases as the distance from the Sun increases: the density of Earth's Moon is 3,344 kg/m³, the Galilean moons about 2,600 kg/m³, and Saturn's moons about 1,350 kg/m³. Uranus, which is almost twice as dense as Saturn, gives birth to denser moons. The chain of links is defined as: distance from the Sun →\rightarrow cap →\rightarrow parent density →\rightarrow moon density. On Pluto, the small moons were ejected from the equator before the split and moved away ahead of the line, which never caught up with them. The common-denominator matrix (Part L) shows no bodies that contradict the mechanism; it points to locations that require further research, and Part M gathers them as open questions.

What this paper proposes

• Claim: the birth of moons is a uniform process throughout the solar system. Fast spin of a soft, wet parent raises material out of it (as a pear, a bulge or the whole body) up to the corotation line, and the material that crosses the line becomes a moon. The differences between the systems arise from variation in density, spin, amount of water, stage of differentiation and distance from the Sun, not from separate mechanisms. These factors converge into a single law – the moon law (A.10).

PART AThe uniform mechanism

This part formulates the scientific mechanism against which all the planets are examined. The mechanism is built from five kinds of conditions: material, spin and geometry, the physical mechanism, survival, and geophysical signatures. These conditions are summarised by a single structural parameter and by the stages of differentiation that set the composition of the ejected material, and they converge into one physical law – the moon law. All the conditions are derived from two earlier test cases: Earth and the Moon [Papers 1–9] and Mars and its moons (Phobos and Deimos) [Paper 10].

A.1Introduction: from two cases to a rule

In both test cases an identical mechanical process took place in a uniform chronological sequence: a young planet, of low viscosity and high spin rate, raised inner material toward the corotation line. The material that crossed the line consolidated into a moon, while the parent continued its differentiation and slowed down, until the possibility of birth was blocked.

The difference between the cases comes down to the mechanical character of the construction:

• Earth: the Moon was built on the tip of a pear (Pear-shaped equilibrium figure). Material accumulated at the tip of the structure over time, the Moon swelled at its site of formation, and it detached when its centre of mass crossed the corotation line [Paper 3]. The mass of the original Moon is 1.2% of Earth's mass.

• Mars: the bulge raised volcanic structures, and from them the moons Phobos and Deimos were ejected through a point-injection mechanism [Paper 10]. For this reason, the moons of Mars are tiny relative to the parent.

The formulated mechanism is tested as a general law: a planet that satisfies the required set of conditions will give birth to a moon, whereas a planet that does not satisfy them will remain moonless. A planet that gave birth to a moon will display the defined structural and dynamical signatures.

A.2Material conditions

• Material sorted outward: the material that rises in the pear structure or is ejected from the volcanic structure is light sialic and silicate material, pushed outward in the planet's sorting process. In the outer solar system, ice joins this component. The moon is composed mainly of this material, but contains remnants of heavy elements (iron and metals) at a concentration that depends on the parent's stage of differentiation at the moment of birth. Earth's Moon has an iron core of between 1% and 2% of its mass [Paper 3].

• Partial sorting of the parent: a parent that has completed differentiation cannot give birth to moons. In a state of full differentiation, the core and the layers are rigidly organised (heavy material inside and light outside) and the viscosity is high, which prevents the internal hydrodynamic motion needed to form a pear. Birth requires a stage in which differentiation is active but has not yet reached its end.

• Water and low viscosity: the presence of water lowers the viscosity of the material and the overall density of the parent. Low viscosity allows stretching into a pear structure or flow in volcanic conduits, whereas viscous, rigid material prevents this.

• Low density: a low-density body has a larger radius for a given mass. As a result, surface gravity is weaker, and the surface is geometrically close to the corotation line.

This conservation allows the original spin rate of the parent to be reconstructed backward (protocol check number 3).

A.4Mechanism conditions

• Mechanics of the pear and the bulge: the formation of a structural elongation in the parent is a necessary condition for the birth of a moon. Fast axial spin pushes mass toward the geometric equator; the region in which most of the mass is concentrated is the most affected by the spin, and there the elongation begins to develop. This process continues as long as gravity at the equator is weakened by oblateness and a high spin rate. The elongation recedes when the parent slows, cools, and increases its viscosity as differentiation advances [Papers 3, 10].

• Crossing the corotation line: material that does not cross the corotation line collapses back onto the parent at the end of the process. Physical birth is defined at the exact moment when the centre of mass of the consolidating body crosses the corotation line; at this point, the centrifugal force combined with internal hydrostatic pressure tears the body away from the parent [Paper 3]. The remaining material left below the line is gathered back into the interior of the planet.

• Dynamics of the structural race: development of the pear structure carries angular momentum outward and slows the planet's spin. The slowing of the spin in turn moves the corotation line outward beyond the reach of the body. Birth requires a strict race condition: the radial growth rate of the pear must exceed the rate at which the corotation line recedes. Earth's Moon was released because its rate of construction met this condition; had the pear been more massive or built more slowly, the line would have moved beyond the tip of the structure and birth would have been blocked [Paper 2].

• The structural birth window: birth is restricted to a defined geological stage, set by the parent's degree of differentiation and not by chronological age. The window opens when the planet is hot, of low viscosity, not fully sorted and spinning fast (on Earth the process took place several hundred million years after the start of accretion). The window closes in advanced stages of differentiation, with the organisation of the core, the rise in viscosity, the escape of water and the rise in mean density. The length of the window in years varies from body to body: bodies that dried quickly (Mercury and Venus) closed the window early in chronological terms, whereas the gas and ice giants evolved slowly and kept a long birth window.

• The hydrodynamic pump principle: the sinking of dense materials into the planet's core pushes water and light silicate materials upward from the depths of the closed body. These materials concentrate at the exit openings – such as the volcanic structures on the bulge or the tip of the pear. These openings feed the birth of moons and rings, and through them mass carrying angular momentum is ejected outward. The pump is active as long as differentiation continues and the static crust is not thick enough to block its outlet. On Mars, the separation of metals into the core lasted only a few million years [Dauphas & Pourmand 2011], but structural differentiation and the hydrodynamic pump continued to operate until the end of the wet period, about 3.7–3.5 billion years ago, a time that matches the position of Phobos at the end of the ring stage.

• The atmospheric cap principle: the gas giants captured a hydrogen and helium envelope from the protoplanetary disc before it was dispersed by solar radiation [Haisch et al. 2001]. Capture of the gas envelope became possible only after the inner core body reached a critical mass [Pollack et al. 1996]. Because of their accretion distances, Jupiter (5.2 AU5.2 \text{ AU}) accumulated the most massive envelope, Saturn (9.5 AU9.5 \text{ AU}) a smaller envelope, whereas Uranus and Neptune (19 AU19 \text{ AU} and 30 AU30 \text{ AU}) captured a gas envelope amounting to only 10%–20% of their total mass. The gas envelope acts as a thermodynamic cap over the water: water (molecular weight 18) is heavier than the gas surrounding it (mean molecular weight about 2.3), condenses in the cold atmospheric layers and descends as precipitation into the body's interior (on Jupiter, water clouds are identified at a pressure of 5–7 bar [Li et al. 2020]).

A.5Contracting inside, swelling outside: how differentiation brings the line closer

• The paradox of global swelling: the birth window is set solely by the stage of differentiation (A.4). However, the simplistic assumption that "the swelling of the planet brought the surface closer to the corotation line" does not hold mechanically. A body expanding uniformly without losing angular momentum would necessarily reduce its axial spin rate (like a skater on ice spreading her arms), which would cause a rapid radial recession of the corotation line, at a rate higher than the rate at which the surface approaches.

• The mechanism of volumetric variation: the planet does not undergo homogeneous expansion. During structural differentiation, a massive contraction of the inner core takes place in parallel with the swelling of the outer layers, the central contraction being the mechanism that drives the ejection of the light materials. The dense components, which make up most of the body's mass, sink to the centre and accelerate the axial spin (like a skater drawing in her arms). At the same time, water and volatile components are pushed upward and swell the outer layer. This layer has a large volume but negligible mass, and it therefore increases the overall outer radius with almost no slowing of the axial spin. The dynamic combination of accelerated spin and the rise of the geometric surface brings about the meeting with the corotation line.

• Mathematical development: the angular momentum of the parent is defined by:

L=kMR2ΩL=kMR^{2}\Omega

where kk is the moment-of-inertia factor (for a body of uniform density k=0.4k = 0.4, and its value decreases as mass concentrates toward the centre). Under conservation of angular momentum, the spin rate satisfies:

Ω∝1kR2\Omega \propto \frac{1}{kR^{2}}

Substituting this relation into the equation for the position of the corotation line (A.3) yields the structural relation:

x∝(k2R)1/3x\propto (k^{2}R)^{1/3}

While the swelling of the outer layer increases the radius RR, the contraction of the inner core reduces the factor kk. Since kk appears squared, the effect of the geodynamic contraction is dominant and drives the approach of the line.

Geodynamic application of the contraction-and-swelling principle

• The Jupiter system: defines the mechanism of the chronological order of birth. As differentiation advances, the corotation line moves inward over time. As a result, moons formed at later stages consolidated on lines closer to the parent: Callisto first (on the most distant line), and Io last (on the closest line). The order of radial distance matches exactly the chronological order of birth. This process continues today, owing to the steady sinking of the dilute core (Part G).

• The Pluto–Charon system: structural differentiation pushed the united body beyond the hydrodynamic separation threshold (Rotational fission). At a fixed value of angular momentum, for a uniform homogeneous body (k=0.4k = 0.4) the axial spin period is 2.51 hours, a value lying just short of the separation threshold (2.44 hours). As differentiation advanced and the moment-of-inertia factor fell to k=0.31k = 0.31, the axial spin accelerated to 1.94 hours – a value that crosses the split threshold and causes the neck of the peanut structure to tear (K.1).

• Haumea (Haumea): structural prediction condition: continued inner contraction of the dwarf planet's body will accelerate its axial spin rate and bring the body closer to a hydrodynamic separation threshold (K.2).

• The Uranus and Neptune systems: since the birth stage, an inner contraction of the core has taken place, which by itself should have brought the corotation line inward. In practice, a recession of the line from 2R2R to a range of 3.2R−3.4R3.2R - 3.4R is identified. Hence the rate of water escape carried angular momentum outward with a strength that overcame the effect of inner contraction; therefore, an escape value of 2%–3% of the planet's mass is only a lower bound (Part I).

• Earth and Mars: the birth window opened in both bodies during a stage of intense differentiation: the stage of iron-core formation on Earth, and the stage of hydrodynamic pump activity on Mars. At this stage the corotation line came as close as possible to the surface. The physical principle does not change the calculated numerical values, but validates the geological timing.

A.6Survival conditions

• The dynamical Roche limit: within the Roche limit, the tidal forces exerted by the parent exceed in strength the self-gravity component of a given mass. This condition entirely prevents the hydrostatic consolidation of new moons, and causes the mechanical break-up of any body that crosses the limit inward. The planetary ring systems of the gas and ice giants are all located within this dynamical region.

• The role of planetary rings: a ring system is a site of residence and continuous mechanical erosion, not a site of moon formation (accretion). Moons found today near ring systems represent bodies that migrated dynamically out of the ring's range (inward or outward). The planetary ring acts as an accompanying mechanism that straightens and circularises the eccentricity of the moon's orbit, and is able to drive a radial push outward, as was carried out for Deimos in the Mars system [Paper 10]. The primary mass-ejection mechanism is driven by the pear or bulge structure, not by the ring. In situations where water components leak from the parent into the surroundings of the corotation line and are trapped within the Roche limit, the ring is a continuous dynamic mass flow, renewed as long as the ejection pump is active [Part H].

• Hill radius and the influence of the Sun: each planetary body has a closed gravitational region in which its gravitational field dominates over that of the Sun – the Hill radius (rHr_{H}), calculated as:

rH=a(M3M⊙)1/3r_{H}=a\left(\frac{M}{3M_{\odot }}\right)^{1/3}

where aa is the semi-major axis of the planet's orbit, MM its mass, and M⊙M_{\odot } the mass of the Sun. Close to the Sun, the Hill radius shrinks significantly. In addition, the axial tidal forces exerted by the Sun slow the planet's spin and move the corotation line radially outward. When the corotation line crosses the boundary of the Hill radius, every potential moon orbit lies below the line, which forces inward orbital migration and collapse onto the parent [Ward & Reid 1973; Burns 1973].

• Direction of tidal migration:

• Moon below the corotation line: the orbital velocity is higher than the spin rate of the parent →\rightarrow the tidal bulge induced on the parent lags behind the moon and exerts a braking force →\rightarrow loss of orbital energy and radial migration inward.

• Moon above the corotation line: the orbital velocity is lower than the spin rate of the parent →\rightarrow the tidal bulge induced on the parent leads the moon and accelerates it →\rightarrow gain of orbital energy and radial migration outward.

• Tidal locking and internal structure: a moon consolidating on the tip of the pear structure is released in a state of strict, full tidal locking (synchronisation in which the orbital period equals the spin period). Moons ejected from a volcano can become locked. A.10The moon law

All the dynamical conditions, thermodynamic principles and geophysical signatures merge into a unifying physical law. A moon consolidates from the parent's material on the corotation line, within a window of opportunity defined by the stage of planetary differentiation. The full set of the moon's physical properties is set at three distinct points in time: before birth, at the moment of birth, and in the dynamical stage that follows.

A. Preconditions for birth

1. The mechanical position criterion: birth depends on the corotation line standing at the radial position matching the hydrodynamic mode of construction: about 2 radii (1.75R−2.3R1.75R - 2.3R) for a pear and bulge structure, in the range 1.1R−1.5R1.1R - 1.5R for a volcanic mechanism without a bulge, and about 1 radius for a full split. Since the position of the line in radius units is set solely by the physical ratio between spin rate and mean density (Ω2πGρ\frac{\Omega ^{2}}{\pi G\rho }), a uniform ruler of thresholds applies to all celestial bodies.

2. The birth-window criterion: the time at which the birth window opens and closes is dictated solely by the parent's internal stage of differentiation, not by a static chronological measure of years.

3. The hydrodynamic pump mechanism: the differentiation process pushes water and light silicate components from the depths of the planet to the geological ejection opening. At the same time, contraction of the dense core accelerates the axial spin, while the ejection of volatiles swells the outer envelope; this combination mechanically brings the corotation line closer to the surface. Birth is functional as long as the pump is active and the outlet is open.

4. The Sun as a thermodynamic clock (the arc of births): birth requires active differentiation, a time window, a surface that is not sealed, and a subsurface layer of low viscosity. These factors depend on the radial distance from the Sun and produce an arc function:

• At the inner edge (close to the Sun): the parent undergoes rapid thermal drying that blocks the birth window at an early stage. Mercury and Venus did not give birth to moons; Earth gave birth to a single moon only because its position allowed prolonged accumulation of material on the tip of a pear.

• In the central zone: moving away from the Sun, the parents remain of low density, rich in water, and eject a larger number of moons, up to the peak in the Saturn system.

• At the outer edge (beyond Saturn): low temperatures cause the surface to freeze completely, and a rigid, frozen crust reduces the rate of moon ejection [L.4].

B. The mechanical mode of birth

5. Radial lifting mechanism: the planet's material is raised to the corotation line by means of a global pear structure, a bulge, or the cutting through of the whole body. Where the corotation line is close to the surface (x≈1.1−1.5x \approx 1.1-1.5), point volcanic activity suffices for ejection.

6. Morphological division by position in the system:

• Built: occurs at the inner edge only (Earth's Moon, a single case).

• Ejected: occurs in the central zone, from Mars to Neptune.

• Split: occurs at the outer edge, for small, cold bodies spinning beyond the break-up threshold (Pluto and Charon).

7. Dependence of composition on the stage of differentiation: the composition of the ejected mass is dictated by the geodynamic state of the parent:

• At stage 0 (onset of the process, wet parent): a mass of water is ejected from the outer layer by the centrifugal spray effect, and consolidates into a low-density icy moon only if it is located above the Roche limit.

• At stage 1 (early): mixed material representing the overall composition of the parent is ejected.

• At stage 2 (peak of the pump): material saturated with water and volatiles is ejected, leaving a moon of high structural sponginess after the gases escape.

• At stage 3 (end): a dense, dry silicate residue is ejected.

C. Structural properties of the moon

8. Density: reflects exactly the composition of the material at the ejection opening at the moment of birth. This value is compared with the parent's inner component, subtracting the volume of the atmospheric gas envelope. The thickness of the atmospheric cap sets the water content remaining in the parent's upper layer, and hence the density of the moon. At the end of the process, the parent is always denser than its moons (in comparison with its inner body, without the gas envelope).

9. Sponginess (Macro-porosity): rapid escape of water and volatile components after the ejection stage leaves massive internal voids in the body of the moon.

10. Geometric morphology: the dimensions of the mass and the length of the liquid/soft stage determine whether the body will reach hydrostatic equilibrium and become round.

11. Spin dynamics: moons formed by construction or splitting are born in a state of full tidal locking. Ejected moons can become locked. The existence of an independent spin requires an active, sorted core within the moon, which distributes the mass symmetrically.

12. Primordial orbital parameters: the moon moves in the direction of the parent's axial spin, in the plane of its geometric equator, and on an orbit with eccentricity close to zero (almost circular).

D. The post-birth dynamical evolution stage

13. Radial recession of the corotation line: the escape of water and volatiles through the region of the line carries essential angular momentum out of the system. As a result, the parent's spin rate decreases, and the corotation line is pushed radially outward at a declining rate (fast at first and slower later). Moons left below the migrating corotation line collapse back onto the parent; moons located above the line undergo tidal migration outward.

14. Integration of the atmospheric cap: a thick gas envelope traps the water inside and keeps a corotation line that hardly moves. A thin gas envelope allows free escape of water, which drives the recession of the line.

15. Interaction with ring systems: planetary rings are not a site of moon accretion, but a mechanical erosion mechanism that breaks them down and polishes them. The ring is able to accompany a moon, circularise its orbit and push it radially outward. Under continuous leakage, the ring is a constant mass flow that returns to the parent as precipitation ("ring rain").

16. The feedback effect of the moon's mass: a moon with a massive mass ratio relative to the parent causes a marked slowing of the parent's spin and a massive displacement of the corotation line (as in the Earth–Moon system). A tiny moon does not affect the parent's spin, and any displacement of the line arises solely from the escape of volatile mass (Mars and the gas giants).

The model of the ring as a continuous flow in thermodynamic equilibrium

Since Saturn keeps a spin rate within the birth range and differentiation in the core continues, the hydrodynamic pump is active. The atmospheric cap (70%–85% of the planet's mass) closes over the water but is not completely sealed, and a little water leaks to the region of the corotation line, where the mass is trapped within the Roche limit and feeds the ring.

At this rate of mass removal, without continuous supply, the ring system would break up within a few hundred million years, a finding that led the scientific literature to the assumption that the rings are chronologically young.

According to the uniform mechanism, the ring is not a static accretion remnant but a dynamical system of continuous flow in equilibrium (Steady-state flow): it is continuously renewed by the hydrodynamic pump from below, and removes mass as rain from above. Strict mechanical requirement in the steady state: the rate of radial water leakage from the parent to the ring is exactly equal to the rate of mass removal by ring rain; this mechanism explains why the ring displays the thermodynamic signature of a young body even though the geodynamic process is ancient. Alternative hypotheses of a primordial moon that crossed the Roche limit and broke up mechanically [Wisdom et al. 2022] agree with the model's statement that the ring is a site of destruction and erosion and not a site of construction.

• Miranda (born last, on a close orbit): ejected at stage 2 (peak of the hydrodynamic pump's activity). The body formed as a magmatic mass saturated with free water, which underwent rapid post-birth escape of volatiles that left high structural sponginess and low density (1,200 kg/m³).

• Inter-system comparison (the two-pot model): the moons of Uranus are markedly denser than the moons of Saturn (mean silicate mass fraction f = 0.49 versus f = 0.36, [A.9]). The thin atmospheric cap of Uranus allowed free escape of water, which led to the drying and thickening of the parent's upper ejection layer (Figure A.5); therefore, the material scooped from the opening that consolidated into moons was poor in water and dense in mass compared with the saturated material trapped beneath Saturn's thick cap.

B. Morphological signatures of ejection under hydrostatic pressure (grooves and bands)

A planetary body injecting viscous material from a narrow volcanic opening under internal pressure leaves defined mechanical signatures on the surface of the resulting moon:

1. Straight (linear) directional ejection: the mass is pushed along a straight line without its own axial spin → formation of an array of parallel grooves (Grooves) along the geometric direction of stretching.

2. Rotational ejection (mechanical screw mechanism): the mass is pushed outward while undergoing axial rotation against the walls of the narrow opening → grooves wound around the body, and at the end rings around a centre.

The grooves of Phobos, arranged in parallel planes that cross one another, and the bands of Miranda, which surround a central block (Coronae), match a forced exit from a narrow opening. In the literature the grooves of Phobos are explained as material thrown from craters on Mars [Murray & Heggie 2014], and the bands of Miranda as material that rose from within [Pappalardo et al. 1997].

Both bodies (Phobos and Miranda) have small dimensions and low density, and both represent a cross-section of the peak of the hydrodynamic pump's activity at stage 2 of the parent.

Vector analysis of the water-escape torque and the axial tilt

The mechanical analysis rules out the hypothesis that the water-escape mechanism can be the dynamical cause of the extreme axial tilt of Uranus (98°):

• The primordial angular momentum at the moment of birth is defined as a rigid vector (fast axial spin of 8.3 hours, marked as a blue arrow, Figure I.2).

• The present angular momentum is only 48% of the primordial value and is tilted at an angle of 98° (marked as a black arrow).

• For the transition of the system between the two states to be carried out solely by the mass of ejected water, the residual angular-momentum vector of the lost water would have to be 117% of the primordial value, at a tilt angle of 24° relative to the geometric axis (marked as a dashed red arrow).

• Functional mechanical block: water ejected through volcanic openings on the geometric equator, and at the height of the corotation line, can carry with it only angular momentum exactly parallel to the planet's axis of spin (Lescaped∥ΩL_{\text{escaped}} \parallel \Omega). Hence, water escape fully explains the slowing of Uranus, but the question of the origin of the extreme axial tilt of 98° remains an open structural question for calculation (Part M).

I.1The 98° tilt: possibilities examined

Uranus's spin axis is tilted by 98° from its orbital plane, and all its regular moons orbit in the plane of the tilted equator. This section gathers the dynamical possibilities examined during the research for this paper, the vector calculations carried out, and the competing hypotheses in the scientific literature.

• The mechanical principle for changing axial tilt: the angular momentum of a rotating body is defined as a rigid vector in space (having magnitude and direction), and the direction of the axis in space matches the direction of the vector. Changing the tilt angle requires changing the direction of the vector, achieved by adding or removing momentum in a direction not parallel to the axis, or by applying a sustained external torque (Torque). A change in the magnitude of the vector alone (an axisymmetric slowing or spin-up mechanism) changes the spin rate but does not rotate the axis.

Analysis of the dynamical possibilities and mechanisms

Possibility 1: axisymmetric water escape through the corotation line

At the moment of birth the spin period was about 8.3 hours, and it slowed to 17.2 hours in the present era; Uranus today keeps only 48% of its primordial momentum. A vector transition between the momentum states requires removal of a residual vector of magnitude 117% of the primordial momentum, at a tilt angle of 24° relative to the spin axis (Figure I.2).

• Mechanical block: water ejected through the openings of the hydrodynamic pump at the equator carries angular momentum exactly parallel to the axis of spin only. This mechanism shortens the length of the vector (axial slowing) but does not change its spatial direction. The possibility is rejected.

Possibility 2: one-sided asymmetric mass ejection

Concentrated ejection of material from a single geographic point located outside the plane of the equator and in a defined direction (like a rocket engine attached to the side of the body) can carry momentum in a direction not parallel to the axis. Possibility 3: displacement of the geometric centre of mass

When a moon is ejected, the parent undergoes a mechanical recoil in the opposite direction. The displacement of the body's centre from the common centre of mass is defined by the equation: Δr=mmoonMUranus⋅amoon\Delta r = \frac{m_{\text{moon}}}{M_{\text{Uranus}}} \cdot a_{\text{moon}}. For Titania the calculation yields Δr ≈ 17 km, for Oberon Δr ≈ 21 km, and for all the moons a few tens of kilometres (negligible compared with a planetary radius of 25,559 km). The spin axis always passes through the centre of mass and is displaced with it, but its spatial direction does not change. An asymmetric distribution of mass forces the body to roll within itself (True Polar Wander), but does not rotate the momentum vector in space. The mechanism alone does not tilt the axis, but it sets a requirement: if the exit of moons is to affect the axis, the moons of Uranus were in the past much heavier than they are today.

The removal of the water into space breaks the resonance and fixes the final angle. The moons are dragged adiabatically with the plane of the equator and lock their orbits within it. The mechanism fits the model and requires a specific dynamical calculation.

Possibility 5: a primordial moon that underwent inward tidal collapse

According to the model of Saillenfest and colleagues (2022), the presence of a primordial moon with a mass of at least 0.04% of Uranus's mass (twice the Titan–Saturn mass ratio) migrating outward beyond 10R accelerates the precession frequency until resonance is reached and tilts Uranus beyond 80°. At this stage the orbit loses dynamical stability, and the moon collapses and falls inward into the parent, which causes the tilt angle to freeze immediately.

Within the uniform mechanism, this hypothesis is a particular case of possibility 4, and provides a physical explanation for the fate of the body: the moon fell back after the receding corotation line crossed its orbit, like the dynamics of the 10 inner moons today. Summary and empirical tests for verification

The uniform model does not decide finally the question of the origin of the axial tilt, but it establishes two clear geophysical conclusions: the water-escape mechanism (which explains the slowing of the axial spin and the recession of the corotation line) cannot tilt the direction of the axis; by contrast, structural changes in mass distribution (the presence of water and moons on distant orbits in the plane of the equator) control the precession frequency with a strength much greater than that of the planet's inner body, and are therefore the most natural physical mechanism within the model.

The set of tests and predictions for verifying the model:

1. Mapping of the gravity field (deep gravimetry): a massive primordial moon that underwent mechanical collapse into the interior of a soft parent (possibility 5) left a defined asymmetric gravity anomaly – an excess of mass at the equator on one side.

2. Calculation of the historical precession rate: running the differential equation of motion along the chronology of births and water escape, for mathematical validation of the crossing of the resonance window and of the residence time required to reach an angle of 98°.

Uranus preserves the pattern of both sides of the line and the morphological signature of tidal locking. The inner moons falling today validate geometrically the position of the corotation line at the moment of birth, and the water escape establishes its radial recession, while the question of the mechanism of the 98° axial tilt is defined as an open problem for further calculation (Part M).

Triton: mechanical analysis of a body captured from the Kuiper belt

A. The dynamical and chemical parameters

Triton orbits Neptune on a reverse orbit (Retrograde) at a constant orbital velocity, with an extreme orbital inclination of 157° (equivalent to an angle of only 23° from the plane of the planetary equator, but in the reverse direction, Figure J.1). The orbit is perfectly circular at a radius of 14.4R.

The body has a physical radius of 1,353 km and a mass of 2.14×1022 kg2.14 \times 10^{22} \text{ kg} – a value amounting to 0.021% of Neptune's mass (a mass ratio on the scale of Titan–Saturn, 0.024%). Its dimensions and mass are greater than those of Pluto. The surface is composed of nitrogen, methane and carbon monoxide ice, and shows cryovolcanic activity (nitrogen geysers, [Smith et al. 1989]). Its mean density is high, at 2,061 kg/m³.

Since the birth law requires that a moon released from the parent move in the direction of its axial spin and in the plane of its geometric equator, Triton is not an original moon of Neptune.

B. Reconstruction of the body's origin (the model of a lobe of a peanut that split)

Comparative analysis of the body's mass and density rules out the possibility that Triton is a moon pushed from the Uranus system (heavier than all the moons of Uranus together, 6 times Titania) or from the Pluto system (heavier than Pluto). The body fits exactly as a massive member of a binary body system from the Kuiper belt, which was captured dynamically by Neptune's gravitational field while its partner was cut off and thrown into space [Agnor & Hamilton 2006].

According to the uniform mechanism, this binary system formed at the outer edge of the solar system through the third mode of birth – a full hydrodynamic split (Fission) [A.4, K.1]:

• The primordial united body underwent intense differentiation (A.5): the dense silicate and metallic material sank to the system's centre of mass, located structurally within the large lobe of the two-lobed peanut structure.

• As the spin rate rose beyond the break-up threshold, the geometric neck was cut through outside the global centre of mass.

• As a result, the large lobe (Triton) captured and kept most of the dense silicate and metallic core, while the small lobe left as a body rich in ice and volatiles and of low density. Triton represents the large lobe of a primordial peanut system that was more massive than Pluto–Charon and underwent more advanced differentiation, which makes it denser and more massive than Pluto.

The capture of Triton caused disruption and dynamical collapse of Neptune's original moon system [Goldreich et al. 1989], a mechanism that explains the complete absence of medium-sized regular moons in the present system.

K.1Pluto and Charon: the peanut that split

Charon amounts to 12% of Pluto's mass [Brozović et al. 2015], the highest mass ratio in the solar system for a planetary moon (fraction of the system's mass: Pluto 89%, Charon 11%). According to the uniform mechanism, Pluto and Charon were a single united body – a two-lobed peanut structure (Contact binary) – which underwent a full hydrodynamic split (Rotational fission) at its geometric neck. Two-lobed bodies of this kind are structurally common in the Kuiper belt, as seen in the geometric structure of the body Arrokoth [Stern et al. 2019].

2. The mechanical separation threshold (P≤2.44 hoursP \le 2.44 \text{ hours}): the equatorial velocity equals orbital velocity (x=1x = 1), and the body is torn apart.

B. Mechanics of the morphological fracture on Charon

Pluto is located in the frozen zone of the planetary arc of births [L.4], a structure of a soft subsurface core blocked beneath a static frozen crust. The crust showed elastic resistance to the rotational tensile stresses; as inner contraction advanced and the spin rate rose, the outer crust cracked at the neck all at once by a mechanical fracture mechanism (Brittle failure). The hydrostatic pressure in Pluto's core (about 0.7 GPa) is 700 times higher than the maximum tensile strength of ice, and therefore the fracture was limited to the outer crustal layer and released the tensile stresses at the mechanical threshold:

• The Oz Terra region (northern hemisphere): has a rough, ancient topography (geological age > 4 billion years), which carries the signature of the original crust of the peanut stage.

• The Vulcan Planitia plain (equatorial region): a completely smooth plain representing the soft inner material exposed at the moment of fracture. The mass flowed and rounded back to hydrostatic equilibrium, a stretching process that smoothed the surface. The isolated mountain structures lying in depressions in the plain are blocks trapped within the soft matrix during the stretching. The plain is located exactly on the equator, in the hemisphere permanently facing Pluto (longitude orientation 285° to 30° [Beyer et al. 2017]), the site of the primordial neck.

• The tectonic canyon belt: the line of ridges and rifts separating the two geological units is, according to the model, the mechanical fracture line of the split. In the literature the belt appears to encircle the whole of Charon, and is explained as extension while an internal ocean froze [Beyer et al. 2021].

C. The orbital dynamics of the four small moons

The four small moons – Styx, Nix, Kerberos and Hydra – were ejected directly from the equatorial and neck region of the primordial peanut before the final split stage, when the spin rate reached the separation threshold and the corotation line was locked at the surface. In this dynamical configuration, the equator loses mass by itself owing to centrifugal forces, without the need for a point volcanic pump. The moons were released on circular orbits in the plane of the geometric equator.

The empirical measurements of the New Horizons mission validate this mechanism [Weaver et al. 2016]:

• The orbits are perfectly circular and in the plane of Charon's synchronous orbit.

• The bodies are tiny and not in hydrostatic equilibrium (asymmetric).

• The surface albedo is extremely high, made of clean water ice, a signature of wet inner material ejected into space.

• The orbital periods show orbital resonance ratios (Resonance) close to 1:3, 1:4, 1:5 and 1:6 relative to Charon's orbital period, with Styx, Nix and Hydra locked in a strict three-body resonance (Laplace-like resonance) [Showalter & Hamilton 2015].

D. The two-stage model for the migration of the small moons

From the moment of their detachment, the small moons were placed above the dynamical corotation line and had a positive direction of migration (they receded radially outward). The recession mechanism operated in two successive stages:

Stage A: push by the primordial peanut (quadrupole moment) → Stage B: global tidal push (soft Pluto body)

• Stage A (push by the peanut): an elongated two-lobed body spinning at high speed produces a massive asymmetric gravitational potential field (a strong quadrupole moment), which exerts an outward pushing force several orders of magnitude greater than classical tidal forces at the birth sites.

• Stage B (global tidal push): as the split advanced, the push was converted into the tidal forces of young Pluto. The strength of the push declines sharply as a function of distance (a˙∝a−11/2\dot{a} \propto a^{-11/2}); doubling the distance reduces the displacing force by a factor of about 45. Close to the surface, for a body of high mechanical softness (k2/Q≈0.1k_2/Q \approx 0.1), the migration rate was hundreds of metres per year, and declined to zero in the modern era.

Time integration of stage B alone (assuming a soft Pluto throughout the whole geological period) moves Nix and Hydra to a radial distance of 17R - 23R within 4.5 billion years. This position passes the stopping point of the present corotation line (16.5R16.5R, fixed by the locking of Pluto's spin to Charon's orbit), and it is thus proven dynamically that the corotation line never caught up with the small moons. Since the bodies are today in the range 36R - 55R, a strict mechanical requirement is set: the quadrupole pushing torque of the peanut structure in stage A closed a gap of a factor of 2 in orbital distance. Hypotheses of orbital dragging by Charon [Ward & Canup 2006] are rejected, since this mechanism increases orbital eccentricity, contrary to the circular orbits of the present era [Cheng et al. 2014].

K.6Dwarf or moon?

The dynamical analysis of the outer edge of the solar system points to a duality of definition (Dual nature) for the celestial bodies in the dwarf-planet category:

• Evolutionary path A: an independent body becomes a moon through gravitational capture (Capture). This mechanism is established empirically in the Neptune system, where the geophysical and orbital data define Triton as a dwarf planet from the Kuiper belt captured from outside.

• Evolutionary path B: an independent body acts as a parent and gives birth to a moon system of its own. Pluto and Eris (Eris) represent this mechanism, and behave physically as parents in every respect.

The theoretical concern that some of the dwarf planets recognised today are no more than primordial moons that underwent orbital escape (Escaped moons) or secondary capture is mechanically justified. Establishing absolute geodynamic criteria for distinguishing between an original dwarf planet and a primordial moon that escaped is defined as an open structural problem (Part M).

The unifying part brings all the celestial bodies examined in the solar system together under one parametric matrix, in order to validate the uniform mechanism and present the recurring dynamical signatures.

L.1Moons on both sides of the line

In five planetary systems of the solar system – Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune – a uniform dynamical anomaly is identified: a group of small regular moons located in the immediate vicinity of the parent's present corotation line, distributed on both sides of it (Figure L.1):

• The sub-synchronous group (below the line): has a negative direction of tidal migration and creeps in inward orbital retreat toward collapse onto the parent (green moons). In the Uranus and Neptune systems, these moons sit exactly within the primordial birth range (1.95R−3.0R1.95R - 3.0R).

• The super-synchronous group (above the line): has a positive direction of tidal migration and recedes radially outward (red moons).

This symmetric configuration is direct mechanical proof of the prediction of the moon law: the bodies were born synchronously on the corotation line, and underwent two-way dynamical dispersal according to the direction of tidal migration on the side on which they consolidated. On Uranus and Neptune, the sub-synchronous moons falling today fix the original geometric position of the corotation line at the moment of birth, before it was moved outward by the escape of water.

Where the line stood at the time of the births

The position of the line then was recomputed for each planet from conservation of angular momentum, with every parameter that sets it: gravity (the planet’s mass, including mass that left); the spin today; the radius and density at birth (Earth 7,157 km; Mars 1.5 times today’s radius; Jupiter and Saturn 1.1–1.4 and the ice giants 1.0–1.3 times today’s radius, following the calculations of giant-planet contraction [Fortney et al. 2007; Marley et al. 2007]); compressibility, through the moment-of-inertia factor (0.40 for unsorted material on Earth and Mars; for the giants, from today’s value to a less differentiated body, since gas compresses itself); rotational flattening (a fluid body whose shape is fully relaxed, Darwin–Radau method), which enlarges the moment of inertia and shifts the line; the orbital angular momentum of the regular moons (captured moons excluded); and the solar tide on Earth. Viscosity does not change the position of the line; it sets only how fast angular momentum is transferred and how fast the shape relaxes. The finding: on Jupiter and Saturn, conservation of angular momentum alone places the line in the birth range – in radii of that time the line hardly moved, as the cap principle expects (a thick gas cap, little leakage). On Mars, Uranus and Neptune, conservation alone leaves the line far from the birth range, and between a few percent and about ten percent of the mass had to leave through the line. On Earth the Moon carried most of the angular momentum; with rotational flattening included, the day with no loss is 6.7–6.9 hours (6.2 hours without flattening, Part C), and the small excess required for the model’s 5.77 hours is equivalent to 1.0%–1.8% of Earth’s mass leaving through the line – the scale of a primordial gas envelope.

Mercury and Venus

On Mercury and Venus the line stands today at 100 and 254 radii – beyond the planet’s sphere of control (the Hill sphere, 90 and 167 radii). Had a moon been born to them, it would have been left inside the line, descended and been swallowed. Their line then cannot be reconstructed: they have no moons, and their angular momentum went to the solar tide in an unknown amount. We propose that two factors slowed them. At first, while they spun fast and carried gas, mass that left through the line carried angular momentum away: stopping a young spin of 5–12 hours requires 1.5%–9.6% of the mass to leave – on Venus 50 to 330 times the mass of water in Earth’s oceans, and therefore mainly gas. Evidence of a large loss is Venus’s deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio, about 150 times Earth’s [Donahue et al. 1982]. Then the solar tide completed the slowing and set the final state: mass loss can only slow a spin to zero, whereas Mercury’s 3:2 lock and Venus’s retrograde spin require the solar tide and the tide in Venus’s atmosphere [Correia & Laskar 2001]. The required fraction is similar to that of Uranus and Neptune: both ends of the Solar System lost a similar part of their mass through the line.

Why these two. Mercury and Venus are the only planets whose corotation line has left the planet’s sphere of control, and on exactly these two the spin is no longer the planet’s own: Mercury is locked at 3:2, and Venus spins backward. The line leaves the sphere of control exactly when the day is longer than about 58% of the year (Ω<3 n\Omega < \sqrt{3}\,n, where nn is the orbital rate around the Sun): the planet turns more slowly than it moves around the Sun. The accepted explanation: the Sun controls their spin because they are close to it, and its tidal force grows steeply as the distance shrinks. In the model, the reason is the recession of the line: the mass that left through the line slowed the spin and moved the line outward, and when the line left the planet’s sphere of control, the Sun took over the spin.

The line that moved

The picture joins into one chain (Figure L.1b): mass that left through the line – gas and water – carried angular momentum away; the spin slowed; the line moved outward; and when the line passed a moon that had been outside it, the moon was left inside the line and began to descend. A moon outside the line receives angular momentum and recedes, and a moon inside the line gives up angular momentum and descends – so the passing of the line reverses the moon’s direction of motion. This is how the sub-synchronous group formed: Phobos on Mars and the inner moons of Uranus and Neptune. On Jupiter and Saturn, where the gas cap stayed, the line hardly moved. The tide of the moons and of the Sun also slows the spin; the model adds to it the mass that left, as a factor that acted mainly in the young stage.

L.2The common-denominator matrix

The research matrix (Figure L.2) crosses 13 celestial bodies (planets and dwarf planets) with nine physical criteria of the uniform mechanism, and summarises the degree of empirical fit of the model to present reality.

Indicators of the system matrix:

• Green status (full fit): the measured parameters of the body match exactly the predictions of the moon law.

• Yellow status (partial fit / mechanical requirement): the body fits the model provided a strict dynamical requirement is met (such as loss of angular momentum on Mars and Uranus, or an external push of moons on Jupiter and Uranus), without physical contradiction.

• Red status (the condition is not met): applied to moonless bodies (Mercury, Venus), in which the absence of the criterion directly explains why birth did not occur, as expected from the arc of births.

• Grey status (unknown parameter): missing data not yet measured empirically (such as the density of the moon Proteus at Neptune).

Summary of the matrix findings and the radial evolution

The global analysis proves that the uniform mechanism is valid and continuous throughout the solar system, with no bodies that contradict the basic laws. The morphological variation between the systems reflects a continuous function of radial distance from the Sun, which defines the planetary arc of births (L.4) and the division into the three modes of birth:

Inner edge: construction mode (built) → Central zone: injection mode (ejected) → Outer edge: cutting mode (split)

1. At the inner edge (Mercury, Venus, Earth): the high rate of thermal loss causes rapid drying of the parent material. Mercury and Venus closed the geological birth window before the spin conditions were reached. Earth is located at the point where the length of the birth window allowed the unique, prolonged construction of the Moon on the tip of a pear, which dictates its high mass (1.2%) and its dry content.

2. In the central zone (Mars to Neptune): the bodies are massive and rich in water, and the hydrodynamic pump ejected relatively tiny moons (0.01%–0.02% in the giants), many of them water-rich, at successive stages of differentiation. The thickness of the atmospheric cap (dictated by the availability of gas in the protoplanetary disc) set the rate of water escape; on Jupiter and Saturn the thick cap trapped the water and left a corotation line that hardly moves, while on Uranus and Neptune the thin cap allowed free water escape that slowed the planets and moved the line outward.

3. At the outer edge (Pluto): the body is small and cold, and spun fast at that time. Internal differentiation accelerated it beyond the threshold, and brought about the split of the two-lobed structure (peanut mechanism) at its neck; Haumea has not yet split, with the dense core concentrated in the large lobe (Pluto, Triton).

The final validation column of the common-denominator matrix indicates full agreement of the geophysical picture with astronomical reality. The places where quantitative completion or exact empirical measurement is required are now isolated and gathered in the chapter of open questions (Part M).

L.3Who is examined: the rule for a captured moon

To prevent a methodological bias in which every celestial body that does not agree with the model's predictions would be arbitrarily classified as captured, a strict quantitative classification protocol was set in advance. A moon formed by the uniform mechanism on the corotation line must move in the direction of the parent's axial spin, in the plane of its geometric equator, on an orbit with eccentricity close to zero (almost circular), and be made of the parent's source material.

Of these four criteria, three (eccentricity, distance and composition) may change secondarily even for an original moon owing to complex dynamical interactions (orbital stretching by a neighbour, radial push by a ring, or ejection at different stages of differentiation). By contrast, the fourth sign – a reverse orbit (Retrograde) – cannot develop for an original moon in any mechanical configuration, since a parent does not eject mass against the vector of its own spin; it is therefore given a double quantitative weight.

The defined scoring protocol:

• Reverse orbit (orbital inclination >90∘>90^{\circ } from the effective Laplace plane) = 2 points.

• High orbital inclination in the normal direction of rotation = 1 point.

• High eccentricity (ellipticity) = 1 point.

• Extreme radial distance that cannot be reached from the birth range by a known mechanical push = 1 point.

• Different chemical composition of the whole body (excluding secondary accretion of a surface coating) = 1 point.

Cut-off criterion: a body accumulating 3 points or more is classified as captured.

Methodological note: the orbital inclination is measured relative to the reference surface of the Laplace plane (Laplace plane) and not to the plane of the planetary equator, since on distant orbits the gravitational field of the Sun shifts the stability of the orbit to a secondary reference plane. Earth's Moon, for example, is inclined 18∘−29∘18^\circ - 29^\circ from the plane of Earth's equator, but shows an inclination of only 5∘5^{\circ } from the Laplace plane.

L.4The arc of births

The formation of a moon depends on the simultaneous existence of four physical conditions in the parent: active differentiation, a time window, an open (not sealed) crust, and a subsurface layer of low viscosity. The radial distance from the Sun dictates the length of time during which these four conditions hold together, and produces a distribution function with the morphology of an arc.

• The inner thermal zone (Mercury, Venus): the high rate of thermal loss causes rapid drying of the outer envelope, an extreme rise in viscosity and early sealing of the crust, before the required axial spin thresholds are reached. Mercury and Venus are moonless as a direct result. In the modern era, the corotation line of both bodies extends beyond the active boundary of their Hill radius (Venus: 1.53×106 km1.53 \times 10^6 \text{ km} versus a Hill radius of 1.01×106 km1.01 \times 10^6 \text{ km}; Mercury: 242,900 km242,900 \text{ km} versus a Hill radius of 220,600 km220,600 \text{ km} owing to a spin period of 58.6 days), a configuration that entirely prevents orbital stability. Earth almost did not give birth: it was far enough from the Sun to allow the prolonged construction stage of the Moon on the tip of the pear, without which the material would not have reached independent orbital velocity and would have remained locked to the parent.

• The zone of peak pumping (Mars to Saturn): as the accretion distance from the Sun increases, the celestial bodies keep a low mean density and a high water content, which allow continuous ejection of mass. The rate of ejection of regular moons and their relative mass increase gradually, until the structural maximum is reached in the Saturn system.

• The outer icy zone (beyond Saturn): the low temperatures cause rapid freezing of the outer layers. A rigid, frozen crust mechanically blocks the geological ejection openings and reduces the efficiency of the hydrodynamic pump. Uranus showed a decline in the rate of ejection, and Neptune ejected only a tiny system. Pluto underwent a full hydrodynamic split in a primordial period when the core was hot and soft; in the present era the body is completely frozen and static.

To keep the analysis clean, the table and the vector model (Figure L.3) count regular moons only, entirely omitting captured bodies according to the criteria of L.3 (including Triton and Nereid). In the overall global count (including captured moons), the peak is kept in the Saturn system (Jupiter: about 97, Saturn: 274 or more, Uranus: 29, Neptune: 16), but most of the inventory in these catalogues represents secondary capture and is unrelated to the planetary birth mechanism.
