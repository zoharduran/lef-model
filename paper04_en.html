The environment from which the calculation starts

The paper begins at the end of the conjectured construction. In the LEF model, material from the Earth builds an attached body by feed from within, and the outer shell rises with it. The body sits as a squashed ball on the tip of the parent body’s pear-shaped structure: the width of the contact area shrinks from about 6,000 km to 3,000 km, and then the body detaches, rounded and rigid. Paper 2, LEF Mechanism in Principle, examines the angular-momentum budget, corotation and the release conditions; Paper 3, LEF Mechanism in Practice, deals with the construction and the connection. This paper takes over from the moment the Moon is released and evaluates the orbital dynamics from the release point to the present target state: the orbit that these initial conditions produce, and the requirements for its evolution into the present orbit.

Several rows of the table need explanation.

Conservation of mass. The initial mass, 6.0455×10246.0455\times10^{24} kg, is the mass of the Earth and the mass of the present Moon together: before the division the lunar material is part of the parent body, and during the separation it is removed from the parent’s reservoirs and passes to an independent body. The same mass is thus the Moon, and it is counted once. KK is the strength of the gravity that governs the motion of the two bodies about each other; it is computed from the sum of the two masses, because each body pulls the other.

Evolution of the spin. The 5.5-hour day belongs to the start of construction; at the moment of release the Earth day is 5.77 hours. The lengthening pear slowed the Earth, and the gathering of iron towards its centre (D=2D=2, the ratio of core density to mantle density) sped it up again (Paper 2).

Measuring distances. The present Earth radius, 6,371 km, serves only as a reference unit — a ruler for reporting distances — and does not reflect the dimensions of the young Earth, which was hot and larger.

The release latitude. Three places are named in the series, and they must be kept apart. The base of the pear — the place where the pear rose from the Earth’s surface — stood near Kiritimati, 1.9N 157.5W (Paper 3), roughly on the equator of that time; whether that equator is today’s is left to the study of the axes of the Earth–Moon system, in future work. The coordinate 25N 165E, also from Paper 3, is the back of the pear. The latitude that matters for the orbit is neither of these: it is the latitude of the centre of the 3,000-km contact area on which the Moon sat at the moment of release, at the tip of the pear: 22.5.

The target state. The last row is the target the model must reach: the Moon’s present mean distance aa, its eccentricity ee (how far the orbit departs from a circle; 0 is a perfect circle) and its inclination to the ecliptic, the plane of the Earth’s orbit round the Sun.

Higher gravitational moments. Gravity does not “split” into two laws; what changes during the transfer is the distribution of mass in space. Once the two masses are fixed, the motion of the centres in the monopole approximation — that is, treating each body as if all its mass were concentrated at its centre — is set by KK, while the reorganisation of the core acts mainly through the moment of inertia, the spin and the higher gravitational moments (the non-spherical departures of the field from that of a point, caused by the bulge and the internal layers). A connection of significant mass requires removing that mass from the other reservoirs and recomputing the system; it is not a free addition.

Abstract

The mechanism of formation and separation. In the LEF model the Moon was built from material of terrestrial origin while attached to the parent body, and only afterwards was it released into an independent orbit. This paper examines the orbital outcome of such a release.

A geometric origin of the initial inclination. The inclination of an orbit is the angle between the plane in which the body goes round and a reference plane — here, the Earth’s equator at that time. A body whose centre lies at latitude λ\lambda, and whose tangential velocity at the moment of release acts in the direction of rotation only, receives an initial inclination i0=|λ|i_0=|\lambda| relative to the equator of that time. Local release at an intermediate latitude is therefore a geometric source of the initial inclination.

Fixing the latitude. Two remarks fix what λ\lambda is. It is the latitude of the body’s centre at the moment of release, not of any point on its base: a body 3,000 km across has a single velocity vector, and the extent of its base does not spread the inclination. And it is the latitude of the centre of the 3,000-km contact area on which the Moon sat, at the tip of the pear: 22.5°, the value used throughout this paper. 25° serves as a sensitivity case: a higher latitude gives a larger initial inclination, and more of it must disappear on the way to the present 5.1°. Under the tan-law of Part D, the model’s release from 22.5° returns today’s target inclination, 5.145°, and release from 25° gives 5.79° (an earlier version, with release about 100 km beyond the line, gave 5.079° from 22.5°).

The release state. Combined with Paper 2, the release state is fixed: the Earth day at the moment of release is 5.77 hours (the lengthening pear and the centralising core act together), and the Moon’s centre lies at latitude 22.5. Off the equator the rotation carries the body at ωrcosλ\omega r\cos\lambda, so the body reaches orbital speed at rccos−2/3λ≃17,287r_c\cos^{-2/3}\lambda\simeq17{,}287 km (x=1.0542x=1.0542), the corotation line for its latitude. The Moon begins to separate when its centre of mass reaches this line, and is released about 210 km beyond it, at r0≃17,496r_0\simeq17{,}496 km (x=r0/rc=1.0669x=r_0/r_c=1.0669), when a little more than half of it has crossed the line; the contact resists little, and the body is pulled outward with force.

The first orbit. Detached at this point, the body enters a nearly circular orbit (e0≃0.04e_0\simeq0.04) inclined by 22.5 to the equator of that time, and the release point is its perigee. The tide pushes outward from the first moment, so the Moon recedes from the start. At the moment of release the Moon is round, with a rigid outer shell, and it lies about 7,200 km outside the rigid Roche limit, which stands at 10,314 km. The release distance is the point at which two reduced tests meet — the present inclination and the present eccentricity (Paper 3, §C.5a).

The follow-on tests. Tests of the Laplace-plane transition, of tidal dissipation and of the thermal history are then presented. The solution of the reduced decay equation refers to tani\tan i, not to sini\sin i; the explicit cancellation of Q⊕Q_\oplus in this equation does not cancel the dependence of the full orbit on the cooling rate and on resonances. The analysis establishes the geometric origin of the inclination and the mapping between the release conditions and the initial orbits. The next required step is a joint reconstruction of the present distance, inclination, eccentricity and recession rate, which remains to be computed.

The box makes two claims, and we present them here in plain terms. The mechanical source of the inclination. The first claim concerns direction. Material in corotation with the Earth at latitude 22.5° moves eastward along the local circle of latitude, parallel to the plane of the equator. On release, the ballistic path is dictated by the central gravitational field, so its plane must pass through the Earth’s centre of mass — that is where gravity pulls. The plane defined by the Earth’s centre of mass, the release point at latitude 22.5° north and the eastward tangential velocity vector gives an initial orbital inclination of 22.5° relative to the plane of the ancient equator. Nothing else is needed: mass, distance and spin rate do not enter the result. That is the meaning of the phrase “three lines of algebra, no parameter”: the result is defined in closed form in three lines (equations 1–3 of Part A), and it contains no number that could be tuned to fit. This mechanism differs from the disk picture of the impact model, in which the Moon forms from debris that spreads into a flat ring over the equator; there the body starts with an almost zero inclination, which requires a secondary dynamical mechanism to produce the present inclination.

Calibrating the local corotation line. The second claim concerns distance. In the plane of the equator, the corotation radius is the distance at which the tangential speed equals the circular orbital speed; for a 5.77-hour day it stands at 16,399 km. Off the equator, the distance to the spin axis is smaller by the factor cosλ\cos\lambda than the distance from the centre of mass, which reduces the linear speed carried by the rotation compared with an equatorial body at the same distance. Reaching equality with the orbital speed requires the local corotation radius to shift outward: at latitude 22.5° it stands at 17,287 km, 5.42% above the equatorial radius (x=1.0542x=1.0542). A hypothetical release exactly on the local corotation line would give a circular orbit with a neutral tidal interaction: the Moon’s orbital speed would equal exactly the component of the Earth’s spin acting along its orbit, so the tidal bulge the Moon raises on the Earth would be carried neither ahead nor behind, and would exert no push. The mechanical separation process begins when the Moon’s centre reaches this line, and the physical release takes place about 210 km beyond it, at a distance of 17,496 km. Immediate recession. At this distance the tangential speed is slightly higher than the speed required for a circular orbit at the same radius, so the release point is the perigee of a nearly circular orbit (e0≃0.037e_0\simeq0.037); and the Earth turns faster than the Moon goes round, so the tidal bulge is carried ahead, and its pull exerts on the Moon a positive tangential component that increases the semi-major axis from the moment of detachment. The Roche limit is the distance inside which the Earth’s tide would pull the body apart; for a body with a rigid outer shell it stands at 10,314 km, and the physical release point lies about 7,200 km outside this mechanical limit, which prevents tidal destruction of the body. The reduced decay law. The law tani∝a−1/2\tan i\propto a^{-1/2} describes the decay of the inclination as the orbit widens: the inclination decays from an initial value of 22.5° and reaches the observed value, 5.145°, at the present distance. Part D shows that this is a reduced law; the full convergence path — the Laplace-plane transition and the Cassini state, and a tidal dissipation that varies in time — is part of the detailed requirements in Part E.

PART A

Which inclination results at the moment of release?

THE PROBLEM AND THE SOLUTION

The analysis separates two distinct angles: the geographic latitude of the release point on the surface of the parent body, and the orbital inclination relative to the ecliptic. The inclination relative to the ancient equator is derived first; the transition to the present observational frame is then examined.

This part answers one question: at what angle is the Moon’s new orbit inclined at the moment of release? It first places the question relative to other models (A.1), then derives the answer (A.2), and finally separates three angles that are easily confused when the Earth’s axis and the Moon’s orbit are considered together (A.3). The passage from the ancient equator to the present ecliptic is the subject of Part C.

Defining the reference frames. Every orbit lies in a plane: the body goes round in it, and the Earth’s centre lies in it. The orbital inclination is the angle between this plane and a chosen reference plane. Two reference planes appear in the paper. The first is the plane of the Earth’s equator at the epoch of release — the natural reference at the initial stage, when the Moon has just left the turning Earth. The second is the ecliptic, the plane of the Earth’s orbit round the Sun, against which the Moon’s present inclination, 5.145°, is measured. The derivation uses an equivalent description, through the direction perpendicular to the orbital plane (the angular-momentum vector): the inclination is the angle between this perpendicular and the perpendicular to the reference plane — for the equator, the Earth’s spin axis.

A.1The context of other models

Formation from a disk close to the equatorial plane raises the question of the origin of the present inclination. In the family of giant-impact scenarios, the material thrown out by the collision settles into a disk over the Earth’s equator, and a Moon that grows from that disk orbits almost in the equatorial plane; the present inclination must be supplied at a later stage. The literature includes late dynamical excitation mechanisms, such as interactions with planetesimals [S2] and the coupled evolution of a system with high axial tilt [S3], so formation from a disk does not rule out late excitation of the inclination. The difference examined here is that LEF begins with a substantial inclination already at the moment of release, with no need for late excitation to produce the starting angle. In LEF the inclination is not an added event: it is a direct geometric source, and the inclination is set by the very location at which the body was attached on the surface of the turning Earth.

A.2The derivation

The geometric picture. Before the algebra, the picture. Imagine a merry-go-round whose middle stands at the Earth’s centre, and suppose that the only pull on any body near it is directed towards that central point. A stone is carried round on a ring fixed at a height above the middle, like a raised row of seats. While it is carried, the stone moves horizontally — along its ring, parallel to the floor. Now let it go. It leaves with this horizontal velocity, but the pull on it is directed towards the central point, downward and inward. Its free path must therefore lie in the one plane that contains both the central point and the stone’s velocity: a plane that runs from the stone, in its direction of motion, and dips down through the centre. That plane is tilted to the floor by exactly the angle at which the stone is seen from the centre, above the floor. A stone on a higher ring is seen at a steeper angle and enters a steeper orbit; a stone on the floor itself stays in the plane of the floor. For the Earth, the floor is the equator, the ring is the circle of latitude, and the angle at which the body is seen from the centre is its latitude λ\lambda. The equations below express the same thing in three lines.

Define the spin axis at the moment of release as the zz axis of a Cartesian coordinate system; the xx axis is chosen so that the body’s centre of mass lies in the xx–zz plane; the yy axis points east, in the direction of the tangential rotation at the body’s location. For an eastward corotation velocity, in an inertial frame (one that does not turn with the Earth) and with separation between the centres of mass, the position vector and the velocity vector are defined as follows:

𝐫=r(cosλ,0,sinλ),𝐯=ωr(0,cosλ,0).\mathbf r=r(\cos\lambda,0,\sin\lambda), \qquad \mathbf v=\omega r(0,\cos\lambda,0). \tag{1}

where 𝐫\mathbf r is the position of the body’s centre relative to the Earth’s centre, at distance rr and latitude λ\lambda; 𝐯\mathbf v is its velocity, and ω\omega the Earth’s spin rate. The body’s radius of rotation about the axis is rcosλr\cos\lambda, so the linear eastward speed carried by the rotation is ωrcosλ\omega r\cos\lambda; this is the speed that appears in the environment table.

The specific angular momentum and the kinematic identity. The specific angular momentum — the angular momentum per unit mass, 𝐡=𝐫×𝐯\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v — is a vector perpendicular both to 𝐫\mathbf r and to 𝐯\mathbf v, and therefore perpendicular to the orbital plane. Its direction is the orbit’s “axis”, and under a central gravitational field it is conserved after release. The cross product gives:

𝐡=𝐫×𝐯=ωr2(−sinλcosλ,0,cos2λ).\mathbf h=\mathbf r\times\mathbf v =\omega r^2(-\sin\lambda\cos\lambda,0,\cos^2\lambda). \tag{2}

Its magnitude is |𝐡|=ωr2cosλsin2λ+cos2λ=ωr2cosλ|\mathbf h|=\omega r^2\cos\lambda\sqrt{\sin^2\lambda+\cos^2\lambda}=\omega r^2\cos\lambda. The inclination to the equator is the angle between 𝐡\mathbf h and the spin axis zz, and its cosine is obtained from the ratio of the zz component of 𝐡\mathbf h to its magnitude. Hence, for prograde motion (in the same sense as the Earth’s spin) not passing through the pole:

cosi0=hz|𝐡|=cosλ,i0=|λ|.\boxed{\cos i_0=\frac{h_z}{|\mathbf h|}=\cos\lambda, \qquad i_0=|\lambda|.} \tag{3}

The factor ωr2\omega r^2 appears both in the numerator and in the denominator and cancels; only the latitude is left. The condition “not passing through the pole” is needed because at the pole the body lies on the spin axis, is carried at no speed, and has no defined orbit.

This is a kinematic identity: it follows from the geometry of motion alone, not from any property of the material or any force law beyond a central pull. The mass, the distance and the spin rate do not affect the initial inclination, which is a function of the geometric latitude alone. The latitude itself is an initial condition of the model; the identity does not fix the location of the source or the occurrence of detachment, but makes the latitude and the inclination a single quantity: given one, the other is fixed.

Secondary velocity components. The identity assumes a purely eastward velocity at release, as corotation gives it. Two further velocity components are possible, and they act differently. An additional radial velocity component (straight outward or inward, along 𝐫\mathbf r) does not change 𝐡\mathbf h, because the cross product of 𝐫\mathbf r with a vector along 𝐫\mathbf r is zero; it therefore does not by itself change the orbital plane: it changes the shape of the orbit, not its inclination. A northward or southward velocity component vNv_N, alongside the eastward component vE>0v_E>0, changes the relation according to the equation:

cosi0=vEcosλvE2+vN2.\cos i_0=\frac{v_E\cos\lambda}{\sqrt{v_E^2+v_N^2}}. \tag{4}

Since vNv_N enters only as its square, a northward and a southward component alike make cosi0\cos i_0 smaller, and thus make the orbit steeper than λ\lambda; for vN=0v_N=0 equation (4) returns to equation (3).

The release state in the model. Detachment from a broad connection therefore fixes the velocity of the centre of mass after the internal forces cease. In the model, the Moon begins to separate when its centre of mass reaches the corotation line, and is finally released about 210 km beyond it; the contact area exerts a weak resistance, and the body is pulled outward with force. This force acts along the radius, and, as shown above, a radial component changes the shape of the orbit, not its inclination. The body is released with the eastward velocity carried by the rotation, and therefore the inclination at release is set directly by the latitude of its centre: 22.5°.

A.3Three angles not to be confused

The angular momentum of the system. So far only the orbit has been considered. But the Earth also carries angular momentum, in its spin, and the Earth’s spin angular momentum together with the Moon’s orbital angular momentum make up the total angular momentum of the system. Once the body is free and in the absence of an external torque, it is this sum — not the Earth’s axis and not the orbit’s axis — that keeps a fixed direction in inertial space. The system is therefore defined by three directions: the Earth’s spin axis, the perpendicular to the plane of the Moon’s orbit, and their vector sum. This section gives the angles between them, and defines what they do not represent.

At the release state (Paper 2: Earth spin angular momentum 2.876×10342.876\times10^{34}, lunar orbital angular momentum 5.90×10335.90\times10^{33} kg m2^2 s−1^{-1}) three angles are obtained in the same vector triangle: 22.5° between the orbit and the Earth’s axis, about 3.78° between the Earth’s axis and the total angular momentum, and about 18.72° between the orbit and the total angular momentum. The orbital vector carries about a fifth of the Earth’s spin angular momentum, so the sum vector lies much closer to the Earth’s axis than to the orbit: the 22.5° between the two is shared as 3.78° on the Earth’s side and 18.72° on the orbit’s side (3.78+18.72=22.53.78+18.72=22.5). Neither of the latter two angles is by itself the inclination relative to the ecliptic, nor an explanation of the 23.44° of the Earth’s axis today. The joint evolution of the Earth’s axis and the Moon’s orbit after release, towards the present 23.44°, is not solved in this series and is a subject for future work.

Neglecting a small lunar spin, and at a stage without a significant external torque, the division is described by:

𝐉=𝐒⊕+μr𝐡,μr=M⊕mM⊕+m.\mathbf J=\mathbf S_\oplus+\mu_r\mathbf h, \qquad \mu_r=\frac{M_\oplus m}{M_\oplus+m}. \tag{5}

That is, the total angular momentum 𝐉\mathbf J is the sum of the Earth’s spin angular momentum 𝐒⊕\mathbf S_\oplus and the orbital angular momentum of the pair. The orbital part is the specific angular momentum 𝐡\mathbf h of equation (2) multiplied by the reduced mass μr\mu_r, the standard mass that describes two bodies going round their common centre of mass; because of the large mass difference between the Earth and the Moon, μr\mu_r is very close to the Moon’s own mass.

Continuity during separation. The calculation establishes consistency between states, rather than a sudden impulse (“kick”) to the spin axis at the moment of detachment: the exchanges of angular momentum can accumulate over the construction stage. In other words, the Earth’s axis is not tilted all at once when the Moon leaves; the angles of the triangle describe the division of angular momentum between spin and orbit as it already exists at that moment. A full treatment requires including the lunar spin, torques from ejected material and external torques.

The dashed lines complete the parallelogram of the vector addition. Because the orbit arrow is the shortest of them, the sum leans only a little away from the Earth’s axis.

The fourth direction. The fourth direction named in the caption is the perpendicular to the ecliptic, set by the Earth’s motion round the Sun, independently of the internal parameters of the Earth–Moon pair. Part A establishes the starting inclination: 22.5° relative to the equator of the time of release, set by the latitude of the body’s centre. Part B defines the size and shape of the orbit the body enters at its release point, and Part C follows the orbital plane outward to the reference frame in which today’s 5.145° is measured.

PART B

Does the body enter an orbit that survives and recedes?

THE PROBLEM AND THE SOLUTION

The corotation velocity is not necessarily identical to the circular orbital velocity appropriate to the release latitude. We compute the resulting orbital ellipse, and connect it to the tidal and survival tests developed in Paper 2.

Part A showed that a body released in corotation with the Earth at latitude λ\lambda enters an orbit inclined at the angle λ\lambda to the Earth’s equator. This part addresses the next question: what are the shape and size of this orbit, does the Earth’s tide then push it outward or draw it inward, and is the body far enough from the Earth to stay whole? The question carries real weight: an inclination has no meaning if the orbit that carries it spirals into the Earth or if the tide pulls the body apart.

Statement of the problem and the kinematic constraints. The first sentence of the box needs a word of explanation: the corotation speed given to a body at latitude λ\lambda is not the same as the circular orbital speed at the same radius. A body in corotation about the spin axis of the parent body does not move at the full distance rr from the centre of mass, but at the smaller local radius rcosλr\cos\lambda from the axis. As a result it moves more slowly than a body at the same distance over the equator, and at the equatorial corotation radius the speed of a body at an intermediate latitude is lower than the speed required for a circular orbit. The orbit it enters is therefore an ellipse, and the release location must be set so that this kinematic deviation is taken into account. This section finds where the orbit is a circle, and which orbit the Moon enters at its actual release point, a little beyond it.

The direction of the tide. The Moon raises two tidal bulges on the Earth, one facing it and one on the far side, and the interaction is dictated by the ratio between the Earth’s spin rate and the Moon’s orbital rate. If the Earth turned exactly at the rate at which the Moon goes round it, the bulges would sit directly under the Moon, pull along the Earth–Moon line, and neither speed up nor slow down the orbit. When the spin rate is higher than the orbital rate, internal friction in the Earth carries the bulge slightly ahead of the Moon; the near bulge then pulls the Moon forward along its orbit, adds energy to the orbit, and the orbit widens — the Moon recedes, as it does today. When the orbital rate is higher than the spin rate, the bulge lags behind it, exerts a braking force, and the orbit shrinks, as happens today to Phobos, the inner moon of Mars. The test below decides which of the three cases applies at each release point.

PART C

How does the orbital plane change on the way out?

THE PROBLEM AND THE SOLUTION

The initial inclination is defined against the plane of the parent body’s ancient equator, while today’s inclination is measured relative to the ecliptic. We identify the torques that turn the orbital plane, keeping the two reference planes fully separate.

Part B ended with an orbit inclined 22.5° to the Earth’s equator of that time. The observed 5.145° is measured relative to a different plane, the ecliptic — the plane of the Earth’s orbit round the Sun. This part explains why the reference plane changes as the Moon recedes, and what is required to follow the change. The point is essential: comparing an angle measured from one plane with an angle measured from another is comparing two different quantities.

C.1 Equator, ecliptic and the Laplace surface

Precession of the orbital plane and the twisting mechanisms. The orbital plane does not keep a fixed orientation in space. External perturbing forces twist it, and force the orbit’s axis to circle slowly around a central reference direction, like the axis of a tilted spinning top circling the vertical; this slow turning is called precession, and the inclination is measured from the plane about which it occurs. In the region close to the parent body, the Earth’s equatorial bulge produces a dominant twisting torque, which tends to pull the orbit towards the plane of the equator, and the orbit precesses about the plane of the equator. In the distant region, the gravitational perturbation of the Sun prevails, and the orbit precesses about the ecliptic, as the Moon’s orbit does today.

The Laplace surface and the scale of the transition. The Laplace surface defines the equilibrium orientations of the orbital plane under both influences together: at each distance it is the plane about which the orbit precesses. The surface coincides with the plane of the equator at short range, bends gradually, and converges to the ecliptic at long range. As the Moon recedes, the plane against which its inclination is effectively measured therefore turns from the equator to the ecliptic. The accepted scale for the transition, in the circular and quadrupole approximation, is [S5]:

rL=(2J2R⊕2a⊙3M⊕M⊙)1/5.r_L=\left(\frac{2J_2R_\oplus^2a_\odot^3M_\oplus}{M_\odot}\right)^{1/5}. \tag{9}

where J2J_2 is the quadrupole moment of the gravity field, which measures the Earth’s equatorial bulge, R⊕R_\oplus is the Earth’s radius, a⊙a_\odot the mean distance to the Sun and M⊙M_\odot the Sun’s mass. The equation gives the distance at which the twisting effect of the Earth’s bulge and that of the Sun are about equal: inside rLr_L the bulge dominates, and outside it the Sun. A larger bulge pushes the transition outward, but only weakly, through the fifth root.

Dependence on the internal structure. The transition equation shows what must be known: the gravitational moment J2R⊕2J_2R_\oplus^2, not only the rotation period. The relation between the two is dictated by the core, the shape and the cooling. A faster-spinning Earth bulges more, but how much more depends on the distribution of mass inside.

An order-of-magnitude check only, with the present Earth radius and J2=J2,now(ω/ωnow)2J_2=J_{2,\mathrm{now}}(\omega/\omega_{\mathrm{now}})^2 — that is, under the assumption that the bulge grows with the square of the spin rate, as rotational flattening does — gives about 17.07 Earth radii for the release day of 5.77 hours. C.2 When can the adiabatic approximation be used?

A condition for adiabatic transfer. As the Moon recedes, the Laplace surface at its position tilts gradually from the equator towards the ecliptic. The question is whether the orbit follows the turning plane smoothly, keeping its inclination relative to it. A change is called “adiabatic” when it is slow enough for the orbit to complete many precession cycles while the guiding plane turns only a little; under these conditions the orbit tends to follow the plane, much as a top keeps circling a support that is tilted slowly.

Limits of the classical approximation. If the equilibrium surface changes slowly relative to the precession cycles, a conserved adiabatic action can be sought. This does not imply exact preservation of the inclination angle in every orbit of finite eccentricity and inclination. The classical Laplace surface is formulated for circular orbits, and the instability threshold of about 68.875° belongs to that defined framework [S5]. (This threshold is the tilt of the planet’s spin axis above which orbits lying on the classical Laplace surface become unstable in the transition zone.)

Constraints of the coupled system. A full solution requires combining the tilt of the Earth’s axis at that time, the line of nodes, the eccentricity, the recession rate and the evolution of the field. The line of nodes is the line along which the orbital plane crosses the reference plane; its orientation relative to the Sun decides in which direction the plane is twisted. The Earth’s tilt today is not a proven input for the past. The joint evolution of the Earth’s axis and the Moon’s orbit after release — the Earth–Moon axis system — is not solved in this series and is a subject for future work. The reduced calculation therefore does not fix a transition distance at 14.8 Earth radii, and does not establish an “exact transfer” of the equatorial angle to the angle relative to the ecliptic.

C.3The fossil figure and the Cassini state

Two further features of the present Moon record its passage outward. The fossil figure is the Moon’s present shape, which is more flattened than its present spin and distance would produce; it is read as a flattening frozen in at an early stage, when the Moon was closer and turned faster. A Cassini state is a configuration in which the Moon’s spin axis, the axis of its orbit and the axis of the plane about which the orbit precesses stay in one plane while they precess together; as the Moon receded it passed from one such state to another.

Complementary constraints. The Moon’s fossil figure and the transition between Cassini states are additional constraints, not evolutionary stages whose places on the path are derived directly from the release latitude. A coupled thermal-orbital model by Downey, Nimmo & Matsuyama found freezing of the figure before about 16 Earth radii under the conditions tested [S6]. Hence 21.6 Earth radii is not a unique freezing radius derived from observation, and the sequence “Laplace, figure freezing, Cassini” is not a general result.

The frequencies of the coupled system. An isolated comparison of two precession rates does not prove a new resonance in the range 17–19 Earth radii. Analysis of such a transition requires the frequencies of the full coupled system, including the solar perturbation and the mutual response of the two angular-momentum vectors. The paper does not claim that a channel for generating axial tilt is open only for the LEF path.

What we now have: the Moon starts with an inclination of 22.5° measured from the Earth’s equator, in the region where the equator governs the plane; the transfer of this inclination to the ecliptic, and the stages it passes on the way, are fixed by a full integration that includes the Earth’s bulge, the Sun and the axes of both bodies. D.2 Why cancelling QQ does not solve the thermal history

The strength of the tide is usually defined by two coefficients. The Love number k2k_2 measures the deformation of a body under a tidal pull: 0 for a perfectly rigid body, and a higher value for a softer body. The tidal quality factor QQ measures the dissipation of tidal energy to friction in each cycle: QQ low represents strong friction, a bulge carried far from the line to the Moon, and fast orbital evolution; QQ high represents weak friction and slow evolution. Q⊕Q_\oplus is the value for the Earth.

Cancellation in the division of the equations. The rate function that multiplies both ȧ\dot a and i̇\dot i cancels completely in the division of the equations. This cancellation produces the explicit independence from Q⊕Q_\oplus within the illustrative law: equation (10) relates the inclination to the distance and not to time, so it gives the same final angle at any speed of recession. But if ϵ\epsilon depends on the melting state at the time a given distance is reached, a change in the recession rate changes the melting state reached at that distance, and with it the damping coefficient. An everyday picture: the route of a car between two cities does not depend on its speed, unless the roads change with time; then the speed decides which roads are open when the car reaches them. In addition, the dissipation in the Moon need not be a constant multiple of the terrestrial tide.

Thermal-orbital constraints. Chen & Nimmo found in the models they tested that survival of the inclination places a strong limit, requiring Q⊕>300Q_\oplus>300 in suitable scenarios [S7]. This is a model constraint, not a general material threshold for every material state. Solidification also does not switch off the tide: a solid body can dissipate energy. Models that include dissipation in the solid and in a magma ocean sharpen the conditions under which an ancient inclination survives [S6]. The thermal history of both bodies is therefore part of the inclination problem, and the cancellation of QQ in the reduced law takes from it the ability to decide it.

D.3 One clock test suffices to expose the limit

The tan-law says nothing about time; the “clock test” completes it. The test requires that a simple tidal law, run outward from the release state, reproduce two boundary conditions: arriving at the present distance in a time that matches the age of the system, and obtaining the present recession rate. Both conditions must hold together.

For illustration only, we use a circular-equatorial law in the outward direction, with constant parameters:

ȧ=Aa−11/2,A=3k2Q⊕mM⊕GM⊕R⊕5.\dot a=Aa^{-11/2},\qquad A=3\frac{k_2}{Q_\oplus}\frac{m}{M_\oplus} \sqrt{GM_\oplus}\,R_\oplus^5. \tag{12}

In words: the recession rate ȧ\dot a falls very steeply with distance, as a−11/2a^{-11/2}, because the tidal bulge weakens rapidly as the Moon recedes. The grouped constant AA gathers the tidal strength of the Earth, k2/Q⊕k_2/Q_\oplus, the mass ratio of the Moon to the Earth and the size of the Earth; a larger k2/Q⊕k_2/Q_\oplus means faster recession.

Because of the high power 13/213/2, the term in afa_f is larger by orders of magnitude than the term in a0a_0: nearly all the time is spent in the late, slow part of the journey, and the elapsed time hardly depends on the point from which the Moon started. It is, however, inversely proportional to AA, and therefore directly proportional to Q⊕Q_\oplus.

Numerical calibration. Inserting k2=0.299k_2=0.299, Q⊕=12Q_\oplus=12, R⊕=6,371kmR_\oplus=6{,}371\ \mathrm{km} and a0≃18,200kma_0\simeq18{,}200\ \mathrm{km} gives a duration of about 1.60 billion years to the present distance, and a recession rate of about 3.70 cm/yr at that distance. The present rate is close to the observation, but the duration does not reproduce 4.5 billion years. Increasing QQ to match the duration reduces the present rate by the same ratio: the time grows in proportion to QQ and the present rate falls in proportion to 1/Q1/Q, so no single constant value of QQ can reproduce both measures.

Correcting the time horizon for the duration of construction. This is a calibration test of a simple law, not a history of the ancient Earth. The simple law is also not valid by itself in the inclined ellipse of Part B or near synchronism. Varying dissipation histories and oceanic resonances are the subject of detailed orbital models [S8]: the Earth’s oceans respond to the tide according to the geometry of their basins and the length of the day, and can pass through resonances in which the response is especially strong, so the present rate need not be the long-term average. If the construction lasted a substantial part of the ancient period, the time available after detachment must be computed from that date, rather than automatically adding 4.5 billion years after detachment. In the series, the construction stage lasts about 600 million years from the start of the bulge to release at 3.873 Ga, so the time available for recession after release is about 3.9 billion years, not 4.5. Against 3.87 billion years the clock fits at Q⊕≃29Q_\oplus\simeq29, and there the present rate falls to about 1.5 cm/yr; the conclusion is the same.

The sign criterion. In the constant time lag (Constant Time Lag) framework, the contribution of the parent body’s tide to ȧ\dot a is positive, and drives orbital recession, when [S4; application and calculation here]:

“Constant time lag” means that the response of the Earth’s tidal bulge to the Moon’s pull is delayed by a small, fixed interval; during this delay the spinning Earth carries the bulge forward, ahead of the Moon or behind it. Here J2J_2 measures the Earth’s equatorial bulge; for a fluid body the bulge grows with the square of the spin rate, hence the scaling. Required calculation: an integration through the Laplace-plane transition, through the Cassini state and with a time-dependent dissipation in both bodies, of the kind that existing secular integrators perform. The Laplace-plane transition is the distance at which the plane about which the orbit precesses changes from the plane of the Earth’s equator, which governs at short range, to the ecliptic, which governs at long range (§C.1). The Cassini state is the stable arrangement in which the Moon’s spin axis, the normal to its orbit and the normal to the ecliptic turn together (§C.3). A secular integrator follows the slow, orbit-averaged evolution of the orbit over billions of years. Result: the prediction of the present inclination.

  2. The dissipation history of the Earth. Constraint: no constant QQ fits both the present rate and the age (§D.3). QQ measures how little of the tidal energy the Earth dissipates in each cycle: a low QQ means strong dissipation and fast recession. Required calculation: ocean-resonance models of the early Earth, initialised from the 5.77-hour day at the moment of release (rather than from the 6.4-hour day of the earlier stage, of the pear alone). An ocean-basin resonance occurs when the natural sloshing period of the basin approaches the tidal period; the dissipation then rises sharply, and it changes as the day lengthens and the continents move [S8]. Result: the rate function the integration requires.

  3. The Earth–Moon axis system. The angle of about 3.8∘3.8^\circ between the Earth’s spin vector and the total angular-momentum vector at the moment of release (§A.3) belongs to the Earth–Moon axis system, future work outside the bounds of the series, and no claim is made here about it.

  4. The magma-ocean switch. Constraint: the survival of the inclination depends on whether the lunar magma ocean had solidified before the Moon reached 20 Earth radii; the constraints on this from the models of [S6] and [S7] are discussed in §D.2. The magma ocean is the molten outer layer of the young Moon; while it is liquid, the Moon dissipates tidal energy strongly, and the inclination is damped faster. Required calculation: an asymmetric thermal model of a Moon with a one-sided lid — thick on the far side and thin on the near side, where the maria were still hot at the time of detachment (Paper 3). A lid of uneven thickness cools at a different rate from the uniform shell that the published models assume. Result: the solidification time, that is, the moment at which the switch acts, for this body and not for a symmetric one.

  5. The fossil figure. Constraint: a body that went through outward rounding, flattening and inward rounding while it sat as a squashed ball on the tip of the pear carries its figure from that stage, not from a later tidal lock. In the LEF model the flattening and rounding of the lunar body took place while it was still attached (Paper 7, The Poles). The fossil figure is the slight departure of the Moon’s shape from a sphere that its rigid outer shell has preserved from an earlier time; the degree-2 shape is the largest-scale part of that departure, its overall elongation and flattening, as measured in the gravity field. Required calculation: derivation of the relation between the geometry of the squashed ball on the tip of the pear (Paper 3) and the present degree-2 shape, a calculation not yet made. Result: a test of whether the shape left by the attached stage matches the present one.
