01 · THE MYSTERY

The mystery: why is the Moon's radioactivity concentrated in one circle — and why are the mare floors poor in it?

One of the most surprising discoveries of the Apollo missions and the Lunar Prospector satellite is the extreme chemical inequality of the Moon's face: the radioactive element thorium — together with potassium, rare-earth elements and phosphorus (a material called KREEP in the literature) — is not spread evenly over the Moon, but is concentrated almost entirely in one patch in the west of the near side.

The accepted literature interprets this patch (the "Procellarum KREEP Terrane") as the last remnant of a global "magma ocean" that crystallised on the Moon, and holds that the radioactive heat of the KREEP is what melted the rocks of the near side and created the basaltic maria. But as soon as the data are examined at high resolution, three sharp contradictions of this explanation appear:

  1. The exact geometric centre: why does the thorium patch sit precisely around the point 24.1°N, 335.2°E — exactly the point fixed in advance in Paper 3 as the centre of the last contact disc between the Moon and the Earth?
  2. The paradox inside the contact zone: if KREEP heat is what broke out of the maria and brought the thorium with it, the mare floors should be the richest in thorium. In fact the exact opposite happens: the mare floors inside the contact zone are the poorest ground in thorium in that region!
  3. The absence of foreign mass in the basins: if the great mare basins (such as Imbrium and Serenitatis) were dug by giant asteroids from outer space, their melt rocks should contain the fingerprint of the iron-loving metals (siderophile elements) of those foreign bodies — but they do not!
02 · AN IMAGE

An image: freezing seawater (the thorium retreats with the brine) and a firing range that keeps its distances

To see how these three chemical mysteries are solved in one stroke, imagine two simple experiments from everyday life:

  • Seawater freezing in a tank connected to a drain pipe (the thorium riddle): when salty seawater begins to freeze from the top, the ice crystals that form are almost fresh water — and all the salt is pushed into the liquid brine left below. If this brine sits in a closed plain with no drainage (like the basalt plains that melted in place), the salt stays trapped in the rock when everything freezes. But if the brine sits above a pipe open downward (like the floor of a mare, connected to the feeding channel into the depths!), the heavy, thorium-rich brine sinks and drains back down the channel into the depths — leaving the mare floor poor in thorium compared with the plains and highlands around it!
  • A firing range of light magma early and far, and heavy magma late and near (the iron rings): at the start of a feeding opening's activity, the magma is rich in volatile gases and poor in iron, so it is very light and fluid — and is fired out to hundreds of kilometres (up to 600 km). As the gases escape, the magma becomes heavier, more viscous and richer in iron (FeO) and titanium (TiO2\text{TiO}_2), and lands very close to the rim of the opening — creating a graded iron halo around every mare!
03 · SCIENCE AND MODEL

Science and model: the iron and titanium rings, 95% of the thorium in the contact disc, and the absence of foreign mass

Part J of Paper 05 examines the satellite chemistry maps of Kaguya (iron and titanium at 240 m resolution) and of Lunar Prospector (thorium at 60 km resolution) and presents four decisive findings:

  1. The iron and titanium halo around the maria reaches 600 km (Study 9, §J.1–J.2): around six maria (Smythii, Imbrium, Serenitatis, Crisium, Moscoviense and Nectaris), after removing all basalt pixels, the iron content of the ground drops gradually from 6.9 wt% in the 0–25 km ring, to 5.1 at 25–50 km, to 3.9 at 50–100 km, and to 3.1 wt% at 100–150 km, and stays high above background levels (2.3 wt%) even at 300–600 km (3.4 wt%) — ten times the range of sideways mixing by later craters! By contrast, around Oceanus Procellarum — a basalt plain with no feeding opening that merely spilled over its edges — the iron drops from 10.9 wt% to 2.4 wt% (the level of the far highlands) at 300–600 km.
  2. 95% of the Moon's thorium excess is trapped in the contact disc (Study 10, §J.3): the contact disc (radius 1,500 km around 24.1°N, 335.2°E) covers only 17.5% of the Moon's surface, yet it holds 95.0% of all the thorium excess above 3 ppm (median 5.39 ppm inside the contact against 1.52 ppm outside it). When a disc of the same size is moved over 16,200 possible centre points across the whole Moon, the contact centre fixed in advance in Paper 3 ranks third out of 16,200 (only 47 km from the theoretical maximum point, which gathers 95.14% against 94.99%)!
  3. Inside the contact zone — the maria are poor in thorium while the plains and highlands are identical (Study 13, §J.4): inside the contact disc, the basalt plains (5.62 ppm) and the highlands (5.48 ppm) carry almost exactly the same thorium concentration! By contrast, the four maria within the contact (Imbrium, Serenitatis, Copernicus H and Sinus Aestuum) are 30% poorer in thorium than the plains (only 3.99 ppm, and each of the four maria is poorer than the ring around it by 0.58–1.39 ppm), while their titanium content stays almost unchanged (−5%, ratio Th/TiO2=0.74\text{Th}/\text{TiO}_2 = 0.74) — because the titanium is locked in ilmenite crystals and stays, while the last thorium-rich liquid drained down the feeding channels!
The foreign-mass test in the basins (Study 11, §J.7)

Had the great mare basins been dug by bodies from outer space at a speed of 18 km/s, they would have required an impacting mass of about 1 × 10¹⁹ kg (and for Imbrium alone 3 × 10¹⁸ kg by scaling, or 2 × 10¹⁹ kg for a body 250 km across, which would have left 6 × 10¹⁷ to 4 × 10¹⁸ kg of foreign material). In fact, the ratios of siderophile elements in the melt rocks of Imbrium do not match any known chondrite group, and most of them already existed in the crust before Imbrium and Serenitatis — because the basins are terrestrial feeding openings, not impacts of foreign bodies!

04 · THE MECHANISM IN WORDS

The mechanism in words: the five clocks of every opening — and decoding the ages of Apollo and Chang'e

Part K of Paper 05 resolves one of the greatest misunderstandings in lunar research: why are the basalt rocks brought back by space missions dated to 3.1–3.9 billion years (Apollo), 2.8 billion years (Chang'e-6 on the far side) and even 2.0 billion years (Chang'e-5 in Oceanus Procellarum)? Does this mean that the openings formed billions of years after the Moon left the Earth?

The answer is no — because every feeding opening carries five separate clocks that must not be mixed (like a well that is dug, opened, carries water, is sealed, and only at the end does the last puddle at its top dry up):

  1. The time of creation of the structure (tstructuret_{\mathrm{structure}}): the moment the opening and the dome beneath it were created during the building stage (during the 600 million years before the detachment at 3.873 Ga).
  2. The time of exposure (texpt_{\mathrm{exp}}): the moment the edge of the contact retreated above the opening and exposed it to space (generation I at once, generation II during the rounding, generation III toward the detachment).
  3. The time of eruption (tventt_{\mathrm{vent}}): the period during which the opening fired material outward, creating the craters and the iron rings around it.
  4. The time of closure (tshutdownt_{\mathrm{shutdown}}): the moment the flux fell below the critical threshold (or the pressure stopped at the moment of detachment) and the opening ceased to be active (tshutdown≥texpt_{\mathrm{shutdown}} \ge t_{\mathrm{exp}}).
  5. The age of the surface (tsurfacet_{\mathrm{surface}}): the age of final cooling of the last drop of magma that froze on the surface. A radiometric clock begins to tick only from the moment the rock cools below its closure temperature and traps the radioactive daughter atoms!

So all the basalt rocks of Apollo, Chang'e-5 and Chang'e-6 measure tsurfacet_{\mathrm{surface}} — the time of final freezing of the hot liquid (kept hot for a long time by the Moon's internal heat or by the concentration of thorium in the contact zone), and not the time the opening opened (tstructuret_{\mathrm{structure}})!
