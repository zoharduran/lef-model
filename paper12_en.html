Abstract

The LEF model (Lunar Elevation and Fission) proposes that the Moon was built from the Earth’s own material while it was still attached to the Earth. A fast-spinning, hot Earth raised a pear-shaped extension of its own material; deep, volatile-rich melt was fed up through it into a body on its tip; the Earth’s outer shell over the region rose with that body as the surface of a dome and became its crust; and the body was released when its centre of mass reached the corotation line, the distance at which a body turning with the Earth already moves at orbital speed. Papers 1–11 of the series set out this process, its orbit, the maria, the craters, the poles, South Pole–Aitken, the stripes of the Pacific floor, the moons of Mars, and the requirements the Moon must meet.

This paper is a companion to the series. It computes nothing new. It collects, from all eleven papers, what the series has left open: requirements not yet tested, studies defined but not yet run, calculations the papers mark “to be computed”, and the rows that the summary of Paper 1 marks as partial or awaiting. To these are added fifteen questions from the working notes of the series, nine of them the author’s own proposals, that the papers have not yet taken up. Each such item is stated as a research topic that another group can take up as a paper of its own. Items that recur in several papers are merged into one topic, with every source kept.

There are 118 topics, grouped by theme in ten parts (A–J): the Earth before and during the pear; the body on the tip; the orbit; the maria and the openings; the craters and their source; the poles; South Pole–Aitken; the stripes and the Pacific; Mars and other bodies; and the requirements and key tests. Each topic is given with its question in one sentence, what the model predicts (with the number from the series), the data that exist or are needed, the method, and where in the series it is treated. Part K gives three lists for choosing: the two key observations of the model, the topics that can be done now on public data, and those that need a large simulation, a mission or samples.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

The model is stated; its calibration is work for many hands. Papers 1–11 present one process, from a hot, fast-spinning Earth to a Moon on its present orbit, and test it against the Moon, the Earth and Mars. Along the way each paper fixed what the process requires and left some numbers to be computed with tools larger than one author’s: three-dimensional simulations, equations of state at 50–150 GPa, coupled thermal–orbital integrations, laboratory work, missions and samples. We propose that these are not gaps in the model but the next stage of it. Each requirement is a topic for a paper by another researcher; the model sets the question and states the number it expects. The requirements of the model, and what would test it, are collected in Paper 11 (The Requirements).

118 topics, each with its own test. Every topic names the observation or calculation that decides it. Most are calibration: they will sharpen a number the series gives as a range (the expansion of the hot parent, the time of failure of the contact, the dissipation history of the Earth). Some are confirmations of a prediction already partly met (the order of sealing of the maria, the young craters of the poles, the Pacific low-velocity province). Two are the key observations of the model: the geometry of the exposure zones — whether the crust and the crater record are ordered in three concentric zones about the near-side centre (topics 107–108) — and the source of the craters — whether the material that made them is returned terrestrial material, as the model requires, or foreign material from outside the Earth–Moon system (topics 49 and 109).

What this paper does not do. It does not recompute any result, change any number of the series, or weaken any claim. The numbers quoted are those of the papers, with the paper and section given, so that a reader can go to the full derivation. Two subjects named by the series as future work — the Earth–Moon axes system and the Moon’s magnetism — are mentioned once at the end and are not numbered among the topics.

Introduction — how to use this paper

The papers of the series tell one process in order. Each of them, in its last parts, collects what it requires, how it is tested, and what it leaves to further work. Read one after another, these lists are scattered across eleven papers and several hundred pages. This paper gathers them into one place and states each item so that a researcher outside the series can begin from it without first reading the whole series.

What a topic is. A research topic, here, is one open question of the model that can be the subject of a paper of its own. It comes from one of five places: a requirement that has been stated but not yet tested (the requirements R1–R18 of Paper 11); a study that a paper defined, with its thresholds fixed in advance, but did not run; a calculation that a paper marks as still to be made; and a row of the summary table of Paper 1 (§D.1), which marks each claim of the series as shown, partial or awaiting — in the colours of a traffic light, green, orange and red; and a question raised in the working notes of the series, with the model’s expected answer, but that no paper has yet taken up (fifteen topics, at the end of Parts B, D, E, G, I and J). Where the same question appears in several papers, it is given once here, with all its sources.

How a topic is presented. Each topic opens with a short paragraph in plain words: what the question is about, and why it matters to the model. Five tagged lines follow. The question states it in one sentence. What the model predicts gives the number or the direction the series expects, with the paper in which it appears. Data says what exists or what is needed. Method names the tool. Where in the series points to the paper and section (“Paper 3 §C.5”), so that the full derivation can be found. The numbers are the series’ numbers; none is recomputed in this paper.

PART A

The Earth before and during the pear

Part A covers the parent body: the Earth before the Moon left it. In the model the Earth at that time was hot, rich in volatiles (substances such as water that turn to gas easily), spinning once in about five and a half hours, and still separating its iron toward the centre. From one region of it, near today’s Kiritimati in the central Pacific, a pear-shaped extension of the Earth’s own material rose toward the corotation line, and the Moon was built on its tip. These topics matter because they set the starting conditions of everything that follows: how large the parent was, where its corotation line lay, whether its deep melt was light enough to rise, whether it had the energy to lift the body and the pear, and whether the pear could stand. Most of them need new modelling of a state the series has already specified.

1The thermal expansion of the hot parent

A hot body is larger than the same body cold, and a body full of dissolved volatiles is larger still. The size of the young Earth matters because, at a given mass, it sets the window of rotation periods in which the transfer can work. Paper 2 adopts an expansion of 120 km and shows that the model works over a range of values; a proper calculation would narrow that range.

The question. How much larger was the hot, volatile-rich early Earth than a cold one?

What the model predicts. An expansion of 120 km is adopted, and 98–520 km is admissible. The expansion sets the window of rotation periods in which the transfer can take place: 4.90–5.54 hours at ΔR=120\Delta R = 120 km (Paper 2).

Data. Estimates of the depth of the early magma ocean (a global layer of molten material); expansion coefficients of silicate melts.

Method. An equation-of-state integration of a hot, volatile-rich Earth. An equation of state relates pressure, temperature and density; integrated from the centre outward, it gives the radius.

Where in the series. Paper 2, Further work item 3; §D.3.

2The stratified Maclaurin problem

A Maclaurin spheroid is the flattened shape taken by a uniform fluid body in steady rotation. The young Earth of the model was not uniform: its iron was sinking, so its inner part was becoming denser than its outer part. The position of the corotation line depends on how the mass is arranged inside, and this line is where the Moon starts its orbit.

The question. Where does the corotation line lie for a parent whose iron is sinking, computed self-consistently?

What the model predicts. 15,382–17,569 km, from D=3D = 3 to D=1D = 1, where DD is the ratio of the density of the inner layer to that of the outer layer (D=1D = 1 is a uniform body). At D=2D = 2 the line lies at about 16,300–16,400 km. This is the starting orbit of Paper 4 (Paper 2).

Data. The parent of the series: volumetric radius 6,517 km, rotation period 5.5 hours, moment-of-inertia factor k≳0.397k \gtrsim 0.397. The factor kk measures how much of the mass lies far from the axis: 0.4 for a uniform sphere, less for a body with a dense core.

Method. The Clairaut–Darwin–Radau theory of the figure of a rotating body, applied to a parent with descending iron.

Where in the series. Paper 2, Further work item 1; §A.4.1, §A.6.2. Paper 1 §F.2.

3 Core formation during the transfer, and the Hf–W reservoir history

Hafnium-182 is a short-lived radioactive isotope that decays to tungsten-182. Hafnium stays in the silicate while tungsten follows iron into the core, so the excess of 182^{182}W in a reservoir records when its metal separated. The model needs the Earth’s core to be still forming during the transfer, and one chronology must carry the tungsten signatures through the whole window of construction.

The question. Was the Earth’s iron still descending during the transfer (centralisation D≈2D \approx 2), and can one chronology carry the tungsten signatures through the 600-Myr window?

What the model predicts. An essentially uniform parent at the start (k≥0.397k \geq 0.397). The core centralises during the transfer, and this shortens the day at release from 6.44 to 5.77 hours (Paper 2). The Hf–W excess was set early in the window and carried without reset (Paper 11).

Data. 182^{182}W anomalies of terrestrial and lunar samples; Hf/W ratios.

Method. A reservoir model of metal–silicate separation over the 600-Myr window, with partial equilibration of metal and silicate.

Where in the series. Paper 2, Part D requirements box, §D.3. Paper 11 §D.2. Paper 1 §F.2 (“Hf–W reservoir history”) and §D.1 (orange row of Paper 2).

4 The equation of state of the deep, volatile-rich melt at 50–150 GPa

The pressure test of Paper 3 asks whether the deep source could push its melt up through the pear and into the body on its tip. The answer depends on one number: the density of a melt rich in water and carbon dioxide at the pressures of the deep mantle, 50–150 GPa (gigapascals; one GPa is about ten thousand times the pressure of the air at sea level). A lighter melt needs less pressure to be lifted, so the test turns on how light this melt is.

The question. Is a hydrous or carbonated silicate melt lighter than about 3,864 kg/m³ along the ascent?

What the model predicts. The pressure test passes below 3,864 kg/m³. At 3,500 kg/m³ a margin of 10.49 GPa is left; at 4,000 kg/m³ it does not pass (Paper 3).

Data. Shock-compression and diamond-anvil data on hydrous and carbonated melts; first-principles molecular dynamics.

Method. Experiments and ab-initio molecular dynamics (simulations from the quantum mechanics of the atoms) at the relevant compositions, giving the density threshold as a function of volatile content.

Where in the series. Paper 3, Further work item 3; §A.9–A.10; §D.1 row 1. Paper 1 §F.2 and §E.1 (“a light deep melt”). Paper 11 §D.3 (row R6).

5 The energy of the lift and the energy release of the parent

Lifting a lunar mass and a pear many times heavier out of the Earth’s gravity costs energy. The model takes that energy from the Earth itself: iron sinking to the core releases gravitational energy, as a weight released on a rope can turn a wheel. The topic asks whether the supply matches the cost, and how fast it was delivered.

The question. Does the evolving Earth release the work of lifting the body and the pear, and at what rate?

What the model predicts. The Moon’s lift costs of order 103010^{30} J and the pear’s about 8×10308\times10^{30} J (5.4 times the Moon’s). The descent of the iron frees of order 103110^{31} J (Paper 3).

Data. Parameters of core formation and of cooling under a steam atmosphere.

Method. A thermal-evolution model of an Earth with descending iron, contracting and cooling; from its output, the duration of the feed.

Where in the series. Paper 3, Further work item 4; §A.12; §C.9 (“Energy”); §D.1 row 3. Paper 1 §F.2 (“Energy balance”).

6 The pressure test repeated with the pear’s mass and the flattened parent

The pressure test of Paper 3 was made in the field of a spherical parent. A parent spinning once in 5.5 hours is flattened, and its gravity is stronger in some directions than a sphere’s. The flattening is measured by J2J_2, the coefficient of the gravity field that describes the equatorial bulge. Paper 3 has already estimated its effect; what is left is to carry the test through with the pear’s own mass included.

The question. Does the source pressure still lift and hold the body and the pear in the field of a flattened parent?

What the model predicts. With J2≃0.036J_2 \simeq 0.036 the inward pull rises by 3% (5.4% at the equatorial surface). The cost of the lift rises by about 4 GPa, inside the margin of 10.49 GPa. The test has still to be rerun with the pear’s own mass (Paper 3).

Data. The pear’s geometry and mass (Paper 3 §C.7).

Method. The joint test of Paper 3 §A.9–A.13, carried out in a non-spherical field.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §C.9 (“Pressure”, “The parent’s flattening”); §A.13.

7 The construction rate: feed flux over the window

A lunar mass had to pass up through the pear in the time the model allows. The flow of a viscous melt through a passage grows very steeply with the width of the passage — as the fourth power of its radius bb, so that doubling the width multiplies the flow sixteen times. A broad passage is therefore the model’s requirement, and the topic is to check that a passage of that width delivers the mass in time without freezing shut.

The question. Does the net feed deliver the internal mass in the time available (about 400 + 100 + 100 Myr, with 100 Myr as a sensitivity case)?

What the model predicts. The flux is sensitive to aperture as b4b^4. A passage of about 1,000 km radius is required (0.46 lunar masses; a lateral load of order 102210^{22} N) (Paper 3).

Data. Viscosities of the melt; radii of the passage.

Method. A model of pipe flow and freezing over the permitted ranges of viscosity and aperture.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §D.1 row 2; §A.11; §A.13. Paper 1 §E.1 (“A broad, massive, viscous connection”).

8 The pear’s self-holding equilibrium figure and its budget of angular momentum (R9)

The pear is the Earth’s own material standing out from the surface toward the corotation line. It must hold itself up: its base must be wide enough to carry it, and its shape must be one a rotating, internally pressurised Earth can keep. Paper 11 lists this as requirement R9. The present estimate uses round cross-sections of uniform density; a true equilibrium figure would replace it. The same figure also fixes how much spin the pear passes to the Moon.

The question. Does a rotating, internally pressurised Earth admit an equilibrium figure with a body at its tip — a base of about 9,000 km narrowing in one arc to a contact of 3,000 km, wholly inside the corotation line — and does that figure close the angular-momentum budget?

What the model predicts. The narrowest self-holding base grows from 7,800 to 8,800 km (at 3,000 kg/m³) between the start and release. The pear’s mass is 8.8–12.1 lunar masses for bases of 9,000–11,000 km. The pear passes about a sixth of the system’s angular momentum (the total amount of spin) to the Moon; this is taken from the Earth’s spin and offset by the centralising core (Paper 3).

Data. The parent state of the series; no observations are needed.

Method. An equilibrium figure with a free surface, including the pear’s own gravity along its axis and the internal flow. This replaces the present minimum-width estimate from round cross-sections of uniform density.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §C.7, §C.8, §C.9 (“The shape itself”, “Angular momentum and heat”). Paper 11 §C.6 (R9). Paper 1 §E.1 (R9, awaiting). Paper 2 §A.4.

9 The northward lean of the pear: oblique feed from a source north of the equator

The base of the pear lies near the equator, at about 1.9N, yet the Moon was released from about 22.5N. The tip therefore leaned north. Rotation pushes a raised mass toward the equator, not away from it, so the lean cannot come from the spin. The model places its cause in the feed: the deep source lay north of the equator and fed the pear obliquely.

The question. Can a deep source north of the equator feed obliquely enough to take the tip from a base near 1.9N to about 22.5N against the equatorward push of the rotation?

What the model predicts. The lean comes from the feed path, not from the rotation (Paper 3).

Data. The position of the base (Kiritimati) and of the Pacific low-velocity province, read — with reservation — as the returned pile.

Method. A geometry of the deep source and of the feed path in a rotating, flattened parent.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §D.1 (“The pear leans north”); §A.4; §B.1.

10The Earth’s core during the pear

A pear of about nine lunar masses standing on one side of the Earth moves the Earth’s centre of mass toward it, and the dense core, floating in the mantle, follows. After the pear fell back, the core returned. The model asks whether this episode left a trace in the inner core, the solid centre of the Earth, whose eastern and western halves are known to differ in how fast seismic waves cross them.

The question. Was the Earth’s core drawn 270–370 km toward the pear while the pear stood, and did it return after the pear fell?

What the model predicts. A displacement of 270 km at the start (9,000-km base) growing to 370 km at release (11,000-km base), and a return after compensation. A hemispherical structure of the inner core aligned with the pear’s side is the record to look for (Paper 3).

Data. The east–west asymmetry of the inner core in seismic travel times; the age of the inner core. The test weakens if the inner core is much younger than the Moon.

Method. A three-dimensional hydrostatic model of the Earth with the pear standing and after its fall, following the deficit left in the source.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §E.5; Further work item 7.

11The classical fission route, recomputed

The classical fission idea, from George Darwin onward, held that a spinning fluid Earth turns into an elongated ellipsoid (a Jacobi ellipsoid) and then into a pear that divides. It was rejected because the Earth–Moon system has far too little angular momentum for it. Paper 2 shows that the connected transfer of the LEF model needs almost exactly today’s angular momentum. A modern computation of the classical route would make the comparison exact.

The question. What does a modern computation of the Jacobi sequence and its pear-shaped branch give for this mass and density?

What the model predicts. The classical route needs 2.74 times today’s angular momentum at the Jacobi bifurcation (the point at which the elongated shapes branch off), against 1.007 for the connected transfer (Paper 2).

Data. Tabulated Jacobi and Poincaré sequences.

Method. A numerical sequence of rotating fluid equilibria, with the pear-shaped point verified.

Where in the series. Paper 2, Further work item 6; §A.6.5.

PART B

The body on the tip — shape, rounding, release

Part B covers the Moon while it was still attached: a foamed body on the tip of the pear, fed through its base and wrapped in the Earth’s outer shell. In the model it passed through outward rounding, flattening and inward rounding; its contact with the pear narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km; and it let go at the corotation line. These topics matter because the attached stage is where the Moon’s shape, its two different sides and its crust were made. They ask whether the body was stable, whether its shell could be stiff while its base stayed mobile, how it became round, how the contact failed, and what the near and far crusts should show. Two more ask what the foam and the contact left in the Moon as it is now: high-pressure phases in the samples from the contact zone, and porosity falling with depth. Most are calculations and simulations; three (topics 19, 21 and 23) can be checked now on public lunar data, and one (topic 22) on samples already returned.

12 A three-dimensional simulation of the connected body

The series has specified the attached body completely: its mass, its foamed density, the width of the tip, the pressure of the feed. What it has not done is run that body forward in three dimensions and watch it evolve. Such a simulation is the most direct check of Paper 3: it would show whether the body stands, and whether it goes through the stages in the order the model gives.

The question. Is a body of the lunar mass, foamed to 1,300 kg/m³ on a 6,000-km tip and fed through its base at 150 GPa, stable? Does it go through outward rounding, flattening and inward rounding, and release with the computed margin?

What the model predicts. A stable dome of about 3,650 km. The contact shrinks from 6,000 to 3,000 km. The order of the stages is well advanced before detachment (Paper 3).

Data. None needed; the initial state is fully specified in the series.

Method. A smoothed-particle (SPH) or finite-element simulation with a free boundary, together with one shared timing model for feed, cooling and failure.

Where in the series. Paper 3, Further work item 2; §D.1 rows “Stable attached body” and “Order of stages”; §A.5. Paper 1 §F.2, §E.3.

13 A stiff shell over a mobile base: the coupled thermal–rheological solution

The model asks two things of the Earth’s outer shell at once. It must be stiff enough to keep the craters made on it during the ascent, since those craters are the far side’s record. And the layer beneath it must stay soft enough for the shell to ride up on the growing dome. Viscosity, measured in Pa·s, is resistance to flow; the question is whether one cooling history gives a high viscosity above and a low one below, for long enough.

The question. Is there one rheological range in which the outer shell stays stiff enough to keep its craters while the interface beneath it stays mobile enough to ride up on the dome?

What the model predicts. A shell about 18 km thick, with viscosity ≳1023\gtrsim 10^{23} Pa·s through the ascent (a requirement, not a measurement), over a weak interface between sial and sima — the light outer layer of the early Earth and the denser layer beneath it (Papers 3, 11). The making of the record, its preservation and the rise must overlap in time (Paper 6).

Data. Viscosity laws for anorthositic and sialic crust; cooling under a steam atmosphere.

Method. A coupled thermal and rheological balance along the construction.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §D.1 row “Strengthening exterior and mobile base”; §A.7; §D.4 item 4. Paper 11 §D.1, §D.3 (“Rise and preservation”). Paper 6 §O.1 (“A record carried on the shell”). Paper 1 §E.1 (“A stiff shell”).

14 The step from a/c≈1.20a/c \approx 1.20 to round before release

Late in the attached stage, with a 5.77-hour day and a mean density of 2,600 kg/m³, a fluid body would be a flattened Maclaurin spheroid with its equatorial radius aa about 1.20 times its polar radius cc. At release the Moon must be rigid and round. The series states this step as a requirement; the calculation that carries the body from one state to the other is a topic in itself.

The question. How does the rigid shell carry the body from the Maclaurin ratio of about 1.20 late in the attached stage (5.77 h, 2,600 kg/m³) to a round body at release?

What the model predicts. At release the body is rigid and round: shear modulus μ≥10\mu \geq 10 GPa, axis ratio ≤1.04\leq 1.04. This is a requirement, not computed in the series (Papers 2, 7).

Data. None; a calculation.

Method. An elastic–viscous figure calculation of a shelled body under decreasing spin and increasing density.

Where in the series. Paper 7 §C.1 and front matter (“What it requires”). Paper 2 §B.7. Paper 1 §C.7.

15 The drift of the release latitude below 22.5

The latitude at which the Moon was released becomes, in the model, its first orbital inclination i0i_0. While the contact contracted from 6,000 to 3,000 km, the body sat on a viscous layer, and the rotation pushed it gently toward the equator. If it slid at all, it slid south, so the release latitude can only be lower than 22.5. The size of that slide has not been computed.

The question. How far did the body slide toward the equator on the viscous contact layer during the contraction from 6,000 to 3,000 km?

What the model predicts. Anchoring holds the body on the wide tip; in the outward rounding it has freedom but little time. The drift can only lower the latitude, so 22.5 is an upper bound on i0i_0 (with 25 as a sensitivity case). Its size is not computed in the series (Papers 3, 4).

16 Stefan adhesion and the time of failure of the contact

Two flat plates with a thin film of liquid between them are hard to pull apart: the liquid must flow into the widening gap, and that flow resists. This is Stefan adhesion. The Moon’s contact with the pear was such a film, a partly molten layer. How it finally let go — by cavities opening in it, by necking, or by a change from flowing to breaking — sets how far beyond the corotation line the Moon was released.

The question. How does a partially molten layer between two bodies let go — by cavitation, necking, the passage from viscous to brittle failure — and when?

What the model predicts. A slow phase, then a runaway once the gap reaches d=3d0d = 3d_0. The release margin is Δr≃2d0≈210\Delta r \simeq 2d_0 \approx 210 km beyond the corotation line (d0≈105d_0\approx105 km), where the release-distance search of Paper 3, §C.5a finds the present inclination and the present eccentricity meeting, when a little more than half the Moon has crossed the line.

Data. Laboratory separation of plates joined by partial melt.

Method. Laboratory and numerical work on non-Newtonian Stefan adhesion (a fluid whose viscosity changes with the rate of flow), giving the range of the margin and the time of release.

22 High-pressure phases from slow pressure in samples from the contact zone

While the body was attached, its lower face pressed against the top of the pear, and as the contact narrowed from 6,000 to 3,000 km the same load was carried by a smaller and smaller area: the pressure on the outer metres of that face rose as the contact shrank. 23 Whole-Moon porosity by depth: the record of the foam

In the model the body began as a foam, about 1,300 kg/m³ at the start of rounding, compacted to 2,600 kg/m³ at release and to 3,344 kg/m³ today. A foam that is compacted by its own weight closes its pores only where the pressure is high enough, and the weight of the overlying column grows with depth. The model therefore expects a Moon compacted at depth with a porous upper crust: the pores that survive are the remains of the foam near the surface. GRAIL measures porosity directly, through the bulk density of the crust: about 2,550 kg/m³, lighter than the grains it is made of, which corresponds to a mean crustal porosity of about 12%. Paper 8 read the porosity at the surface and at 10 km for South Pole–Aitken and its surroundings (topic 77); the same reading for the Moon as a whole has not been made.

The question. Does the porosity of the whole Moon, not only of South Pole–Aitken, fall with depth as a foam compacted by pressure should leave it, and at what depth do the pores close?

What the model predicts. The foam collapsed where the pressure sufficed; residual porosity survives in the upper few kilometres, and the deep body is compacted. Porosity that does not fall with depth, or that rises with it, does not support the reading. A fall with depth is also expected of pores opened by fracturing at the surface; the test therefore lies in the depth of closure and its spread over the Moon, set against a foam compacted from within.

Data. GRAIL bulk density, grain density and porosity grids (Wieczorek et al. 2013), with the linear and exponential depth models of Besserer et al. (2014). Method. Area-weighted means of porosity at the surface and at depth, in the exposure zones and over the whole Moon, with the maria and the large basins masked in every zone alike; the depth of closure compared with a compaction model of the foam under its own weight.

Where in the series. Paper 8 §B6 (porosity of South Pole–Aitken and the far side); Paper 7 §C.3 (compaction from 1,300 to 3,344 kg/m³); Paper 2 Appendix G, row E42 (volatiles and porosity). The maria and the openings

In the model a mare is not a basin flooded after an impact; it is an opening over a feed dome: a place where hot, volatile-rich melt rose from below through the shell of the body sitting on the Earth’s pear, and later poured out as basalt (Paper 3, Paper 5). 38 The pits and caves of the Moon: vapour voids, not lava tubes

Images from orbit show steep-walled pits in the lunar surface, and in 2024 radar data showed a cave passage opening beneath the pit of Mare Tranquillitatis. In the literature the pits of the maria are skylights into lava tubes: channels left when the surface of a lava flow froze and the melt beneath it drained away. The author’s proposal is different: the pits and caves are voids left by trapped vapour that broke out. The material of the model was rich in water and other volatiles, and when the far-side openings sealed the pressure beneath them was trapped (Paper 5, §I.5); vapour held in the shell and forcing its way out leaves voids behind it. The two readings differ in shape and in place. A lava tube is a long, shallow tube that runs with the flow, and it exists only where lava flowed. A vapour void is a deep, dispersed network, and it can lie wherever vapour was held. The pit of Mare Tranquillitatis lies within the mouth of its opening. The test has not been made.

The question. Are the pits and caves of the Moon voids left by trapped vapour that broke out, rather than lava tubes?

What the model predicts. Vapour voids: deep, dispersed networks rather than long, shallow tubes, and not confined to ground where lava flowed. The decisive case is a cave on the far side, far from any basalt: it cannot be a lava tube, since no lava flowed there.

Data. The LROC catalogue of lunar pits (Wagner and Robinson 2014, and its later updates), with the setting of each pit; the LROC mare map; LROC Narrow Angle Camera images of the pit walls; Mini-RF radar, with which the passage beneath the Tranquillitatis pit was found (Carrer and co-workers 2024).

Method. Each pit is placed by its setting and by its distance from the nearest mapped basalt, and the far-side pits far from basalt are listed apart. In the literature the pits outside the maria are read as collapses in the cooled melt of craters, so the setting alone does not decide; for the pits with images of their walls or radar beneath them, the shape of the void is read as well: long and shallow along a flow, or deep and branching.

39 The crust jump under an opening, by the age of its basin

Under the centre of an opening the crust is thinner than in a ring around it: the feed dome was pushed up from below, raised the dense mantle beneath the opening and thinned the light crust above it. Paper 5 calls the difference the crust jump and measured it opening by opening: 29 of the 36 marked openings show a jump of 8 km and more, with a median of 17 km, against 4 of 169 ordinary craters, with a median of 1 km. The author’s proposal adds time to this measurement. A basin that formed while the Moon was still hot did not keep its jump: the warm material beneath it flowed slowly back, and the jump relaxed. A young basin kept more of it. The literature reads the subdued gravity of some old basins in the same way, as viscous relaxation in a warm early Moon (for example Kamata and co-workers, 2013). Two cautions shape the test. The basins must be ordered by age without the calibration of crater counts to years (Neukum’s), which assumes a constant rate of bodies from outside, the assumption the model does not accept. And a large basin has a larger jump for its size alone, so size must be held fixed.

The question. Is the crust jump under an opening, relative to its size, larger in young basins and smaller in old ones?

What the model predicts. Yes. In an old basin, formed while the Moon was hot, the jump relaxed; a young basin kept more of it.

Data. The crust jumps of Paper 5, §D.2 (the four GRAIL crustal-thickness models, averaged; the centre to 0.3 of the radius against the ring at 1.3–2.0 radii); the relative order of the basins by superposition, what lies on what (Wilhelms 1987), and by the density of the craters superposed on them (Fassett and co-workers 2012).

Method. The jump divided by the radius, or compared between basins of similar size, against the order by superposition and by crater density, for the openings that show the jump; a rank correlation for each ordering. Neither ordering is converted to years.

Where in the series. Paper 5, §D.2 (Study 3), which measures the jump itself but not its dependence on age. The dependence on age is not yet in the series; raised in the author’s working notes (16.10.2026 collection).

41 The rim of a mare as a layered record: rim height and the duration of ejection

In the literature the high ground around a basin is its rim, raised in one event by the blow that dug the basin and by the collapse that followed. In the model the margins of a mare were built from outside, layer on layer, by the material the mare itself threw out (Paper 5, §F.3). Material that fell back into the mare vanished in it; material that fell on the margins stayed there and left a mark of its time. Two consequences follow. The longer an opening threw material, the higher its margins should stand. And margins built in this way are layered, with ages spread over the time of ejection, whereas the rim of a single collapse has one age. Paper 5 measured that every large floor lies below its rim (16 of 16, by a median of 3.4 km) and found a raised collar of returning material around the far maria (topic 37). Whether rim height follows the duration of ejection, and whether the rims are layered, has not been tested.

The question. Are the rims of the maria built of layers whose ages spread over time, and does the height of a rim grow with how long its mare threw material out?

What the model predicts. The higher the rim, the longer the ejection lasted. The rim is layered, with a spread of ages; a rim made by a single collapse after an impact would have one age.

Data. 42 Blunt or sharp: the crest where a large crater’s rim is missing on the side of the plain

On the plains of the contact the large craters have lost their rims on the side that faces the plain. The literature reads a low rim on a mare plain as flooding: basalt rose around the crater and hid its lower slopes, as water rising around a sandcastle hides its base but leaves its top untouched. The model reads it as heat: the plain of the contact, heated from the Earth beneath it, wore away the rim where it touched it and lowered the crest itself. The two readings part at the crest. A flood rises along the outer flank and does not reach the top, so a flooded crest stays sharp; a crest worn by heat is blunt. Paper 5 and Paper 6 measured this (Study 15). On the plains of the contact the rim is missing on 24% more of the directions that face the plain than of those that face the highland, and the crest that survives on the side of the plain is blunter: its sharpness lower by 35 m/km2^2, its width 50 m below the top larger by 0.14 km, and its angle wider by 5.3∘5.3^\circ. On the plains beyond the contact the two sides differ by little. What remains open is the direct comparison: matched crater against crater, at the same diameter, latitude and interior depth, only four pairs of the contact and beyond could be formed, too few to compare the two groups.

The craters and their source

The model’s craters were made by returning material: material thrown from the openings of the Earth and of the body itself, which landed on the shell at about the speed it left (Paper 6). 56 Crater-count ages against direct ages of the same surface

In the literature the age of a surface is read from the number of craters on it, and the count is turned into years by a calibration curve (the chronology of Neukum and co-workers) that assumes a known and steady rain of bodies from outside, anchored on the samples returned by Apollo and Luna. Paper 6 replaces that rain: in the model almost all the craters were made by returning material, whose rate rose and fell with the activity of the openings and of the Earth. A count of craters then measures how much a surface received, not how long it stood open (Paper 6); where the surface received more than an outside rain would have given it in the same time, a count converted with the calibration reads it as older than it is. Where one surface has been dated both ways — by its craters from orbit and directly, by radioactive decay in a returned sample — the direct age should therefore come out younger, and systematically so. Chang’e-6 has given one such pair so far: crater counts of 2.98–1.54 Gyr for the western mare at the landing site, and a laboratory age of 2.807±0.0032.807\pm0.003 Gyr for about 99% of the basalt fragments (Paper 8, B9, B15). One pair, lying inside the counted range, cannot show a trend; the comparison over many surfaces has not been made.

The question. Where the same surface has been dated both by crater counting and directly, is the direct age systematically younger than the crater-count age?

What the model predicts. Yes. The craters came mostly from returning material at a rate that was not steady, so a count converted to years by a constant flux overstates the time of exposure (Paper 6, Part M). A direct age that is younger than the count supports the model. The model also reads a direct age as the time the material cooled and closed, the age of the surface, not of the opening beneath it (Paper 5, §K.1).

Data. Published crater-count model ages and radiometric ages of the same units: the Apollo and Luna sites, Chang’e-5 (about 2.0 Gyr) and Chang’e-6 (Paper 5, §K.1; Paper 8, B9).

Method. Pair each dated sample with the crater-count age of the unit it came from, counted on that unit alone. Since the calibration curve is anchored on the Apollo and Luna ages, those sites agree with it by construction; the test rests on surfaces outside the calibration set, using counts published before the sample was dated. The relative clocks of the series — superposition and crater density compared between surfaces — are kept as they are; only the conversion to years is tested.

57 The rays of young craters: material within the surface

Rays are the bright streaks that run outward from some young craters, sometimes for hundreds of kilometres. In the accepted reading they are ejecta: material thrown out by the crater and laid on top of the ground around it. The author’s proposal reads them differently: a ray is a record within the ground beneath the surface, not ejecta lying on it — material that met a cold, glassy surface and froze into it. A ray forms only where the material lands on a floor that has already cooled; on a floor that is still hot, no ray forms. This holds for the floors of the maria, and equally for the basalt plains of the near side — Oceanus Procellarum and the plains around it, where Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus lie — which, exposed to the great heat of the nearby Earth, are very similar to the mare floors. Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus landed after their floor had cooled, and they carry rays. Diophantus and Delisle landed while the floor was not yet fully frozen, and they carry none. On these floors a ray is therefore a mark of time: a rayed crater landed on a floor already cold, a crater without rays on a floor still warm.

The proposal differs from the accepted reading in three observable things. The first is depth: a layer laid on top is thin, and small fresh craters inside a ray would cut through it to the darker ground beneath, while a ray frozen within the ground continues downward. Tycho, a large rayed crater in the southern highlands, is the test case: its rays cross highland ground and also reach the maria. The second is the state of the floor: on the mare floors and the basalt plains, rayed and rayless craters of the same size should be told apart by whether the floor had cooled when the material landed. Rayed craters as such are not confined to the near side: Paper 7 (§D.3) found the 61 rayed craters of Grier et al. about as dense on the far side as on the near (2.01 against 1.70), so the test must look at the rays themselves — where the streaks are seen, on which floor and how far they reach — and not only at the craters that carry them. The third is number: among the small and medium craters of the near side, those without rays should outnumber those with rays. The reason is the order of events: the floors cooled while the Moon receded, and as it receded less material reached it, so fewer craters landed on cooled floors than on warm ones. Neither reading has yet been tested on these terms.

The question. Are the rays of young craters a record frozen within the ground rather than ejecta lying on it, and do they form only where material landed on a floor that had already cooled?

What the model predicts. The rays lie within the ground, not on top of it. On the mare floors and the basalt plains, craters that landed after the floor cooled carry rays (Copernicus, Kepler, Aristarchus), and craters that landed while the floor was not yet fully frozen carry none (Diophantus, Delisle). Among the small and medium craters of the near side, those without rays outnumber those with rays, because less material landed as the Moon receded. Tycho, a rayed crater in the highlands, is the case on which the depth of the rays is tested.

Data. PART F

The poles

Paper 7 reads the crowding of old craters toward the lunar poles (a pole-to-equator density ratio of 1.20 ±\pm 0.05 in the clean count) as a mark of the body’s change of figure: the craters formed while the body was flattened by its spin, and when it rounded inward the polar surface contracted while the craters kept their size, so that they came closer together. 59 The figure with strength and the two-layer relaxation

A Maclaurin figure is the flattened shape that a uniform, fully fluid body takes when it spins. It gives the largest flattening the body could have had, and therefore an upper bound on the polar crowding. A body with a stiff shell flattens less, and so narrows the prediction from below. The interior, with a viscosity (resistance to flow) of 102110^{21}–102210^{22} Pa·s, and the shell, at about 102310^{23} Pa·s, also relaxed at different rates, and the way they shared the contraction sets the strain left in the shell.

The question. How much of the fluid flattening did a body with a stiff shell take, and how did the interior (102110^{21}–102210^{22} Pa·s) and the shell (102310^{23} Pa·s) share the contraction?

What the model predicts. The fluid figure gives the upper bound; a stiff shell narrows the prediction from below. The strain in the shell is shortening at the poles and fracturing at the equator (Paper 7).

Data. The measured clean ratio, 1.20 ±\pm 0.05.

Method. A Maclaurin figure with strength; a two-layer visco-elastic relaxation (a model in which each layer responds first elastically and then by slow flow) along the attached stage.

PART G

South Pole–Aitken

South Pole–Aitken (SPA) is the largest and oldest basin on the Moon, a broad depression on the far side. In the series it is not the scar of a blow from outside. Paper 8 (South Pole–Aitken) reads it as the far-side end of a heat path: while the Moon was attached to the tip of the pear, heat from the Earth’s interior entered the body through two exits on the near side — Imbrium, the main exit (the burner), and Serenitatis, the secondary — crossed it, and came up beneath the thin far-side crust. 69 A redox budget for the reduced mantle beneath SPA

The oxygen fugacity of a melt measures how much free oxygen was available when it formed. It is given relative to a reference reaction, the iron–wüstite buffer (IW): Δ\DeltaIW is the number of orders of magnitude above or below it, so a more negative value means a more reduced melt, poorer in oxygen. Basalt fragments returned by Chang’e-6 from inside SPA give Δ\DeltaIW −1.93-1.93, against −0.80-0.80 for the near side — a mantle beneath SPA markedly poorer in oxygen. The model reads this as the work of the same heat source: prolonged heat drives oxygen off, and metallic iron from the Earth’s interior takes up what remains.

The question. How much heat and how much metallic iron are needed to lower the oxygen fugacity from Δ\DeltaIW −0.80-0.80 to −1.93-1.93, and does this agree with the heat budget of topic 68?

What the model predicts. Oxygen driven off by heat and consumed by iron from the Earth’s interior (Paper 8, B12). The heat and the iron that the budget requires should fall within what the heat path delivered.

Data. The Chang’e-6 basalt fragments (23 fragments, measured by two methods).

79 Vapour that evaporated metals in SPA, and where the vapour went

Paper 8 (B11) found that the heat beneath SPA drove off volatile elements: the ratio of potassium to thorium is low and flat across the whole heated region (451–455 within 30° of the centre, 16%–20% below the far-side highlands at 563 and the rest of the far side at 537), and in the Chang’e-6 basalts both potassium and iron are isotopically heavy — δ41\delta^{41}K of 0 to +0.09+0.09 and δ56\delta^{56}Fe of 0.13–0.21, against −0.29-0.29 to −0.04-0.04 and 0–0.11 in the near-side basalts of Apollo and Chang’e-5. Iron evaporates only under strong heat. The author’s proposal adds a carrier: a water-rich magma, cooked by the burner for a long time, released hot vapour in large amounts, and hot water vapour raises the evaporation of metals, which leave as compounds with water or chlorine at a lower temperature than in a vacuum. This would explain how iron too lost its light atoms. If it is right, the other metals that become more volatile in vapour — sodium, zinc and copper — should be depleted together with potassium and iron and be isotopically heavy as well, chlorine should be heavy, and the mantle source should be very dry. Paper 8 records an unusual drying (B11, B12), its source still to be cited, and leaves open where the vapour went: to space through the opening, recondensed at the margins of the basin, or both, and how much was lost. The other metals and the vapour budget have not yet been measured.

The question. Is the mantle source beneath SPA very dry, and are zinc, copper, sodium and chlorine depleted and isotopically heavy together with potassium and iron? Where did the vapour go: to space, or did it condense at the margins?

What the model predicts. Prolonged heating of a water-rich source made hot vapour that carried off metals. The source is very dry; potassium, sodium, zinc, copper and iron are depleted together and isotopically heavy; chlorine is isotopically heavy. Potassium and iron are measured (Paper 8, B11); the other elements are the prediction of this topic. Vapour that recondensed at the margins would leave there the light isotopes that the centre lost.

Data. Chang’e-6 samples: the water content of the mantle source, and the isotopes of K, Fe, Zn, Cu and Cl, against the Apollo and Chang’e-5 basalts; the sodium and potassium recondensed in the Chang’e-6 glasses (Li et al. 2026); the potassium-to-thorium ratio at the rim (567 at the margin; Paper 8, B11).

Method. Measure the volatile metals and chlorine in the Chang’e-6 basalts as one set, so that a common depletion with heavy isotopes can be told apart from mixing of materials (the joint mixing model of K, Th, FeO and mineralogy named in Paper 8, B11). For the fate of the vapour, compare the isotopes of the condensed phases with those of the residue, and look at the margins for the excess of volatiles and of light isotopes that recondensed vapour would leave; the balance between centre and margins gives how much left the body.

Where in the series. Paper 8, B11 (“Open”: where the vapours went and how much was lost), B12 (the dry mantle), and B6 (the voids opened by escaping gas). PART H

The stripes and the Pacific

The floor of the North Pacific is crossed by long, straight, parallel stripes that run east–west for thousands of kilometres, from Mendocino in the north to Clipperton in the south. Paper 9 (The Stripes) reads them as the leading side of the pear on which the Moon was built. The pear stood on the Pacific with its base near Kiritimati (1.9°N, 157.5°W) and leaned back against the Earth’s rotation, its tip lagging to the west. Its front, the leading side, facing the rotation, was stretched while the pear stood and folded into stripes when the pear fell back into the Earth; its back, the trailing side, crumpled into the arcs of the western Pacific. 85 Stripes Study 6 — continuity beneath the ridges

The Pacific floor is crossed not only by the stripes but by chains of seamounts and ridges that run across them. Which came first? If the stripes are an older fold of the leading side, they should pass beneath the ridges unbroken, on the same circle on both sides. Paper 9 observed four such crossings. It also proposed a test of order: if a fold formed under a standing ridge, the ground under the ridge was drawn together, and the ridge should be shortened, crowded or bent where the fold passed beneath it; if the ridge is untouched, it came later. The convergence of the stripes west of the ridges is a first reading on oblique views (orange in Paper 9 §H); oblique views exaggerate height and distort distance, so the measurement must be made on a flat map.

The question. Does each stripe continue on the same circle on both sides of a ridge, and is the ridge shortened where the fold passed beneath it?

What the model predicts. The stripes pass beneath the ridges (four crossings observed) (Paper 9, B.5–B.6, F.2).

Data. Bathymetry.

Method. A flat map without vertical exaggeration, with the coordinates of each stripe read from the cursor on both sides of each ridge; the length of each ridge measured where a stripe crosses it.

Where in the series. Paper 9 §G, Study 6; §B.5–B.6, F.2; Paper 1 §D.1.

86 Stripes Study 7 — the spatula ridges against ridges built from below

Lift a spatula from wet plaster and the plaster is drawn up into ridges behind it. Paper 9 reads three Pacific chains — the Line Islands, the Marshall–Gilbert–Tuvalu chain and the Hawaiian–Emperor chain — in this way: drawn up from above by the lifted tip, spaced as the tip was wide, 2,800–3,500 km apart. Elsewhere in the world there are ridges that the literature reads as built from below, by a source of heat rising beneath the floor: Ninetyeast, Walvis and Chagos–Laccadive. A chain built from below over a fixed source shows a plume age progression: the ages of its volcanoes increase steadily along its length. The Line and Marshall chains show no such progression. Hawaii–Emperor does show one, from 0 Ma at Hawaii to about 81 Ma at the far end of the Emperor chain; the model requires that, on a spatula ridge, such a progression be a later sequence of eruptions on a ridge that already stood.

The question. Do the Line, Marshall–Gilbert–Tuvalu and Hawaiian–Emperor chains differ from Ninetyeast, Walvis and Chagos–Laccadive in age, shape, flexural moat, crustal root and orientation? How does the Hawaiian age progression fit?

What the model predicts. The chains were drawn up from above by the lifted tip, spaced as the tip was wide (2,800–3,500 km). Line and Marshall show no plume age progression (Paper 9, D.1–D.4, F.3; the comparison is orange in §H). A flexural moat is the trough that forms around a load as it bends the floor beneath it, and a crustal root is the thickened crust beneath it; a ridge built from below and a ridge drawn up from above should differ in both.

Data. Radiometric ages; gravity and flexure data; seismic measurements of crustal roots.

Method. A side-by-side comparison of the six chains on the five measures; the Hawaiian ages examined as eruptions on a standing ridge.

Where in the series. Paper 9 §G, Study 7; §D.1–D.4, F.3; Paper 1 §D.1.

87Stripes Study 8 — the mirror imprint

When the Moon sat on the tip of the pear, its near side faced the Earth. The two faces stood facing each other, so the near side should have left an imprint on the floor beneath it as a mirror image — as with a seal pressed into wax, east and west are exchanged while north stays north. The imprint should also be larger than the near side is today, by 1.37: the Moon then had a density of 1,300 kg m−3^{-3} against 3,344 today, and the same mass packed into a smaller volume makes every distance on its surface shorter by that factor. With Imbrium in place (13.19°N, 179.04°E), the mirror predicts Serenitatis near 11.5°N, 166.3°E and the contact centre near 8.2°N, 179.3°W.

The question. Are circular features found on the floor at the mirrored places of Serenitatis, Nectaris, the contact centre and the other near-side maria, at the scale 1.37?

What the model predicts. A mirror image of the near side, with Imbrium in place (Paper 9, C.3).

Data. Bathymetry; gravity.

Where in the series. Paper 9 §G, Study 8; §C.3.

88 Stripes Study 9 — the stripes beneath the dated layers

The sea floor of the Pacific is dated at most about 180 Myr, while the pear fell back at the release, 3.873 Ga. The model reconciles the two by depth: the dated sea floor is the last layer laid on an older fold. The stripes are then a structure of the layer beneath the dated surface, a relief that the new layers have draped rather than created. This reading is a first reading (orange in Paper 9 §H). It is tested where the stripes are crossed by seismic reflection profiles — sections of the floor made by sending sound waves down and recording their echoes from the layers below — and by drill holes.

The question. Does the fold continue below the youngest layers, with thinner layers over its crests and thicker layers in its troughs?

What the model predicts. The dated sea floor (at most about 180 Myr) is the last layer laid on an older fold (Paper 9, F.1).

Data. Seismic reflection profiles; drill holes of the Deep Sea Drilling Project and its successors (DSDP, ODP, IODP).

Method. The thickness of each layer measured across the fold, on profiles and holes that cross a stripe.

Where in the series. Paper 9 §G, Study 9; §F.1; §H; Paper 1 §D.1.

89 The Pacific province as the returned pear (R10, R11)

When the Moon left the tip, the rest of the pear — about nine lunar masses, seven to twelve in the range of Paper 9, some 11% of the Earth’s mass — had nothing to hold it out and fell back whole. All of it lay inside the corotation radius, so none of it could stay in orbit (requirement R10 of Paper 11). Its heavy part sank to the base of the mantle beneath the base of the pear. We propose — with reservation, since the nature of the deep provinces is still open and the Pacific one may in part be core-related material that did not finish sorting — that this returned pile is the Pacific large low-velocity province: a region of continental size at the bottom of the mantle, just above the core, through which seismic waves travel more slowly than through the mantle around it (R11). The African province, the second of the two, is read as the undisturbed lobe opposite. The position has passed: five of five tomographic models vote the point beneath Kiritimati slow, and it lies inside the −1%-1\% contour of SMEAN and SMEAN2, averages of several shear-wave models, at 2,800 km depth. What remains is composition, shape and the isotopes of the plumes that rise from it. Seismic waves slow down both in hotter material and in material of different composition, so the test must reach beyond velocity.

The question. Is the Pacific province richer in iron, denser, lower and broader than the African one? Is its northern part the base of the pear? How is it related to the Hawaiian ultra-low-velocity zone, a thin patch at the base of the mantle where waves slow far more than in the province around it? Do the plumes that rise from it carry only the Earth’s signature?

What the model predicts. A returned, iron-rich pile beneath the base of the pear; about nine lunar masses (seven to twelve) returned whole, with no orbiting remnant (R10); the base over the northern part of the province, some 1,500 km north of its centre (Papers 3 and 11). Plumes from the province carry only the signature of the Earth.

Data. Tomographic models; constraints on density from the Earth’s normal modes (the whole-Earth vibrations excited by great earthquakes); isotopes of plume basalts (He, W, Ne, Xe).

Method. A comparison of the two provinces in density and shape; an isotope survey of the plumes of the Pacific and of the African province.

Where in the series. Paper 3 §E.1–E.4; Paper 11 §C.5, C.6 (R10, R11); Paper 1 §F.3, item 5; Paper 1 §D.1 (the Paper 3 row awaiting).

90 An independent structural reconstruction of the Pacific lithosphere

The series places the source region on the Pacific by reading the Moon: the base near Kiritimati, the back of the pear near 25°N, 165°E, the tip at 9.3°N, 169.3°E, about 3,760 km west of the base. Mars and other bodies

Paper 10 follows the idea of the series on a second planet by another route. 92 The gas push in a long conduit: the critical step

A free volcanic blast is limited to about 1 km/s. Paper 10 requires 2.4–2.9 km/s, and obtains it from trapped gas pushing a sealing plug along a confined path, as in a gas gun, whose limit is about 6.3 km/s. Along a path of 200–300 km the mean pressure needed is 0.25–0.47 GPa, comparable to the confining pressure of the surrounding material at that depth, 0.26–0.39 GPa, so the walls hold. The energy is 1.3×10221.3\times10^{22} J for Deimos and 3.6×10223.6\times10^{22} J for Phobos, with 101610^{16}–3×10163\times10^{16} kg of steam. The question. Can trapped gas in a deep chamber push a plug of crust along a conduit of 200–300 km to 2.4–2.9 km/s without breaching the walls or bypassing the plug?

What the model predicts. A mean driving pressure of 0.25–0.5 GPa, matched by the confining pressure along the path; a gas mass one to three times that of the plug (Paper 10).

Data. Rheology and strength of the Martian crust and mantle at depth; volatile contents of Martian magmas.

Method. A one-dimensional and then a two-dimensional model of a gas-driven plug in a deformable conduit, with gas expansion, wall stress and leakage past the plug computed together.

93 The soft plug that seals: the cycle that builds it

In Paper 10 the plug is not a rigid block of rock but a warm body of high viscosity that seals the conduit as a rubber stopper does, a few kilometres below the vent. The impact marks on both moons, folded inward, are read as marks made on warm, plastic bodies.

The question. Over what range of viscosity and temperature does a plug of Martian crust seal a conduit against 0.25–0.5 GPa and then leave as one body rather than fragmenting?

What the model predicts. A soft, sealing plug that leaves whole; the first plug small, light and wet, the second larger, drier and harder (Paper 10).

Method. A model of the cooling, sealing and failure of a viscous plug in a conduit, run through repeated cycles.

95 The ring of condensed steam: mass, structure and coupling

A plug fired from the surface reaches the height of the ring but not its orbit, and alone falls back within one revolution. In Paper 10 the missing speed comes from a ring that Mars built early from its own steam: a clear, hot gap near the planet, ice crystals and dust higher up, and clumps beyond about 13,000 km, the Roche limit for ice. To give a body its missing speed by perfect sharing of momentum the ring must hand over at least about 0.6 of the body’s mass; real coupling needs several times that, of order 101610^{16} kg. The account stands or falls with the ring.

The question. Can a young Mars build and keep such a ring, and does a body crossing it gain its orbit, round it and draw it into the equatorial plane before it is ground away?

106 The height difference between the two poles and the spin at formation

On Mars the two poles do not stand at the same height. The laser altimeter of Mars Global Surveyor (MOLA) shows the north polar region several kilometres lower than the south. The difference is part of the Martian dichotomy, the contrast between the low, smooth northern plains and the high, cratered southern uplands, and the literature has other explanations for it, among them a giant impact and a one-sided flow in the mantle. The author’s proposal relates the difference to the spin: the faster the body turned when it formed, the larger the difference between its poles should be. In the series the speed of spin is what sets how close the corotation radius lies to the surface and how much material a young body can lift (Papers 2, 10); the topic asks whether the same quantity also leaves its mark on the heights of the poles. The hypothesis has not been tested.

The question. Does the height difference between the north and south poles of a body grow with the rate at which the body turned when it formed?

What the model predicts. The faster the spin at formation, the larger the difference between the poles. This is a hypothesis to be tested, not a result of the series.

Data. Polar topography from orbital altimetry: MOLA for Mars (Smith et al. 1999, Science 284), LOLA for the Moon, and the corresponding data for the other bodies with measured topography; the spin of young Mars from Paper 10 (§§B.1–B.2) and the spins of the series for the Earth and the Moon.

Method. First, a model step: spin alone flattens a body equally at both poles, so the calculation must name the one-sided element that turns a faster spin into a larger difference between the poles, and derive the relation from it. Second, a comparison: the difference between the poles measured on the ground beneath the polar ice caps, which on Mars are kilometres thick, relative to the equipotential surface of each body, and set against the spin at formation. On Mars the poles of today may not be those of the time of formation, since a load as large as Tharsis can turn the planet beneath its spin axis; the comparison must allow for this.

Where in the series. Not yet in the series; raised in the author’s working notes (16.10.2026 collection). Related: topics 104 (one conduit on the Martian equator, and Tharsis), 105 (the other bodies and fast-spinning super-Earths) and 115 (north–south asymmetry with South Pole–Aitken removed).

GRAIL, the twin spacecraft that mapped the Moon’s gravity, gave four standard models of the thickness of the lunar crust (Models 1–4, differing in assumed density and porosity). 109 No foreign material: the isotopic decision (R14)

In the model about 99% of the lunar craters were made by returning terrestrial material: material raised from the Earth that came back down onto the Moon. The usual reading is the opposite, that the craters were made by asteroids and comets. The two readings differ in the material left behind. Elements formed in different parts of the early solar system carry different nucleosynthetic isotope patterns — small differences in the proportions of the isotopes of chromium, titanium, molybdenum, ruthenium and osmium, which act as a fingerprint of origin. Metal that was inside the ancient samples when they formed must carry the fingerprint of whatever made them. The model also makes a mass argument: if the visible craters were made by bodies from outside, the upper 5 km of the crust would have to hold 4.4–5.0×1019\times10^{19} kg of foreign mass, 2–6%, whereas the measured content is 0.9–2% at most, and the ruthenium measured so far is terrestrial. Fine space dust in the regolith is allowed by the model; it is not the material of the craters.

The question. Does the metal that was part of the ancient samples when they formed — the iron–nickel of the two dated Chang’e-6 norites, and the siderophile elements (elements that follow iron) in the melts of each basin of the maria — carry a terrestrial or a non-terrestrial isotopic signature (Cr, Ti, Mo, Ru, Os)?

What the model predicts. About 99% of the craters are made of returned terrestrial material (Papers 1, 5, 6, 8, 11). The norite metal is terrestrial (Paper 8, B14, fits in part; the isotopes decide).

Data. The Chang’e-6 norites CE6C0100YJFM002-158 and CE6C0200YJFM001EGP07-16; melts from Apollo and Chang’e crater sites; Pacific plume basalts, as the terrestrial reference.

Method. Nucleosynthetic isotope analyses. A mixing model of a terrestrial returning body with a terrestrial target yields one pattern; asteroidal bodies, including differentiated ones, yield a second, foreign pattern. The source state is fixed before the measurements are compared.

Where in the series. Paper 8 B14, §E.1–E.2, Part F row 1, §G.2 item 8; Paper 5 §J.7, Further work item 4, §M.3; Paper 6 §L.5, Further work item 6, §O.1; Paper 11 §C.6 (R14), §D.3; Paper 1 §E.2, §F.3 item 4, §D.1.

110A multi-system isotope balance (R1)

The Earth and the Moon are almost identical in their oxygen isotopes: Δ17\Delta^{17}O, the measure of the oxygen fingerprint, differs by −1±5-1\pm5 ppm. For the model this is expected, since the Moon is made of terrestrial material. But oxygen is one system of several. The same starting composition — the shell of the early Earth plus the deep upwelling that fed the pear — must also give the lunar values of titanium, chromium, tungsten and the volatile elements once it has passed through separation and evaporation. A balance that closes in one system and fails in another would not support the model.

The question. Does one composition — early-Earth shell plus deep upwelling, passed through separation and evaporation — fit the lunar data in O, Ti, Cr, W and the volatile elements together?

What the model predicts. Terrestrial material throughout. Oxygen: Δ17\Delta^{17}O =−1±5=-1\pm5 ppm between Earth and Moon (Paper 11).

Data. Published lunar and terrestrial isotope data.

Method. A multi-system mixing and fractionation model run with one set of inputs for all systems.

Where in the series. Paper 11, Further work item 2; §A.1, §B.1, §D.3; Paper 1 §E.1 (R1).

111 The metal budget: the core as a gauge (R2, R13)

The Moon’s core is small: about 1.4% of its mass (range 1–2%), with a radius of about 330 km. A rising foamed melt entrains droplets of that iron; some settle out on the way, some are carried up. The size of the lunar core therefore measures how deep the feed reached. The topic is to compute the entrained fraction and see whether it gives 1.4%.

The question. What fraction of descending iron does a rising foamed melt entrain, and does it give a lunar core of about 1.4% (1–2%) from a source near, but not at, the descending iron?

What the model predicts. The small core measures how deep the feed reached (Papers 3 and 11).

116 The composition of the lunar core: the Earth’s iron, caught on its way down

Topic 111 asks how large the lunar core should be; this topic asks what it is made of. Pure iron is not what planetary cores hold: some lighter element is always dissolved in the metal, and which element it is records how the core formed. Silicon and oxygen enter iron mainly when metal and silicate meet at the high pressures and temperatures of a deep, large core; sulphur enters at low pressure, as metal droplets settle through a silicate mantle. In the model the lunar core is the Earth’s iron. When the pear grew, the Earth’s iron was still descending toward its core; a large amount of it took part in the pear, most of it sank back to the Earth with the returning material, and the Moon kept what reached the tip (Paper 3, §C.10). That iron then gathered at the Moon’s centre. The lunar core is therefore terrestrial metal, but metal caught on its way down: it had not yet joined the deep core and equilibrated at its pressures. Its light element should record that: the sulphur of metal that equilibrated at the shallower pressures of the descent, rather than the silicon and oxygen of metal equilibrated at the base of a large core.

The question. Is the light element of the lunar core sulphur, rather than silicon and oxygen?

What the model predicts. The lunar core is terrestrial iron, carried up with the feed while the Earth’s iron was still descending (Paper 3, §C.10); its light element is mainly sulphur. A core whose light elements are mainly silicon and oxygen, as in metal equilibrated at the base of a large core, does not support this reading.

Data. The seismic core of Weber et al. (2011), radius about 330 km with a solid inner core of about 240 km, and its density and wave speeds; new seismic data from a future lunar geophysical network; laboratory measurements of the density and sound speed of iron–sulphur and iron–silicon–oxygen liquids at lunar core pressures.

Method. The measured density and wave speeds of the outer core compared with those of the candidate alloys at lunar conditions; a model of the light-element content of the Earth’s metal during its descent, carried to the tip and gathered at the Moon’s centre.

Where in the series. The composition of the core is not yet in the series; it is raised in the author’s working notes (16.10.2026 collection). The core as a gauge of the feed: Paper 3 §C.10; Paper 11 §A.2, §B.2 and §C.6 (R2, R13); topic 111.

117 The core offset as the residual of the centre-of-mass offset

The Moon’s centre of mass lies about 2 km from the centre of its figure, toward the Earth. Paper 11 explains it by the crust: a light crust thickened on the far side moves the balance point toward the near side, by 1.24–3.11 km for density contrasts of 300–750 kg/m³ in its example, and it assumes a symmetric core that adds nothing in this direction. Topic 112 asks for one density model of crust and centre of mass together. This topic adds a separate term. The author’s proposal is that the core itself is off-centre: the burner pushed it beyond the geometric centre of the Moon; before it had fully cooled it moved back toward the centre; and the Earth’s gravity then drew it toward the Earth until it froze where it is today. The scale is simple arithmetic: a core of 1–2% of the lunar mass moved by a distance dd moves the centre of mass by about 1–2% of dd, so 2 km corresponds to dd of about 100–200 km — and to more, since only the core’s excess mass over the mantle it displaces counts. One fact must be faced: the Earth’s gravity pulls the core and the rest of the Moon almost equally, and only the tide, which is weak, separates them. During the connection and the push of the burner the forces may have been different; that is to be computed.

The question. How much of the centre-of-mass offset, in size and in direction, is left after the crust and the topography are accounted for, and does that residual fit a core displaced toward the Earth?

What the model predicts. A residual toward the Earth, matching a core displaced by the order of 100–200 km or more. A residual of zero, or one pointing away from the Earth, leaves no room for a displaced core.

Data. LOLA topography (the centre of figure); GRAIL gravity and the GRAIL crustal-thickness models; the seismic core of about 330 km, about 1.4% of the mass (1–2%).

Method. The full offset vector computed from crust and topography with one density model (topic 112); the residual vector converted into the displacement of a core of the seismic size, with its excess density over the mantle; and a calculation of the forces that could hold the core off-centre — the tide, and the conditions of the connection and the burner — to see whether such a displacement could be reached and frozen in.

Where in the series. Paper 11 §A.7 (the total centre of mass includes the core), §B.7 (the crustal example with a symmetric core; the axis of the contact against the mean Earth line), Further work item 4; Paper 3 §E.5 makes the same kind of calculation for the Earth’s core under the pear. The displaced lunar core is raised in the author’s working notes (16.10.2026 collection).

118An orphan minimum of crustal thickness

In the model the crust of the Moon is thinned in a few known ways. Broadly, it is thinnest over the contact and thickens through the belt to the far side (topic 107). Locally, it is thinned beneath the openings, where a feed dome rose under each mare: Paper 5 found a jump in thickness of 8 km or more beneath 29 of the 36 marked openings, against 4 of 169 ordinary craters. And it is thinned beneath the large basins. A local minimum of thickness that lies under none of these — an orphan minimum, neither under a basin nor under an opening — has no place in the model. Such a minimum would be a difficulty, and the crustal maps have not yet been scanned for one.

The question. Does the crustal-thickness map contain a local minimum that lies neither under a basin nor under an opening?

What the model predicts. No such minimum exists. Every local minimum of the crust lies under an opening or under a basin; the broad thinning of the contact region is the ordering of the exposure zones, not a local minimum. An orphan minimum would be a difficulty for the model.

Data. The four GRAIL crustal-thickness models (Wieczorek et al. 2013); the openings of Paper 5; the catalogue of lunar basins identified in GRAIL gravity (Neumann et al. 2015).

Method. An automatic search for local minima in each of the four models, with the depth and size of a minimum fixed before the scan — for example the rings and the 8-km threshold of Paper 5 §D.2 (centre to 0.3 radius against a ring of 1.3–2.0 radii); each minimum found is matched against the openings and the basins, and the unmatched ones are listed in all four models.

Where in the series. Paper 5 §D.2 (the crust beneath the openings and the control craters); Paper 11 §B.8 (the exposure zones); Paper 3 §B.7 (the uneven crust). The scan for an orphan minimum is raised in the author’s working notes (16.10.2026 collection).

K.1 List 1 — the two key observations of the model

Future work outside this paper

Two subjects are named in the series as future work and are not numbered here. The first is the system of axes of the Earth and the Moon from release to today: four planes — the Earth’s equator, the Moon’s orbit, the Moon’s equator and the ecliptic — moving in two kinds of motion at different rates, with an unknown memory held by a frozen bulge. Its first step is the obliquity channel: the tilt of 3.8° given to the Earth’s spin at release, and whether the coupling of Sun, Earth and Moon grew it or damped it on the way to today’s 23.44°; a change of the Earth’s axis after separation may also make South Pole–Aitken appear out of line with the burner. The second is the magnetism of the Moon: the source of its ancient field, and its pattern over South Pole–Aitken — erased at the centre, strong on the trailing side, with the swirls of Ingenii — read in the series as a record of heat and of iron from the Earth. It includes the test of the depth and direction of the magnetisation, which should be strongest at the margins of the heated core and sourced at depth.
