The Craters

Craters Without Asteroids

The computational environment

This paper asks whether material thrown out of the forming body and falling back on it could land hard enough to make craters. The answer depends on a handful of quantities — the mass that pulls the material back, the radius it is thrown from, the spin of the surface, the speed of the throw — and on what each of them is taken to mean. The table fixes them once. Its upper rows set the stage and the source of the thrown material; its middle rows give the numbers of the worked example used throughout Parts J and K; its lower rows say what the target is, how the shock is computed, and which numbers of the series are not used here.

Several rows need a word of explanation. The stage and the source. In the LEF model the Moon was not thrown off finished: it was built as a body standing on the Earth, on the tip of a long extension of the Earth’s own material, the pear, and fed from within through openings in its shell (Paper 3). Before the body rose, its future outer shell lay on the parent as part of the parent’s surface; as the feed beneath it pushed up, the shell became the surface of a rising dome. The craters of this paper were made on that shell, while it was still open to the sky — first while it lay flat over the feed, then on the flanks of the dome — and the shell carried them up with it. The material that made them came from below, from the interior of the Earth, whose pressure and material passed through the pear to the openings; it did not come from pieces broken off the shell itself.

The mass, the radius and the spin. M0M_0 is the mass of the Earth and the future Moon together, since at this stage the Moon’s material had not yet left. The ejection radius of 6,721 km, a little larger than the radius of today’s Earth (6,371 km), is the distance from the centre at which the example is thrown, and the day of the example is 5.5 hours long. A spinning surface throws everything on it a little outward, so the pull a thrown body feels at that radius is somewhat less than the pull of the mass alone; with these values it is 8.25 m/s² (§J.3). The spin is held constant only for the minutes or hours of one flight; nothing is assumed about how it changed over the whole construction.

The field. A monopole field is the pull of a mass concentrated at a point: it depends only on the distance from the centre. K=GM0K=GM_0 is the strength of that pull. The real parent was neither a point nor unchanging, and once the body stood on it the field was that of two connected masses; the monopole is the simplest field in which the flight can be followed exactly, and Parts J and K say where it ceases to apply.

The ejection velocity. The speed uu at which the material leaves the opening is treated as a dial, set in turn to 2, 3, 5 and 8 km/s, and each setting is followed to its landing. The paper does not claim to have derived it from the pressure of the source; what the source must supply to reach it is stated as a requirement (§J.1).

The target and the shock. The surface that receives the landings is a strong outer layer over a softer, mobile base — strong enough to hold a crater, and carried up by what lies beneath. For the pressure at the moment of landing, both the arriving material and the target are taken as dense basalt of 2,700 kg/m³, a reference case with well-measured shock properties; it is not a claim that the arriving material was dense basalt rather than a foam of melt and gas.

The times and the distances. The whole construction, from the start of the bulge to release at 3.873 Ga, spans about 600 Myr: roughly 400 for the bulge and the pear, about 100 for the flattening and about 100 to release. That window is the time in which most of the craters were made; after release the Earth added small and middle craters from the drops it threw (Part M). The window is not the time a single flight took, and it is not read from crater counts. The Moon at release was round, its outer shell rigid. The distances of about 17,570 and 17,631 km belong to later stages, when the body stood on the Earth at a distance; they are the centre distances of those coupled states, not the radius of the surface on which the craters of the example land.

Abstract

The question. In the LEF model, deep material was ejected through openings in the Earth’s envelope, and part of it returned to the outer shell that later rose with the forming Moon as the surface of its dome. This paper examines a question separate from the transport of mass: can such returning material arrive at a velocity sufficient for cratering and for shock in the target?

The flight. In a central-gravity approximation without drag, the return velocity, the energy requirement and the velocity relative to a rotating surface are derived. Material ejected radially and returning to the same radius lands at a relative velocity equal to the ejection velocity. In the 5 km/s example a flight of about 28 minutes and a landing about 1,032 km west of the ejection site are obtained; for an ideal head-on basalt landing an estimated contact pressure of 45.6 GPa results. Gravitational return therefore does not require a slow landing, but the ejection velocity itself still requires an energy source. The derivation also shows that the dependence of landing velocity on target altitude does not imply a universal trend with time.

The conditions. The paper links the tests of ejection, flight, encounter and shock, with constraints placed on drag, break-up, mass of the returning body and preservation of the record: a kilometre-sized body crossing a vapour atmosphere of 100 bar keeps about half its speed, and material thrown faster than the escape speed does not come back. Nearly all the Moon’s craters, some 99%, are of returned terrestrial material. Read as external impacting bodies, the craters seen today would leave 2–6% of foreign material in the upper 5 km of the crust; the crust’s siderophile elements amount to 0.9–2%, and where their ruthenium isotopes have been measured they are the Earth’s.

The source. The source of the returning material is the maria, the 34 openings over feed domes of Paper 5 — most of them; Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, passed the Earth’s pressure and material into the Moon and did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). The ground around each carries its iron and titanium, falling off with distance: iron from 6.9 wt% within 25 km of the edge to 3.1 at 100–150 km, with an excess out to 600 km. Not every crater is seen: on the far side craters were buried together with the sources that made them, and on the floors of the maria three of every five craters of 10 km and more melted into the floor, while of 20 km and more only one is sharp, some two hundred times fewer than on the highlands; on the maria of the contact the large craters vanished altogether.

The longitudes. The longitudes add a record of how the craters fell (Parts F–I). Material ejected by the body itself lands toward its trailing half, 90°E, the more crowded (about 250 against 140–175 craters of 20 km and more per million km², maria and South Pole–Aitken removed); the leading half, 90°W, meets it head-on, and its craters carry more central peaks, jagged and lower rims and the roughest floors. Each part of a crater keeps a record of its own: the centre the energy of the landing; the rim the forces at that moment, highest on the far side and lowest on the leading half; the floor the ground beneath, lowest on the layered far side and highest on the near side. Inside the crater one composition runs from the centre to the rim at every longitude: the crater is filled with the returning material that made it. The attribution of a lunar crater population to this mechanism is tested by the criteria of §O.1.

What the abstract means, in plain words. The literature holds that the Moon’s craters were dug by bodies from space — asteroids and comets — striking at 15–20 km/s. This paper proposes a source much closer to home. While the Moon was being built on the Earth, material from deep inside was thrown up through openings in the shell, flew a few hundred or a few thousand kilometres up, and fell back on that same shell. The question is whether material falling back in this way can land hard enough to dig a crater and to leave the marks of shock in the ground. The paper answers it in two halves: first by following the flight itself, then by reading the craters of today’s Moon as the record such landings would leave.

Why the material comes back as fast as it left. A ball thrown straight up, with no air to slow it, comes back to the hand at the speed it was thrown: on the way up it trades speed for height, and on the way down it trades the height back for the same speed. Material thrown up from the parent does the same — 5 km/s out, 5 km/s back. The spin adds one thing. A point on a spinning surface already moves sideways, and material thrown straight up from it carries that sideways motion with it; when the material comes back down to the same distance from the centre, it again has exactly that sideways motion, because the turning motion of a body flying freely about the centre is conserved, as a skater keeps her spin when her arms come back to where they started. The ground it lands on moves sideways at the same rate, so sideways the two are at rest with respect to each other, and the whole of the 5 km/s is a straight-down blow. Up high, however, the same turning motion is spread over a larger circle, so the material turns more slowly than the ground beneath, as the skater turns more slowly with her arms out; the ground runs ahead, and the material comes down behind its starting point, to the west. At 5 km/s the flight rises 1,932 km, lasts 28 minutes and lands 1,032 km west (§K.1).

Why 5 km/s into basalt gives 46 GPa. When two pieces of the same material meet head-on, each slows the other equally, so the material just behind the front of the shock moves at half the landing speed: 2.5 km/s at a landing of 5 km/s. The front itself runs through basalt faster than that; measured on basalt, its speed is 3.5 km/s plus 1.3 times the speed of the material behind it, here 6.75 km/s. The pressure behind the front is the density times the two speeds: 2,700 kg/m³ ×\times 6,750 m/s ×\times 2,500 m/s, which is 45.6 GPa (§J.2). That is well above the pressures at which the minerals of basalt begin to record shock and change their structure [S4]; at 2 km/s the same calculation still gives about 13 GPa. A landing of a few km/s is therefore not a soft fall: it is a shock event, of the same kind as the literature reads in lunar craters, though below the speeds of a body from space.

What it costs. To be thrown at 5 km/s, each kilogram must be given 12.5 MJ of energy at the opening. This energy is supplied by the pressure from the Earth: the spin and the sorting, one mechanism, create the pressure in the Earth, and it passes through the pear to the openings and sprays the material out of them. The volatiles the material carries, expanding as it rises, assist. On the way back the material may cross a dense atmosphere of vapour; a body a kilometre across, crossing a vapour column with 100 bar at its base, keeps about half its speed (§J.3). Material thrown faster than the escape speed — the speed at which the pull of the parent can no longer bring it back — leaves and does not return. These are the conditions the mechanism must meet; Part O states each of them as a test.

What “nearly all the craters are of returned terrestrial material” means. It means that the matter which dug the craters was the Earth’s own: deep material that left through the openings and came back, not bodies arriving from outside the Earth–Moon system. Some 99% of the craters are of this kind; foreign material, if it is found at all, will be in very small amounts, apart from the fine dust that space delivers to every airless surface. This has a test that does not depend on the flight. A body from space brings its own mass, and part of it stays in the ground; such bodies are rich in the siderophile (“iron-loving”) elements — iridium, osmium, platinum, ruthenium — which the Moon’s own crust almost lacks, so a small addition stands out at once. Had the visible craters been dug from outside, they would have left 2–6% of foreign material in the upper 5 km of the crust. The whole of the crust’s siderophile elements, counted as if all of them lay in that layer, comes to 0.9–2%; and where the isotopes of ruthenium, which tell one source in the solar system from another, have been measured in lunar crater melts, they are those of the Earth (§L.5).

Where the material came from. The openings that threw it are the maria: in Paper 5 a mare is an opening over a feed dome, the body pushed up from beneath through which the material rose; its dark plain is only the last layer that froze in it. Most of the maria threw material; Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, passed the Earth’s pressure and material into the Moon and did not eject (Serenitatis only slight popping). If the maria threw the material, the ground around each must carry the mare’s iron and titanium, the most near the edge and less farther out — and it does. Not every crater survived to be counted. On the far side, the layers that later built its thick crust covered the old openings and the craters around them together. On the floors of the maria, which were still hot and soft when much material landed, three of every five craters of 10 km and more melted into the floor and lost their depth. The maria of the contact — the patch of the near side that still touched the Earth while attached, and that froze late, Serenitatis first, very close to the Earth, Imbrium after detachment — keep no large crater at all.

What the longitudes add. The Moon keeps one face toward the Earth, so one half always faces forward along its orbit (the leading half, centred on 90°W) and the other faces back (the trailing half, centred on 90°E). Once risen, the body threw material from its own openings as well, slowly — at most about its own escape speed — and its turning decided where that material fell back. On the trailing half the ground moved away from the falling material, which caught up with it from behind and landed gently; on the leading half the ground moved on into the falling material and met it head-on. The trailing half therefore collected more craters, and the leading half the harder landings, which show in the shape of its craters: more central peaks, rougher floors, more jagged and lower rims. Each part of a crater keeps a different record: the central peak, the rebound of the landing, records how hard it was; the rim records the forces of that moment; the floor records the ground beneath. And the composition of the crater changes in the same way from centre to rim at every longitude, because the crater is filled with the material that made it.

Introduction — the literature and the model

Paper 3 deals with feeding the body from within and with the rise of the outer shell as the surface of the dome, and proposes that the craters formed on the shell travelled with it. Here the process that runs during feed and rise is treated: what happens to material that left the ejection opening, returned and landed on the surface. The physics of the dome and of the connection is not derived again.

A crater and shock features record a landing, but the source of the returning body requires further evidence. Secondary craters — craters dug by material thrown out of a larger crater and falling back — are already known [S1], and the study of transfer of material from the Earth to the Moon preceded this model [S2]. Returning material making craters is therefore not new in itself. The novelty examined here is an internal ejection source at the construction stage, and landing on the shell before it became part of the lunar surface. The title of the paper is not a claim that all craters formed without asteroids.

WHAT THIS PAPER PROPOSES

The oldest craters on the Moon were made in it on the Earth. While the shell of Paper 3 still lay on the parent, deep material ejected through its openings rose, turned, and came back down on it. Gravity does not slow a returning body: material thrown radially from a rotating surface returns to the same radius at the speed it left — 5 km/s out, 5 km/s back, 28 minutes in the air, a thousand kilometres to the west. Five kilometres per second into basalt is 46 GPa at the contact, above every shock-metamorphism threshold. The craters so made rose with the shell onto the Moon and kept their size (Paper 3, §B.6; Paper 7, §D.5). Nearly all the Moon’s craters — some 99% — are of returned terrestrial material; foreign material, if it is found at all, will be in very small amounts, space dust apart.

What the mechanism must supply is stated as three numbers. The ejection energy — u2/2u^2/2, 12.5 MJ/kg at 5 km/s — is supplied at the openings by the pressure from the Earth, created by the spin and the sorting and passing through the pear; the volatiles in the material assist. A concentrated returning body must cross the vapour atmosphere: a kilometre-sized body through 100 bar keeps half its speed. And the shell must be strong enough to hold a crater kilometres across until it rises. Each is listed in §O.1 with its test; a shock trend with altitude and a shock ceiling are properties of one path, not of the mechanism, and are not claimed.

The maria are the source. The openings that threw the returning material are the maria, most of them; Imbrium and Serenitatis, the maria of the contact, did not eject, Serenitatis only slight popping (§C.1; Paper 5). The ground around every mare carries its iron, fading with distance. Not every crater is seen: on the far side the craters were covered by its layers together with the sources that made them (§D.1), and on the near side the floors of the maria melted about three in five (62%) of the craters counted on them (§D.2).

The longitudes record how hard the craters fell. The body threw material from its own openings, not far and not fast — at most about 2 km/s, its own escape speed. What it threw from the far side fell a little behind, toward 90°E; what it threw from 90°E fell back on 90°E; what it threw from 90°W fell back and was met head-on. The trailing half, 90°E, is therefore the more crowded, and the leading half, 90°W, the one landed on hardest: its craters carry more central peaks and more jagged rims (Parts F–I).

How the box reads. The first paragraph is the proposal and its physics in one line each. The craters were made on the shell while it was still part of the parent’s surface; the shell then rose, as the surface of the dome, and became the outside of the Moon, carrying its craters with it without changing their size. Gravity takes back on the way down exactly the speed it took on the way up, so a throw of 5 km/s is a landing of 5 km/s; a landing at that speed on basalt produces a pressure of 46 GPa at the point of contact, enough to leave the marks of shock that the literature reads as the signature of a crater made by a body from space. “Space dust apart” means that the fine dust every airless surface collects is not counted among the craters.

The second paragraph turns the open points into requirements. The energy needed to throw a kilogram at speed uu is u2/2u^2/2: 12.5 MJ at 5 km/s, which the pressure from the Earth supplies at the opening, with the expanding gas of the material assisting. A body falling back through a dense vapour is slowed by it, and a kilometre-sized body through a column of 100 bar keeps about half its speed — still a few km/s. And the shell must be firm enough that a crater made in it is not smoothed away before it rises. A higher target or a different path changes the speed of a single landing, but that does not make the shock rise or fall in a fixed way with time, and no such trend is claimed (§K.3).

The third paragraph names the source and says why the record is incomplete. The maria, most of them, are the openings; the ground around them carries what they threw. Two processes hid craters: on the far side, the layers that later thickened its crust covered the old openings together with the craters around them; on the near side, a mare floor still soft with heat took the craters that landed on it and melted them into itself, so that of the craters counted on the floors about three in five lost their form.

The fourth paragraph describes the second stage of cratering, after the body had risen and turned with the Earth. It threw material from its own openings, slowly. Where the ground moved away from the falling material the landing was gentle and the craters many; where the ground moved into it the landing was hard, and the craters there carry the marks of a hard landing.

A.2 The three heights

The three heights. The word depth hides three different heights, and the halves differ in one and not in another, so they are kept apart (Figure 1). Each is measured against a level of its own: (1) the rim above the land — how high the rim crest stands above the land around the crater; (2) the floor below the land — how far the floor lies below that same land; (3) the rim to the floor — how far the floor lies below its own rim, which is the same height whether read from the floor up or from the rim down. The third is the sum of the first two. A picture helps: a bowl set into a table with its lip standing above the tabletop. The lip above the table is the first height, the bottom of the bowl below the table the second, and the lip to the bottom the third. Two craters can have the same third height and differ entirely in how it is divided between the first two — one with a high rim and a shallow floor, the other with a low rim and a deep floor — and that division is what the longitudes record.

Each height is defined so that it can be measured on an elevation map without judgement. The rim crest is the highest point at each of 72 azimuths, between 0.85 and 1.15 of the radius; the land around is a plane fitted to the ring between 1.5 and 2 radii; the floor is the central part of the crater, out to 0.4 of the radius, with the central peak left out (§G.1). An azimuth is a compass direction from the centre of the crater; 72 of them are one every 5°, so the rim is traced all the way round. The land is read well outside the rim, at 1.5 to 2 radii, so that the rim and its nearest ejecta do not raise it. The central peak is left out of the floor, because a peak standing on the floor would otherwise make the floor look higher than it is. All three are divided by the diameter, so that a crater of 20 km and a crater of 80 km can be set side by side: what is compared is the shape, not the size.

Figure 1 shows a crater in cross-section: the dashed line is the level of the land around it, the short green arrow on the left the rim standing above that level, the orange arrow the floor lying below it, and the long red arrow on the right the whole drop from rim crest to floor. The central peak rises from the floor and is left out when the floor is read.

Craters were compared only within the same size bin — 20–30, 30–50 and 50–100 km. The irregularity of the rim and the roughness of the floor were compared within the deeper half of each bin, so that a rim softened or a floor filled would not pass for landing strength; the three heights were compared over all the craters. The reason for the deeper half is this: a crater that has been worn or partly filled looks smoother than it was made, and if such craters were mixed in, a half of the Moon with more worn craters would look as though it had been landed on more gently. Restricting the comparison to the deeper, better-kept craters removes that confusion.

The rims are not equally kept. By how well the rim is preserved, 43% of the 90°W craters fall in the better-preserved half, 50% at 90°E, 57% at 180° and 46% at 0°. If the rims were equally kept everywhere, each longitude would have 50%; the leading half, 90°W, lies below that and the far side, 180°, above it. This is erosion, not age. The trailing half is sheltered from the direction of the Moon’s motion, and over the years its rims were kept; the leading half faces that direction, and its rims were worn down. It is the reason the rim and floor measures above are read in the deeper half of each bin.

What we now have: the terms of the model, a vocabulary for the parts of a crater, and three heights, each with its own reference level, that are read separately in Parts F–I.

B.1The three stages of Paper 3

In the model the Earth, spinning fast, raised a bulge that was drawn out into a pear shape, and the body that became the Moon was built on its tip, its outer shell standing on the Earth as the surface of a dome (Paper 3). The construction ran through three stages, named by the change in the body’s shape.

The craters were made in the three stages of Paper 3. In the outward rounding, as the body seated on the tip of the pear became a ball, the craters already on the shell changed with the surface, alike everywhere, and new ones kept arriving. In the flattening that followed, most of the craters formed, and the largest: the differentiation had advanced, and the material ejected was light and rich in water. Differentiation is the separation of a melt into heavier and lighter parts, as cream rises on standing milk; by the flattening it had gone far enough that what reached the openings was the light, water-rich part. The force that threw it was the pressure from the Earth, created by the spin and the sorting and passing through the pear to the openings; its volatiles — water and the other substances that turn to gas easily — assisted, and the light material was thrown far and fast. In the inward rounding, as the flattening relaxed and the body densified, the craters kept their size and the poles gathered them (Paper 7).

C.1 The maria: where the returning material came from

The openings from which the returning material was thrown (Part J) are the maria (Paper 5). At the flat stage they are the openings of the shell on the parent, and the material they throw lands on it at the speeds of §K.1; later they are the openings of the body itself, and what they throw falls back on it at the body’s escape speed or less (§K.4). The two speeds are very different. On the parent, material thrown up at a given speed comes back to the same height at that same speed — 5 km/s out, 5 km/s back (§K.1) — as a stone thrown straight up returns to the hand at the speed it left. On the body, which is far smaller, the ejection was weaker: the escape speed, the speed above which thrown material does not come back, was about 2.0 km/s at 1,300 kg/m³ and 2.3 km/s at release (§K.4), and everything slower than that fell back.

One mechanism behind the throwing (the author’s insight). The spin, the sorting and the pressure are one mechanism: the spin creates centrifugal pressure and drives the sorting; the sorting concentrates heavy material and presses the rest outward. This is what throws material out of a young, fast, still-unsorted body — and most of it keeps returning. A body already sorted and slow can no longer form a pear or throw out a moon.

Most of the craters near a mare were made by that mare; where two maria lie close together, the returning material of one lands in the region of the other. A large part of what a mare throws falls back into its own mouth and is melted again; a landing outside the mare takes the material out of the cycle, and where it lands it makes a crater. The picture is that of a pot boiling over: most of the drops fall back into the pot, and only those that fall on the stove stay where they land. The material leaving a mare at any moment carries the mare’s concentration at that moment, so that the chemistry of a mare and of the craters beside it are expected to correspond. This last sentence is the basis of the source test of §C.5.

How the literature reads a crater. A crater is a hole dug by a body from outside — an asteroid or a comet — and a mare is a giant basin of the same kind, filled hundreds of millions of years later by basalt risen from the mantle. Some 170 floor-fractured craters, with shallow, cracked floors carrying mare patches and pyroclastic vents [S9], are read as ordinary craters lifted from below by an intrusion that spread as a sill beneath the floor. A pyroclastic vent is a place where gas-rich melt erupted as a spray of glassy beads; a sill is a flat sheet of melt that forced its way sideways between layers, lifting what lay above it.

How the model reads it. A crater is the place where returning material landed. There is no blow from outside and no intrusion: what lies beneath a crater is the layers it landed on, and what stands at its centre is the energy of the landing. The two readings agree on what is seen and differ on what made it. The difference can be tested, because the model ties each crater to a nearby source whose material it should carry; the reading of a body from outside ties it to no particular place on the Moon.

C.2What the crater takes from the mare

The floor of a mare, its feed dome and its rim are the subject of Paper 5 (Parts D–F), and are not repeated here. Two things are taken from it. A mare floor froze after most of the returning material had landed, so that it is the last record of the craters (§D.2). And the rim of a mare was built by spray: the maria that carry many small craters on their rims are likely the maria rich in volatiles, whose active spray landed on the rim and made small craters there (§O.1). The first point explains why mare floors carry few craters: they were still liquid, or melted again, while most of the craters were being made, and kept only what landed after they froze. The second gives a test that is listed with the others in §O.1: the small craters on the rim of a mare should be more numerous where the mare was richer in volatiles.

The trailing half is the densest and the leading half among the sparsest, in the order the table of §K.4 predicts. The prediction runs as follows. The attached body turned with the Earth. Material it threw up from its trailing half came down while that half moved away from it, and so caught up with the ground gently, and stayed; material thrown from the far side lagged a little behind the turn and drifted toward 90°E as well. The trailing half therefore collected its own ejecta and those of the far side. The leading half moved on into what it had thrown, as a runner moves into rain, and was struck harder but not more often.

The difference lies in the small and middle craters: below about 70 km the trailing half holds 1.2 to 2.4 times the leading half’s population per size bin, while above it the two are alike or the leading half the richer. This is what the model expects: the body’s own ejecta were weak, and made the small and middle craters; the largest craters formed in the flattening, from the strong ejections of that stage, and are spread more evenly. The size distribution points the same way: the population at 90°E is displaced toward smaller craters, by a factor of 0.75–0.81 against 90°W, as softer landings of the same mass would make it, though the interval of that factor still includes one. A factor below one means that the same population of landings made somewhat smaller craters on the trailing half; because its interval still includes one, this measure supports the reading but does not yet decide it alone. The crowding of 90°E is also read through the maria of that half (Paper 5); the two readings are one, since the maria are openings and the openings eject.

Behind the scenes. An earlier version of this count called 90°E the leading face, and had the attached body overtake material lagging behind it. Under the convention of the series 90°E is the trailing half, and the excess counted there had been given the wrong name. Reading the flights from the body’s own openings, rather than from the parent, put the excess where it is and added the second prediction, of landing strength.

The phenomenon. The Moon’s large craters are not spread evenly in longitude. The gap in knowledge. The literature expects an excess on the leading half, 90°W, because the Moon runs into the bodies in its path; the model expects an excess on the trailing half, 90°E, because the body’s own ejecta fell back toward it (§K.4). A clean count that removes the maria, which erase craters, has to decide between them. The research question: with the maria and South Pole–Aitken removed from every region, which of the four longitudes holds the most craters of 20 km and more, and does the difference lie in the small craters or in the large?

For bodies arriving from outside, a synchronously rotating Moon meets more of them on its leading side; models of the present bombardment predict a leading-side excess of some tens of per cent [S22, S23]. A synchronously rotating Moon is one that turns once on its axis in each orbit and so keeps the same face to the Earth; its leading side then always meets the oncoming bodies first, as the windscreen of a moving car collects more rain than its rear window. The excess is used to correct the crater chronology of the Moon [S22]. Counting in equal caps on a global catalogue [S6] is the standard method. What is new here: the four longitudes are counted side by side, the maria and SPA removed from each cap, and with the large basins removed as well, so that no region is favoured by what was erased in it.

The trailing half, 90°E, holds more craters of 20 km and more than the leading half, 90°W; the excess lies in the small and middle sizes, made by the body’s own ejecta, and not in the largest craters.

The sample: the craters of 20 km and more of the Robbins catalogue [S6] in four caps of 30° radius on the equator, centred on 0°, 90°E, 180° and 90°W. The tools: the catalogue, the LROC mare map, a mask of SPA and of the 20 large basins, and code that counts craters and areas on the sphere. Is the difference larger than chance? A count of craters scatters by about its square root; the interval of a ratio of two densities follows from the two counts. C.4 The crater record is the record of the maria

The crater record is the record of the maria. If the maria made the craters, the density of craters is a map of where the maria worked — the visible maria and the buried feed domes of §D.1 alike — and the last craters to form are those of the last active maria; older craters beneath them were erased by the same heat. An active mare scatters its own craters around it — the rim of Crisium is ringed by them — and where there are no maria and only heat, craters were erased and none were made. Material thrown by a mare can also land far from it: a crater far from any mare may belong to a visible mare or to a buried feed dome. A buried feed dome is a source that worked early and was later covered by the layers of the far side, together with the craters it had made; it is seen today only in the gravity field (Paper 5; §D.1).

The record is the last one written, not the first. A surface keeps the craters that landed on it after it last froze; what landed before was melted or covered. The large maria of the near side are the last of all: at release they were cut off from the Earth’s pressure; they were then hot and of low viscosity, and froze slowly, from the outside inward, over hundreds of millions of years — after most of the returning material had landed. Their surfaces are smooth and carry few craters, at most remnants here and there: Mare Imbrium holds 10 craters of 20 km and more on 871,000 km² of floor, 11 per million km², against 205 on the highlands (Paper 5, §I.5; credited in an earlier version to Paper 3, §C.10). The floor of Imbrium thus carries about one crater for every eighteen that the same area of highland carries: it froze after most of the craters had fallen.

C.5The source test

The question this section answers is whether a crater carries the material of the mare that made it. If it does, the maria are not only where the craters lie but where their material came from.

The source test. Material thrown by a mare carries its chemistry. A crater near a mare that carries that mare’s signature — iron, titanium, thorium — was made by it; a crater without it came from farther away. The test is stated in §O.1: the craters around each mare are chosen before their chemistry is read, and the fraction carrying the mare’s signature is measured against distance from it. Choosing the craters first, and only then reading their chemistry, keeps the choice from being steered, even unknowingly, by the answer.

One caution is built into the test, and it concerns how hard the returning material landed. A hard landing digs out and exposes the ground beneath, and the crater then shows mostly the chemistry of that ground; a soft landing may hardly expose the ground, and more of the returning material stays on the surface. How hard it landed depends on where: the leading half, 90°W, takes the hard landings, the trailing half, 90°E, the soft ones. It was measured by longitude, and the inside of the crater holds the same material on both halves (§L.4). The caution therefore does not divide the result: the material inside the crater is the same whether the landing was hard or soft.

The source test, measured. The maria are the 34 openings of Paper 5, identified on the gravity gradients, each over a feed dome (34 in Paper 5, Appendix A; the crust test there used the 36 original marks); the dark plains without a feed dome — Procellarum, Tranquillitatis, Fecunditatis and the like — are not maria but crust melted in place (Paper 5, §C.2), and they are read apart. A gravity gradient is how fast the gravity changes from one place to the next; it is strongest at the edge of a dense body beneath the surface, and so draws its outline. The test was run on 11,574 craters of 5–60 km outside the basalt, around six maria (Crisium, Imbrium, Moscoviense, Nectaris, Serenitatis, Smythii) and three plains (Procellarum, Tranquillitatis, Fecunditatis), chosen and frozen before any chemistry was read, with 5,922 matched control craters more than 600 km from any basalt [S10]. The controls are craters of the same sizes, far from any mare, where no mare’s material can be expected; they give the level of the ground that owes nothing to a mare. C.6 Crater size and mass of the returning body

The question this section answers is how large a returning body had to be to make a crater of a given size on the parent. It matters because the parent differs from the Moon of today in two ways that pull in opposite directions: its gravity was stronger, which makes a crater smaller for the same blow, and the landing was slower than an asteroid’s, which also makes it smaller. A crater of the same size therefore needed a heavier returning body. The tool is a scaling law: a rule, fitted to experiments, that connects the size of a crater to the size and speed of what made it and to the gravity of the target.

In an ideal gravity-regime scaling law, with the other material properties fixed:

The term v2/gv^2/g says that a faster landing digs wider and a stronger gravity holds the crater back. The exponent μ\mu is a property of the target material, measured in experiments: higher for a solid, non-porous target and lower for a loose or porous one; this paper examines 0.35 and 0.55, and β\beta is derived from it. Gravity regime means that the size of the crater is limited by the weight of the material that must be lifted out, not by its strength, which is the case for craters kilometres across.

mp∝g3μ/2v−3μ.m_p\propto g^{3\mu/2}v^{-3\mu}.\tag{2}

It says that the mass needed grows with the gravity of the target and falls with the landing speed: a heavier blow is needed where gravity is stronger and where the landing is slower.

Comparing an external impacting body at 15 km/s in gravity 1.62 with a landing at 5 km/s in gravity 8.25, a mass ratio of about 7.45 is obtained for μ=0.35\mu=0.35, and about 23.47 for μ=0.55\mu=0.55. The first case is the reading of the literature: a body from outside landing on today’s Moon, whose gravity is 1.62 m/s². The second is the model’s: returning material landing at 5 km/s on the parent at the ejection radius, where the gravity was 8.25 m/s². The gravity is about five times larger and the speed three times smaller, and both raise the mass needed; the ratio is therefore between about 7 and about 23, depending on μ\mu. These are sensitivity tests of the scaling law [S5], not a mass reconstruction of particular craters. The target coefficient, density of the returning body, strength, layering and the transition from excavation cavity to final crater require calibration.

This test matters because the proposed crater formed on the parent body before being transported. It must not be computed automatically in the present lunar gravity; once made, it rode on the shell and kept its size (Paper 3, §B.6; Paper 7, §D.5). A low magma density does not fix the porosity of the crustal surface or its μ\mu. The density of the body as a whole says nothing direct about how loose the uppermost layer was where the crater formed, and it is that layer that sets μ\mu. How much of the crust the returning bodies built is therefore not fixed here: a reconstructed catalogue, a calibration for each target and a fraction of retained mass are missing. Each of the three is a requirement for turning the sensitivity into a mass budget, and they are listed with the further work.

Its message is the steepness of the curve: across the plausible range of μ\mu the ratio changes by a factor of three, which is why the target must be calibrated before a mass is assigned to any crater.

What we now have: the source of the craters named (the maria and the buried feed domes), their count by longitude with the trailing half the densest, the mare’s iron found in the ground around every mare and fading with distance, and the scale of the returning body bounded by a sensitivity, not yet by a calibration.

PART D

The craters, visible and buried

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

not every crater that was made is seen today. The approach: the craters are counted where they lie on the ground, where they were buried with the sources of the far side, and where the floors of the maria covered them, each against the land around it with the maria removed from both sides.

Part C showed that the maria are the source of the returning material, so that the density of craters is a map of where the maria worked. A map of that kind can be read only if one knows which craters it has lost. This Part counts the missing craters. A crater can disappear from view in two different ways, and the model keeps two different words for them.

Buried is used here only for the far side, and only together with the source. A feed dome that worked early and then stopped was covered, with everything around it, by the layers that later built the far side; the craters it had made lay on the same ground and went under with it. Nothing of them is seen; the dome beneath is found by its gravity alone (§D.1).

Melted is used for the floors of the maria. A mare is an opening over a feed dome, and while it was active its floor was liquid or soft. Returning material that landed on it made a crater in ground that had not yet frozen: the crater sank into the floor and closed, and at most its ring is seen today, standing almost at the level of the mare (§D.2). The literature knows these rings too, but calls them buried or flooded craters: in its reading a crater was dug into an older, solid surface, and basalt that rose much later filled it. The two readings differ in what came first. In the literature the crater is older than the basalt; in the model the crater was made while the basalt was still active. For that reason this paper does not call a ring on a mare floor “buried”; the word appears below only where the literature’s reading is described. The two readings also leave different traces — in the mass beneath the crater (§D.3) and in its rim (§E.6) — and these are tested.

D.1 Craters buried with the sources of the far side

The question of this section is whether the craters made by the hidden sources of the far side went under together with them, and whether the large craters seen today depend on the visible maria alone.

The sources that are no longer seen. The visible maria are not the only sources. Paper 5 (§D.4–D.5) finds 73 feed domes in the gravity field: dense round bodies, each the frozen remainder of the material that rose beneath an opening. Of the 40 on the far side, 36 are buried under bright highland, with the same iron and thorium above them as around them and a crust thicker by 8–13 km. Both facts say the same thing: the surface over these domes is the ordinary cover of the far side, laid over them after they stopped working, and the extra crust is the thickness of that cover. A source that worked early, through the flattening, and stopped, was covered by the layers that built the far side — the parent’s sial and returning material, laid down layer upon layer (Paper 3, §B.7) — and the craters it had made were covered with it. This has a consequence that can be measured. A source scatters craters around itself, so an active source that stayed in view would be surrounded by a crowd of craters. Around the buried domes the craters do not gather: their density on the dome and around it is the same as or lower than farther out (0.48–0.95 of the ground at 3–5 radii), because the craters made in their time were buried with them. A ratio of one would mean “as many as on the ground farther out”; every value here is at or below it.

The large craters. The large craters, of 20 km and more, were mapped by their density over the whole Moon, with the mare ground removed, and the visible maria and the feed domes laid over the map (Figure 3). The mare ground is removed because a mare floor has lost its craters for its own reason (§D.2); keeping it would make every region with maria look poor in craters and would mix the two questions. Their densest ground lies in the north of the far side, between 140°W and 180° and between 120°E and 150°E, in the south of the near side below Nectaris and Nubium, and in the southeast of the far side; the sparsest lies around the large maria of the near side and around Orientale. Away from today’s maria the large craters are denser, not sparser: for 10–50 km, 467 per million km2^2 within 150 km of a mare edge against 1,040 beyond 1,000 km — more than twice as many far from the maria as next to them. That does not make them foreign to the visible maria: what a mare throws can land far from it, as a stone thrown hard lands far from the hand. The large craters seen today come from the feed domes of both kinds — the buried domes of the far side and today’s visible maria.

Why some sources made the largest craters is part of the same history. The water came late: differentiation — the sorting of the parent’s material by density and by what it carries — advanced through the construction, and the later outflows were the richer in volatiles (Paper 3, §B.7); a mare rich in volatiles threw the water-laden material that made the largest craters of the flattening (Paper 3, §B.6). The other side of the same sorting is the iron: the deeper the layer, the more iron in it, and as the volatiles left, the heavy material stayed — the frozen feed dome is the densest and most iron-rich body of all. That is why the domes can be found in the gravity field even where nothing on the surface shows them.

The phenomenon. The gravity field shows dense round bodies under bright highland, where no mare is seen, most of them on the far side (Paper 5, §D.4). The gap in knowledge. If these are sources that worked early and were then covered, the craters they made must have been covered with them; and the large craters seen today must not depend on the visible maria alone. The research question: do the craters gather around the buried feed domes, and where on the Moon do the large craters lie relative to the visible maria and the buried domes?

Buried maria, “cryptomaria”, are known from dark-halo craters that dug up basalt from under a highland cover, and from their gravity and composition [S24]; the literature reads them as early lava flows covered by the ejecta of later basins. The global density of large craters is the standard measure of the age of a surface: the longer a surface has been exposed, the more craters it has collected. What is new here: the crater density is read on and around every buried dome against the ground farther out, and the global map of large craters is read against both the visible maria and the buried domes together.

The craters made by a buried source in its time were buried with it, so the craters do not gather on and around the buried domes; and the large craters seen today come from both kinds of source, so they are not sparsest far from the visible maria.

D.2 Craters melted into the floors of the maria

The second way a crater is lost is on the floor of a mare. This section asks how many of the craters on the mare floors landed while the floor was still active and melted into it, and how many were made after it froze.

The test. Every crater of 10 km and more whose centre lies on basalt was taken from the catalogue of Wang & Wu [S16], which gives the depth of each crater from the global elevation models, and assigned to the floor of a mare — within the core of the feed dome beneath one of the 34 maria of Paper 5 — or to a plain, the basalt outside every mare. The difference matters: the floor of a mare is the surface of the opening itself, over its feed dome; a plain is crust melted in place, without a channel from below. Its depth-to-diameter ratio was compared with that of the highland craters of the same size, the basalt removed from the highland sample. The highland craters serve as the measure of a normal crater, one made on firm ground and left as it was made. A crater shallower than 95% of the highland craters of its size is counted melted: it landed on the active ground, melted into it, and is seen only as a ring. One shallower than 75% of them is smeared: its form survives, but its floor has filled and its walls have slumped. The rest are sharp, made after the ground froze. Put the other way round, the rule would call only 5 of every 100 ordinary highland craters melted; a share far above that on the mare floors cannot be the ordinary spread of crater depths. A crater that melted and vanished into an active mare is in no catalogue; it is seen only as the absence of craters on the floor (Paper 5, §G.2).

The phenomenon. Many craters on the floors of the maria are seen only as rings: their floors stand almost at the level of the mare. Others stand on the floor whole and deep. The gap in knowledge. How many of the craters on a mare floor melted into it, and how many were made on it after it froze, has not been counted against a control of the same size, with the floors of the maria apart from the plains. A plain picture helps: a pond that is freezing. Stones of wax that fall into the warm water melt and leave a ring on the surface, or vanish altogether; stones that fall after the ice has formed lie on top, whole. Counting them tells when the ice formed relative to the falling stones. The research question: of the craters of 10 km and more on the floors of the maria and on the plains, what share melted, how many were made after the ground froze, and does the share change toward the contact?

What is known. Craters seen on the maria only as rings, “ghost craters”, have been known since the first lunar maps, and craters under the near-side maria described in the literature as buried [S21] have since been found in the gravity field. The literature reads them as craters dug by external impacting bodies on an older surface and then flooded by basalt that rose later. The density of the craters counted on top of a mare is used to date its surface [S20]. What is new here. The floors of the maria — the basalt over the feed domes — are set apart from the plains, every crater is measured by its depth against highland craters of its own size, and melted and sharp craters are counted in one sample, by one rule.

In the model the floors of the maria froze after most of the returning material had landed (§C.2). The hypothesis has three parts. (a) Most of the craters counted on the floors of the maria are melted. (b) The craters made on the floors after they froze are few, far fewer than on the plains and the highlands. (c) Toward the contact, where the ground stayed liquid the longest, the craters melt more, and on the maria of the contact the large ones vanish altogether.

D.3 The mass under the melted craters of the maria

A crater that landed on an active mare entered it and vanished; what is seen of it on the floor is a ring. Whether the material that vanished left its mass behind was read in the gravity field, under each crater, against the ground around it. This is the test that can separate the two readings of a ring. If the crater was dug into solid ground and later filled with basalt, as the literature holds, the basalt in it forms a body with the shape of the crater, and a body with a shape has an edge. If the returning material sank into a liquid mare and mixed, as the model holds, it left extra mass with no shape and no edge.

The measurement. Under every crater of 20 km and more on the near side, the height of the base of the crust was read from the GRAIL crustal model [S31]. The base of the crust is the boundary between the light crust and the denser mantle beneath it; the crustal model places it from the measured gravity. Where the base stands higher than around, the dense mantle comes closer to the surface, and there is more mass. A plane was fitted to the ground at 1.5–3 radii and removed, and the centre (within 0.3 radius) was read against it; removing the plane takes away any broad regional tilt, so that only what lies under the crater itself remains. The maria are the 34 openings of Paper 5, each with the core of its feed dome; the plains are the basalt outside them.

The test uses the GRAIL Bouguer field [S31]: the measured gravity after the pull of the visible relief has been computed and removed, so that what remains shows denser or lighter material below the surface. Three large craters stand apart — of 68, 122 and 159 km — where the edge of the crater still stands out on the floor.

The phenomenon. On the floors of the maria many craters are seen only as rings (§D.2). The gap in knowledge. In the literature a ring on a mare is a crater flooded by basalt, and the basalt that fills it should weigh as a body shaped like the crater. In the model the crater entered the active mare and vanished in it. Whether the vanished material left mass, and whether that mass has the shape of a body, has not been measured against the floor around each crater. The research question: under the melted craters of the maria, is there more mass than under the floor around them, and does it have the edge of a body?

Craters under the near-side maria described in the literature as buried [S21] were found in the GRAIL field as rings of gravity, and read as craters flooded by basalt. The crustal model and the Bouguer field are those of the GRAIL mission [S31]. What is new here: each crater is read against random points on its own floor, so that the dome beneath the mare is removed, and the edge of the mass is read in the steepness of the gravity.

The returning material that landed on an active mare entered it and vanished, and left its mass in the mare without the shape of a body. Hence: under the melted craters of the maria there is more mass than under their own floor; under the melted craters of the plains, where there was no active mare, there is none; and the mass has no sharp edge.

Type of research: quantitative, with two control groups. The sample: the 17 melted craters of 20 km and more on the floors of the maria; the controls: 50 random points on the same floor for each crater, of the same size, and the melted craters of the plains and the highland craters of the same sizes. The random points answer the question “what does any spot on this floor show?”; the plains and highland craters answer “what does a crater show where there was no active mare?”. The tools: the GRAIL crustal model and Bouguer field [S31], the elevation models [S7], the catalogue of Wang & Wu [S16], the feed domes of Paper 5, and code that reads each crater in rings of its own radius.

The craters were classified by depth before the gravity was read (§D.2), so the choice of craters could not lean toward the gravity result. For each crater the centre was read against the ring at 1.5–3 radii, and the same was read at 50 random points on basalt within the same dome core. The steepness of the Bouguer gravity was read at 0.8–1.2 radii against 0.3–0.6 and 1.5–2.0 radii — that is, in a band around the edge of the crater, against a band inside it and a band outside it. More mass. The melted craters of the maria stand above their own floor by a median of +0.8+0.8 km, in 13 of 16 (Wilcoxon test, probability 0.04); the excess does not grow with the size of the crater once the floor is removed (rank correlation 0.18). The Wilcoxon test asks whether the 16 differences, crater minus own floor, lean to one side more than chance would allow if the true difference were zero; a probability of 0.04 means that chance would produce so one-sided a result about 4 times in 100. A rank correlation of 0.18 is weak: larger craters do not carry clearly more excess. Not on the plains. The melted craters of the plains carry +0.08+0.08, against −0.41-0.41 in the highland. No edge. The steepness at the edge is 1.04 under the melted craters of the maria against 1.00 under their floors (probability 0.09), as under every other group. Here a high probability is the expected result: chance alone would give a difference as large as 0.04 about 9 times in 100, so the edges of the melted craters are not steeper than the floor around them.

Result. The hypothesis is supported: the material that landed on the active maria vanished into them and left its mass there, without the shape of a body; on the plains it left none. Limitations. The craters are few and lie on seven maria, most on Smythii; the excess is small against the dome beneath; the gravity resolves some 8 km, so a thin edge could escape it. Directions for further research. The same reading on the far-side maria, and on the craters of 10–20 km with a field of higher degree.

PART E

Size and state: the maria and the craters in time

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

the craters on the floors of the maria are not all in one state: some are melted, some smeared, some sharp, and the state follows the size. The approach: the state of every crater on the near-side mare floors is measured against its size, and read through the temperature and viscosity of the maria and of the arriving material, and through time. This part is shared by Papers 5 and 6: it belongs to the maria and to the craters alike. The same text stands as Part G of Paper 5, and the study numbering is joint with Paper 5: the main study of this part is Study 5 in this paper and Study 8 in Paper 5.

The previous parts named the maria as the source of the returning material (Part C) and counted the craters that are no longer seen, among them those that melted into the floors of the maria (Part D). This part looks at those floors directly. In the model a mare is an opening over a feed dome (Paper 5), and its floor is the frozen surface of the melt that stood in that opening. A frozen floor is also a surface that receives craters, and the craters it carries tell how soft it was when they arrived. This part reads that record. By size and state we mean two properties of each crater set side by side: its diameter, and how well it has kept its form. A crater is sharp when it is as deep as, or deeper than, a fresh highland crater of the same diameter, with a standing rim and an open bowl; smeared when its rim still stands but its floor is partly filled and its walls are softened, so that it is noticeably shallower than a highland crater of its size; and melted when little more than its ring is left, the floor filled up to nearly the level of the surrounding plain. The question is simple: does the state of a crater depend on its size, and if so, what does that say about when the floors of the maria were still soft?

The part runs in seven steps. Section E.1 shows the three states side by side on one mare. Section E.2 measures the state of every crater of 3 km and more on the near-side basalt against its size (Study 5). Sections E.3 and E.4 give the two causes the model reads in the result: the cooling of the maria, and the change of the material that kept arriving from the Earth after release. Section E.5 joins the two into one sequence in time. Section E.6 turns to the large craters of the plains of the contact, whose rims were worn from below (Study 15), and E.7 states what the part shows.

Throughout, the contact is the last 3,000 km of the Moon’s base on the Earth, the disc where the Moon last touched the Earth, centred today at 24.1°N 335.2°E (Paper 3, §B.4); beyond the contact is the rest of the near-side basalt. A floor of a mare is the basalt over the core of a feed dome (Paper 5, Appendix A); a plain is basalt without a feed dome beneath it, such as Procellarum or Tranquillitatis.

E.1Three states on one floor

Before measuring thousands of craters it helps to see the three states on one surface. The yardstick is the depth-to-diameter ratio, the depth of a crater divided by its width. Fresh craters of a given size have a typical ratio, so a crater much shallower than its highland neighbours of the same size has been filled or worn, and one as deep or deeper has kept its form.

Within Mare Imbrium three craters show three states. Sinus Iridum, 246 km, is melted in half: its northern rim stands as the Jura Mountains, its southern half is gone, and its floor is the floor of the mare; its depth is a quarter of that of a highland crater of its size (depth-to-diameter 0.008 against 0.034). Seen from above it is a bay: an arc of mountains open toward the mare, with the mare’s own smooth surface running in where the southern rim would have been. Plato, 101 km, on the northern shore, kept its rim, but its floor is filled and smooth: it is smeared, at 0.020 against 0.034, a little more than half the depth of a highland crater of its size. Its ring of walls is complete, but inside it lies a flat, dark floor instead of a bowl. Kirch, 11 km, on the floor of the mare, is sharp, deeper than the highland craters of its size (0.166 against 0.078): a clean bowl with a crisp rim. The large crater is melted, the middle one smeared, the small one sharp.

The three stand on or beside the same mare, so differences of place, latitude or of the ground beneath cannot explain them. What differs is the size, and with it, as the following sections show, the time at which each arrived.

E.2The state of the craters by size

Two kinds of melting. A crater that landed on an active mare could be melted in two ways. Melted and vanished: it landed, melted, spread and disappeared while the mare was active, merging with the material of the mare; no body is left beneath the surface, at most remnants, and no ring; it is absent from every catalogue, and is seen only as the deficit of craters on the floors. Melted to a ring: it lost depth, its floor filled, and only its ring is seen; it is in the catalogue as a crater far shallower than a highland crater of its size. In this part melted alone means a crater melted to a ring, still seen; vanished means a crater melted and vanished. The distinction matters because the two are counted in different ways: a melted crater is counted by its state, a vanished one only by its absence, as a shortfall against a surface that kept its craters.

The measurement. Every crater of 3 km and more on the near side whose centre lies on basalt was taken from the catalogue of Wang & Wu [S16], with its depth from the global elevation models, and assigned to the floor of a mare — within the core of the feed dome beneath one of the 34 maria of Paper 5 (Appendix A there) — or to a plain. Its depth-to-diameter ratio was compared with that of the highland craters of the same size, latitude within 60°, basalt removed from the highland sample. Shallower than 95% of the highland craters of its size, a crater is melted: only its ring is seen. Shallower than 75% of them, it is smeared. The rest are sharp. Of the 3,478 craters, 324 lie on the floors of the maria and 3,154 on the plains.

The comparison is made size by size for a reason. Large craters are naturally shallower for their width than small ones, so a single depth threshold would call every large crater filled. Comparing each crater only with highland craters of its own size removes that effect: a crater is called melted only if it is shallower than almost every highland crater of the same diameter. The highland craters are the control because the highland was solid when they formed and has no basalt in it.

Why does a melted share that rises with size tell when the floor was still soft? A crater’s final state is set by the floor it lands on, not by its size alone: the size effect has already been removed by comparing each crater with highland craters of its own size. The maria of the contact were the last to freeze (§E.3): they lay where the Moon last touched the Earth, were fed the longest and are the deepest — deepest in the body of the mare itself, inward, not in the height of its floor (§E.3). Any large crater that landed on them while they were liquid melted and vanished, and so did most small ones of that time. What is left on their floors is what arrived after they froze: small pieces, which made sharp craters on solid ground. The contact maria therefore carry fewer craters than the others, and a larger share of those they carry is sharp. Their sharp small craters are not a sign that they are old and untouched; they are the late arrivals from the Earth, landing on a floor that had only just become hard.

The phenomenon. On the floors of the maria some craters are melted to a ring, some are smeared, and some are sharp (§E.1); and the floors carry few craters. The gap in knowledge. Whether the state of a crater on a mare floor follows its size, and whether craters vanished into the floors of the maria while the plains kept theirs, has not been measured on all the craters against a control of the same size. The research question: do the floors of the maria carry fewer craters than the plains, like for like; does the share of melted, smeared and sharp craters on the floors change with size; and how do the craters on the floors of the contact differ from those beyond?

Craters on the maria that the literature describes as partly buried by later basalt have long been used to measure the thickness of the basalt: a crater whose rim still stands above the flow tells how deep the flow is [S25]. The flows are dated by crater counts on top of them [S20]. The viscosity of the mare basalts was measured on returned samples [S19]. In that reading, the state of a crater follows the thickness of the later flows, and the maria and the plains are one kind of surface. What is new here: the floors over the feed domes are set apart from the plains, and all the craters of 3 km and more are classified by one rule against highland craters of their own size.

The large pieces arrived in the time when the maria were active; on an active mare they melted, many of them spreading and merging with the material of the mare until they vanished, others left as rings. The small pieces arrived at the end of the storm of the melt, when most maria had frozen, and stayed sharp. Hence: the floors of the maria carry fewer craters than the plains at every size, within the contact and beyond it; on the floors the share of melted craters rises with size and the share of sharp craters falls; and on the maria of the contact, the last to freeze, the large craters are gone and the few craters that remain are sharp, late arrivals from the Earth.

Type of research: quantitative; densities and states compared across surfaces, sizes and zones. The sample: the 3,478 craters of 3 km and more on the near-side basalt, 324 on the floors of the maria and 3,154 on the plains; for the densities, all the craters of 2 km and more on the same surfaces, ray fields removed; the control: highland craters of the same sizes, latitude within 60°, basalt removed. The tools: the Wang & Wu catalogue [S16] with its depths, the LROC mare map, the feed domes of Paper 5, Appendix A, and code that classifies each crater by the rule of §E.2.

The rule was set on the highland craters alone, size bin by size bin, before any crater on the basalt was classified, so that the lines between the classes could not be tuned to the result. Each crater was then classified, assigned to a mare floor or a plain, and its distance from the centre of the contact (24.1°N, 335.2°E) recorded. The counts are the tables of §E.2.

Vanished. Within the contact the floors of the maria carry a third of the craters of 2–5 km of the plains, an eighth of those of 10–20 km, and none of 20–40 km against 23 per million km2^2; beyond the contact, about half at every size. The change with size. A test for a steady trend across ordered groups (the Cochran–Armitage test, which asks whether a share rises or falls steadily from one size bin to the next rather than wandering at random) gives, on the floors of the maria, a rise of the melted share and a fall of the sharp share with size, each with a probability below one in ten billion of arising by chance; crater by crater, the larger the crater, the shallower it is against the highlands (rank correlation −0.26-0.26; a rank correlation measures whether one quantity tends to fall as the other rises, from −1-1 for a perfect fall to +1+1 for a perfect rise). The contact. Result. The hypothesis is supported: on the floors of the maria craters melted and vanished, merging with the material of the active mare, while the plains kept theirs; the state of the craters that remain follows the size, melted for the large and sharp for the small; and on the maria of the contact, the last to freeze, the large craters are gone and the craters that remain are sharp. Limitations. The depth of a small crater is less well measured than that of a large one; the classes rest on a rule that sets the lines at 95 and 75 of every 100 highland craters; the large craters on the floors are few; and the state alone does not tell whether a crater was melted or covered by a later flow. E.3 The maria: temperature, viscosity and time

Study 5 established the pattern. This section and the next give its causes: here, the floor that receives the crater; in §E.4, the material that makes it. The key property of the floor is its viscosity, its resistance to flow: water has a low viscosity, honey a higher one, cold tar a very high one. A floor of low viscosity closes over a crater; a floor of very high viscosity keeps it.

The viscosity. A mare basalt melt is fluid: the lavas returned by Apollo have a viscosity of the order of 1 Pa s near their liquidus [S19], about 1,200 °C. The liquidus is the temperature above which the melt is wholly liquid; 1 Pa s is about a thousand times the viscosity of water, close to that of glycerine. As the melt cools below the liquidus, crystals grow in it; as they approach half of its volume the melt stops flowing, and its viscosity rises by many orders of magnitude before it freezes at the solidus, about 1,050 °C. The solidus is the temperature below which no liquid is left. A mare passes through three states as it cools — liquid, mush, solid — and the material that lands on it meets each in turn. The mush is the middle state, a crowd of crystals with melt between them, like wet sand or setting plaster: it holds a shape for a time but slowly yields.

What a landing does in each state. On a liquid mare the returning material sinks and mixes, the crater closes behind it, and at most a part of the rim that stood on firm ground remains: the crater is melted. On a mush the crater holds its shape for a while; its walls slump, its floor fills, its rim survives: the crater is smeared. On a frozen floor the crater keeps its full form: it is sharp. The picture is that of a spoon pressed into a pot of cooling porridge: in the hot pot the hollow closes at once, in the thickening pot it slowly fills and its edges sag, and in the cold, set pot it stays. Sinus Iridum is the first case — its southern rim, standing on the liquid mare, is gone, its northern rim, on the firm shore, remains as the Jura Mountains; Plato is the second; Kirch the third.

Time. A mare freezes from above. The maria of the contact were fed the longest and are the deepest. Deepest here is the depth of the body of the mare itself, measured inward, not the height of its floor: the centres of their floors stand slightly raised, Serenitatis by +60+60 m and Imbrium by +43+43 m (Paper 5, §G.3). The material at that depth was hot, so they stayed liquid the longest; and they lay closest to the Moon’s own heat source, a second reason they were the last to freeze. The large craters that landed on them while they were liquid melted and vanished into the mare: not one of 20 km and more remains on their floors. The maria of the limbs, cut off earlier and shallower, froze first, and some of the large craters on them survive as rings.

E.4 The arriving material: temperature, size and distance

The second clock is in the material that lands. In the model about 99% of the craters are made by returning material from the Earth (Part B), and the Earth’s output did not stay the same after release.

The Earth went on throwing. At release the Earth did not stop. Its openings went on throwing material; most of it fell back to the Earth, and part reached the Moon and landed on it. To reach the Moon at a distance rr from the Earth’s centre, the material must leave the Earth’s surface at vmin=2GM⊕(1R⊕−1r),v_{\min}\ =\ \sqrt{2GM_\oplus\left(\frac{1}{R_\oplus}-\frac{1}{r}\right)},\tag{4} 8.8 km s−1^{-1} for a Moon at 17,000 km, 9.9 at 30,000 km and 10.6 at 60,000 km (R⊕=6,371R_\oplus=6{,}371 km). The equation is the balance of energy: a piece leaving the surface must carry enough energy of motion to climb out of the Earth’s gravity from the surface to the Moon’s distance. As the Moon receded, the speed needed rose toward the Earth’s escape speed, the speed at which a piece leaves the Earth altogether. At 17,000 km, close to the distance of release, a piece that the Earth could throw at 8.8 km s−1^{-1} reached the Moon; once the Moon stood at 60,000 km, the same piece fell back, and only those thrown at 10.6 km s−1^{-1} or more arrived.

The large first, the small later. As the Moon receded, a piece needed a higher speed to reach it (equation 4). The Earth’s gravity brought the large pieces back, while the small ones, which the Moon draws easily, still arrived. The large pieces came out first; as the storm of magma on the Earth calmed, smaller pieces, still hot, were thrown. While the Moon was close, large pieces reached it; as it receded, only smaller ones did. The size of the largest crater made on the maria falls with time.

All hot; the floors cooled. The material left a hot Earth with a low viscosity, in the state of the returning material that built the craters of the shell (Part J), and it stayed hot to the end: the large pieces first, then, as the storm of magma calmed, smaller pieces, still hot. What changed was the floor that received them. A small piece landing on a floor that had already frozen makes the sharpest crater: Kirch. Its ratio of 0.166, more than twice that of a highland crater of its size, is what a small piece on a frozen floor leaves.

E.6The rim: worn by heat or flooded

The floors of the maria of the contact carry no large crater. The large craters of the contact are not missing, however: they lie on its plains, and their rims carry a second kind of wear. This section asks what wore them.

The large craters of the contact are on its plains. Of the 41 craters of 40 km and more on the basalt within the contact, not one lies on the floor of a mare: 37 lie on the plains, 22 of them on Procellarum, and 4 on the basalt of Imbrium and Serenitatis outside the cores of their domes. None is sharp, 33 are melted and 8 smeared. Beyond the contact, on the plains, 27 of 85 are sharp and 25 melted (an earlier version gave 27 of 86, counting with them a crater of 64 km on the basalt of Serenitatis that lies 1,507 km from the contact centre, just outside the disc). Most are of middle size: half are of 40–60 km, and four exceed 120 km.

The literature. The craters on the maria were made by bodies from outside; the basalt that came after them flooded the land around them. Flooding raises the ground outside a crater, so the rim that remains exposed stands lower above it, though the crest itself is not lowered [S25, S32]; a crater deeper than the flow keeps its rim as a ring above it. In that reading a low rim on a mare plain measures the thickness of the basalt laid around it. The picture is of a sandcastle with water rising around it: the water hides the lower slopes and the castle looks lower, but its top is untouched.

The model. The plains of the contact lay over the Earth: the heat that reached them came from below, from the Earth beneath them. A crater that landed on them after the middle of the storm, a piece of middle size thrown from the Earth, met a hot plain; the plain wore the rim away where it touched it, on the side that faces it. The crest itself was lowered and blunted. In the picture above, this is not water rising around the castle but warm ground beneath it softening one side, so that the top itself slumps. On Imbrium the heat came from below the crater, from the Moon, through the opening: the crater was melted from its floor upward, and its rim, standing on firmer ground, was kept.

The separating test. The two readings predict different things, and the test is built on the difference. Flooding raises the ground on the side of a crater that faces the basalt, on every plain alike. Heat from below wears the rim on the side that faces the hot plain, where the plain was hot: on the plains of the contact, not on the plains beyond it. The phenomenon. The large craters on the basalt of the contact are melted, none sharp, and not one lies on the floor of a mare (Study 5). The gap in knowledge. A low rim on a plain of basalt is read as the rim exposed above a flood; whether the rims of the plains of the contact were lost in another way has not been tested. The research question: are the rims of the large craters on the plains of the contact lower and more broken than elsewhere, and are they lost on the side that faces the plain only where the plain was hot?

Craters on the maria that the literature describes as partly buried by later basalt are used to measure the thickness of the basalt: the flood raises the ground outside the crater and the exposed rim stands lower above it [S25, S32]. What is new here: the rim crest is traced in 36 directions, each classed by the ground it faces, and the plains of the contact are compared with the plains beyond it, where the same flooding would act.

The plains of the contact were heated from below by the Earth beneath them; the hot plain wore away the rims of the craters that landed on it, where it touched them. Hence: on the plains of the contact the crests are lower than on the plains beyond and in the highland, and the rim is lost more on the side that faces the plain; on the plains beyond the contact, which were not heated from below, no such loss on the side of the plain.

E.7What this shows

The craters on the maria record the Earth after release. Material kept arriving from it — large at first, smaller with time, hot throughout — while the maria cooled beneath it. The large craters that landed on liquid floors vanished or were melted to rings; the middle ones that met a mush were smeared; the small ones that came last, onto frozen floors, stayed sharp; and on the plains of the contact, heated from the Earth beneath, the rims of the large craters were worn on the side of the hot plain. The activity of the Earth waned with time, but it went on after release, and the size and state of the craters on the maria are its record.

The rim

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

the rim of a crater records the forces at the moment of landing. The approach: the rims of 1,457 craters were measured along 72 azimuths at four longitudes, in craters of the same size, maria and South Pole–Aitken removed.

Parts C–E counted the craters and read their state on the maria. Parts F–I now read the shape of a single crater, part by part, and compare it between four regions of the Moon. The principle that guides the reading is the one fixed in §C.1: a crater is the place where returning material landed, and each part of it keeps a record of its own. The floor records the ground the material landed on — the layers beneath it, and what later filled it (Parts G and H). The centre, where the central peak stands, records the energy of the landing (Part I). The rim records the forces that acted on the thrown-up material at the moment of landing (this Part). Read together, the three tell how hard the material came down and on what kind of ground.

The four regions. The craters were taken in four caps of 30° radius, centred on 90°W, 180°, 90°E and 0°. By the standard convention (§A.1) the half of the Moon centred on 90°W is the leading half, the one that faces forward in the Moon’s motion, and the half centred on 90°E is the trailing half; 180° is the centre of the far side and 0° the centre of the near side, the side that faces the Earth. In the model the craters were made while the body still turned with the Earth, and the material that the body itself threw out came down toward its trailing half, which is the more crowded (§C.3); the leading half met that material head-on, as a person walking into slanting rain is struck hardest on the side that faces it. If this is right, the leading half was landed on hardest, and that must show in the shape of its craters.

How the rim is compared. A rim is traced as the highest point along each of 72 directions from the centre (one every 5°), between 0.85 and 1.15 of the radius (§A.2). Two craters can be compared only if they are of the same size, since a large crater has a different shape from a small one; the craters are therefore compared within three size bins, 20–30, 30–50 and 50–100 km, and every height is divided by the diameter DD, so that a value of 9.2×10−3D9.2\times10^{-3}D means 9.2 thousandths of the diameter — 230 m on a crater of 25 km. The rims are not equally well kept: the leading half, facing the direction of motion, is the most worn (§A.2). So that a worn, softened rim does not pass for a weak landing, the irregularity of the rim is read only in the deeper half of each size bin, the better-kept craters.

A difference in landing strength between the two halves changed what was thrown up around the crater, not how deep the crater reached; the depth, as Part G shows, is set by the ground beneath, and the two halves stand on ground of the same kind.

How the model reads the rims. The craters were made while the body still turned with the Earth, and the same rotation that sorted the landings acted on the material thrown up by each landing. On the far side, facing away from the Earth, the centrifugal force of the shared rotation lifted the thrown-up material outward, and the rims stand high. On the leading half the material came down with gravity and with the forward motion of the ground meeting it, and the rim was pressed low. On the trailing half the landing was ordinary, and the ground moving away from the falling material drew the floor a little with it, so that the rim stands a little higher than on the leading half. The near side, facing the Earth, was pressed by the same centrifugal force against its own ground, and its rims were compressed.

The picture is that of a body turning about a common centre with the Earth, the near side innermost and the far side outermost. On the outer, far side the outward push of the rotation acted against the body’s own gravity and let the thrown-up material rise and stay high, as water flung from the outer rim of a spinning wheel; on the inner, near side the same push pointed into the ground and pressed the thrown-up material down. The four rims thus give four forces at the moment of landing: an outward pull on the far side, a head-on meeting on the leading half, an ordinary landing on the trailing half, and a pressing on the near side (§P.1). What we now have: the rim records the forces at landing; it marks the leading half as the one landed on hardest and the far side as the one where the thrown-up material was lifted.

Why it matters. If the basalt outside had filled the crater — over its rim or through a breach — the floor would stand at the level of that basalt, as water stands at one level in two connected vessels, or below it. A floor standing above the basalt outside cannot have been filled from it. It was filled from within, at a level of its own.

How the model reads it. The landing of a large body on ground still liquid or soft — a plain melted by the heat of the Earth, or a mare — raised the level of the floor beneath it, higher than it stands today. The raised floor then sank back toward the level of the ground around it; but sinking back takes time, and the ground was cooling while it did. Where the cooling won, the floor froze while it still stood high: about a third of the large craters. Where the floor had time, it settled at the level of the ground or a little below, as most of the melted craters did. A crater of the highlands landed on solid ground; nothing raised its floor, and it kept the depth of a crater. Two further things enter: the viscosity and the temperature of the ground, which set how fast the floor sank back and when it froze; and gravity. On the near side, material arriving from the Earth was drawn to the surface by the Moon’s gravity while the Earth’s pull acted the other way; the two partly offset each other, the landings were gentle, and many raised floors froze before they had returned. The far side was landed on while the body was attached, under the combined pull of the Moon and the Earth: the landings were harder, and its craters, on the maria and on the highlands alike, are the deeper.

The everyday picture is a lump dropped into a pot of thickening porridge: the surface where it fell first heaves and then slowly settles back, and if the pot cools fast enough the heave is left standing. Viscosity, used here, is a material’s resistance to flow — water has little, cold honey much; the more viscous and the colder the ground, the slower the floor sank back, and the more of the rise was kept.

What the gravity beneath adds. The mass beneath the craters points the same way (§D.3, Study 14). Under a highland crater the base of the crust stands lower than around it, by a median of 0.41 km: the landing broke the ground beneath it into light, loose material, and the crater sits over a deficit of mass. Under the melted craters of the plains there is neither deficit nor excess (+0.08+0.08 km): the ground was soft when they landed, and no lens of broken material was left beneath them. Under the melted craters on the floors of the maria there is an excess: the base of the crust stands +3.0+3.0 km high, against +1.4+1.4 under random points on the same floors, so the craters carry +0.8+0.8 km more than their own floor, in 13 of 16. That excess is broad and has no edge: the steepness of the gravity at the crater’s edge is 1.04 against 1.00 on the floor around. A body of basalt filling the crater from outside would weigh with the sharp edge of the crater; material that sank into an active mare and spread in it does not. The craters on the soft ground of the near side therefore lack, in their gravity as in their floors, the mark of an ordinary crater on solid ground.

What we now have: the floor of a crater lies where its ground let it lie — deep in the crushed layers of the far side, near the surface on the near side, and on the soft basalt at the level of the ground or above it, where a rise was frozen before it could settle.

The phenomenon. Large craters on the near-side basalt are shallow: their floors are filled almost to the level of the basalt (Study 5). The gap in knowledge. Where exactly those floors stand against the basalt outside them — below it, at it, or above it — has not been measured crater by crater, and the answer separates two readings: a floor filled from the basalt outside cannot stand above it. The research question: at what level do the floors of the large melted and smeared craters on the near-side basalt stand, against the basalt immediately outside them, and against the floors of highland craters of the same size?

In the literature the floors of craters on the maria were filled by the same basalt flows that formed the maria [S20, S25]; floors raised above their surroundings are known in the floor-fractured craters, where magma rising beneath the crater spread as a sill and lifted the floor from below [S9]. (A sill is a flat sheet of molten material that forces its way sideways between layers and, as it swells, lifts what lies above it.) THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

Parts F and G read the rim and the floor of a crater; Part H its roughness. This part reads the last element of the crater’s shape, the hill that stands in the middle of many larger craters. The rim records the forces at the moment of landing, the floor the ground beneath; the peak, as this part shows, records how much energy the landing carried. Together with Parts F–H it completes one study of crater shape at four longitudes, Study 6, set out in full at the end of this part.

What a central peak is. A small fresh crater is a simple bowl: its walls run down to a rounded bottom. Above a certain size the form changes: the floor becomes flat, and a mound or a cluster of hills rises from its centre. This mound is the central peak. On the Moon the change appears in craters of about 15–20 km across [S27], and the peak is read in the literature as the rebound of the floor after the landing [S28]. A peak therefore marks a landing that delivered more energy than the ground could absorb by being pushed aside; the surplus came back up in the centre.

The series’ reading of the peak. In the model the returning material was deep material of the parent, ejected through its openings (Part J), and it reached the surface still hot. It was of low viscosity — it flowed easily, in the way hot syrup flows and cold syrup does not — and it had not had time to cool in a flight of minutes (§K.1). It landed as a hot, mobile mass. Such a mass does not simply dig a hole; it drives the ground beneath it down and outward, and the ground then springs back in the centre. Because the material in the centre was still soft and hot, the rebound was able to rise, and it then froze where it stood. The peak is that frozen rebound.

J.1Energy available at the opening

The question this section answers is how much energy each kilogram must receive at the opening to leave at a given speed, and where that energy can come from.

For unit mass ejected at velocity uu, the kinetic energy relative to the opening is:

qkin=u22.q_{\mathrm kin}=\frac{u^2}{2}.\tag{5}

In words: the energy of motion of one kilogram is half the square of its speed. Because the speed is squared, doubling the speed takes four times the energy. Velocities of 2, 3, 5 and 8 km/s require respectively 2, 4.5, 12.5 and 32 MJ/kg. A megajoule per kilogram is a familiar scale: 12.5 MJ/kg, the cost at 5 km/s, is a sizeable fraction of the chemical energy stored in a kilogram of fuel. The energy must therefore be delivered at the opening by a definite source, and the account must show it.

In an incompressible material, a local pressure difference can supply at most Δp/ρ\Delta p/\rho per unit mass in ideal conversion. This is the familiar rule of a water jet: a pressure difference Δp\Delta p across a nozzle can give each kilogram at most Δp/ρ\Delta p/\rho of energy, and in practice less. In an expanding material the enthalpy drop or the pressure work along a thermodynamic path must be computed, together with heat, gravity and losses. Here the material is not a still liquid: gas dissolved in it comes out of solution and expands as the pressure falls, and that expansion does work on the melt around it.

The lift cost of 139.78 GPa in Paper 3 is not an additional reservoir at the opening after the material has already been lifted. Nor is the radial pressure remainder of 2.074 GPa in a particular example of Paper 3 an ejection budget that has been allocated and solved. The test requires sufficient energy for equation (5) to remain after the energy costs of lift, flow and load support have been accounted for. Ideal conversion of pressure to velocity is not a guaranteed lower bound on the velocity.

Where the ejection energy comes from. The pressure that throws the material out is the pressure from the Earth. The spin and the sorting — one mechanism (Paper 3) — create it in the Earth’s interior; it passes through the pear into the body, reaches the openings, which are its outlets, and sprays the material out of them, as the pressure in a hose throws the water out of the nozzle. The volatiles assist: water and other gases dissolved in the deep melt under pressure come out of solution as the melt nears the opening, expand, and add their push to the throw. A shaken bottle of soda shows this part on a small scale: the gas leaving the liquid throws the liquid out. The full energy account — how much of the pressure reaches the opening after the costs of lift, flow and load support, and how much the volatiles add — is a requirement of the model: together they must supply u2/2u^2/2 per kilogram for the velocities that the craters require.

J.2Velocity and shock pressure

The question this section answers is what pressure the returning material produces in the ground when it lands at a given speed. The pressure matters because a landing records itself in the minerals of the ground only above certain pressures (shock metamorphism); a crater made by a soft arrival and one made by a shock look different in the laboratory.

What a shock is, and what the Hugoniot is. When a body lands faster than sound travels in the ground, the ground ahead of the contact cannot move out of the way in time. A sharp front forms, a shock, across which pressure, density and velocity jump. Two speeds describe it: the speed of the front itself, UsU_s, and the speed upu_p that the material behind the front acquires (the particle velocity). For most solids, measured in the laboratory, the two are related by a straight line, Us=C0+supU_s=C_0+s u_p; this line is the material’s Hugoniot. C0C_0 is close to the speed of sound in the material, the speed of the front for a very weak shock, and ss tells how much faster the front runs as the shock grows stronger.

Impedance matching. At the contact, the two sides must have the same pressure and the same velocity, otherwise they would separate or pass through each other. When the arriving body and the ground are of the same material, they are equally stiff, and the jump in velocity is shared equally: the arriving body slows by half its speed, and the ground beneath it is set moving at half that speed. Hence the rule below. For two different materials — a hot melt on a cold crust, for example — the stiffer side takes the smaller share, and the split must be solved from both Hugoniots; this is what impedance matching means.

In a planar head-on landing of identical materials, the particle velocity behind the shock front is up=vn/2u_p=v_n/2, where vnv_n is the normal landing velocity. Using the basalt relation Us=C0+supU_s=C_0+s u_p:

P−P0=ρ0Usup=ρ0(C0+svn2)vn2.P-P_0=\rho_0U_su_p =\rho_0\left(C_0+s\frac{v_n}{2}\right)\frac{v_n}{2}.\tag{6}

In words: the pressure jump across the shock equals the density of the undisturbed ground, ρ0\rho_0, times the speed of the front, times the speed given to the material. It is the balance of momentum across the front: in each second the front sweeps up a mass ρ0Us\rho_0U_s per square metre and sets it moving at upu_p, and the force per square metre needed to do that is the pressure. P0P_0 is the pressure before the shock and is small. Because UsU_s itself grows with the landing speed, the pressure grows somewhat faster than in proportion to the speed.

Can a liquid make a shock? A specialist may object that a molten returning body is too soft to shock the ground. It is not: a liquid resists compression almost as strongly as a solid, and a compression wave runs through it in the same way; what a liquid lacks is strength against shear, not stiffness against squeezing. A liquid returning body can transmit a compression wave; full solidification is not a condition for the creation of shock itself. However, the size, density, dispersal and heat of the returning body affect the pressure, the pulse duration and the excavation. Thresholds for mineral changes depend on the type of material and its state [S4]. J.3 The vapour atmosphere is part of the test

The question this section answers is how much speed the returning material loses on its way through the vapour that surrounded the parent in the scenario. This matters because the landing pressure of §J.2 depends on the landing speed, and every kilometre per second lost in the air is lost from the crater.

The flight tables assume a vacuum. In the vapour environment of the scenario, the column of material through which the returning body passes must be examined. Define a ballistic coefficient B=mp/(CDAp)B=m_p/(C_D A_p) and an atmospheric column along the path Σa\Sigma_a. In a drag-only model, with constant mass and area and no other forces:

voutvin=exp(−Σa2B).\frac{v_{\mathrm out}}{v_{\mathrm in}} =\exp\left(-\frac{\Sigma_a}{2B}\right).\tag{7}

The symbols: mpm_p is the mass of the returning body, ApA_p its cross-section facing the flow, and CDC_D its drag coefficient, a number near one for a blunt body. The ballistic coefficient BB is therefore mass per unit of frontal area: it says how much body stands behind each square metre that pushes the gas aside. A cannonball has a large BB and a feather a small one. Σa\Sigma_a is the mass of gas per square metre that lies along the path — the weight of the air column the body must push through, expressed as mass. Equation (7) says that the body keeps its speed as long as the gas it meets is small compared with its own mass per area; when the two become comparable, the speed falls off exponentially. A heavy, compact body passes almost untouched; a light or finely broken one is stopped.

For an example only, a spherical body one kilometre in diameter, density 2,700 and CD=1C_D=1, gives B=1.8×106B=1.8\times10^6 kg/m². Two nearly vertical crossings of a hydrostatic column at an adopted base pressure of 100 bar and geff=8.25g_{\mathrm eff}=8.25 give Σa/B≃1.35\Sigma_a/B\simeq1.35, and a velocity attenuation factor of about 0.51 in the drag model. 100 bar is not a reconstructed pressure of the ancient atmosphere.

The two crossings are the way up and the way down: the material leaves the opening through the vapour and comes back through it. In a column at rest, the base pressure equals the weight of the gas above each square metre, so Σa\Sigma_a for one crossing is the base pressure divided by the effective gravity. With the two crossings together the column is 1.35 times the body’s BB, and the body arrives with about half the speed it would have had in a vacuum. A body thrown at 5 km/s would land at about 2.5 km/s; by Figure 7 this still gives a contact pressure above 12.96 GPa, the value at 2 km/s.

The example demonstrates that drag is not a correction that can always be neglected. For a real trajectory, gravity and drag must be solved together; heating, break-up and the motion of the gas also change BB. Volatiles may assist ejection and at the same time make rapid passage through the atmosphere harder. A single parameter domain must satisfy both the ejection and atmospheric-passage requirements. Put plainly: the same gases that throw the material out (§J.1) also form the vapour it must cross, so more gas helps the throw and hinders the flight. The model requires a range of gas content, body size and vapour pressure in which both are met at once.

J.4 A landing lump, a cloud of material and a preserving surface

The question this section answers is in what state the material must arrive, and what kind of surface must receive it, for a crater to be made and kept.

The same mass arriving as a dense lump or as a dispersed cloud does not create the same shock pulse. A lump delivers its momentum to a small area in a short time, and the pressure is high; a cloud spreads the same momentum over a wide area and a longer time, as a bucket of water poured from a height strikes differently from a single block of ice of the same weight. A tidal deformation time alone does not determine whether a lump breaks up: viscosity, strength, surface tension and sometimes self-gravity must be included. Viscosity resists the stretching of a soft lump; strength holds a solid one together; surface tension holds a drop together, as it holds a drop leaving a syringe; and in a large body its own gravity does the same. There is no basis for assuming that every long-range lump arrives intact, or that every molten returning body loses its cratering capacity.

The receiving surface must be thick and strong enough relative to the size of the crater (Figure 8). A freezing layer one metre thick does not prove preservation of a crater kilometres across. Paper 3 treats the shell: stiff and cold at the surface, mobile at its base. The test here adds landing heating and the need for the record to form before burial or relaxation. Relaxation is the slow flattening of a crater whose ground is too soft to hold its shape, as a pit pressed into thick honey closes over time; a crater survives only if the ground beneath it is stiff enough, to a depth comparable with its size, for long enough.

Figure 8 shows what such a surface looks like today. The far-side highlands around Tsiolkovskiy are covered with craters of every size, each overlapping the next, on the thickest crust of the Moon. In the model this is the oldest record, made on the shell while it still lay on the parent and carried up with it.

J.5 Gravitational binding and a velocity ceiling

The question this section answers is how fast material may be thrown and still return. Material thrown too fast leaves the Earth for good and makes no crater on the shell; the ceiling therefore bounds the speeds the craters can record.

In the central model, ℰ<0\mathcal E<0 gives for radial ejection:

u<uesc=2Kr0−ω2r02≃10.748kms−1.u<u_{\mathrm esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2K}{r_0}-\omega^2r_0^2} \simeq10.748\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}.\tag{8}

Energy conservation in a central field. In a field that pulls toward one centre and does not change in time, the total energy of a thrown body — energy of motion plus energy of position — stays constant along its flight. ℰ\mathcal E is that total, per kilogram (equation 10 of §K.1). A body with negative total energy cannot climb out of the field: it rises, slows, and falls back. A body with positive total energy escapes. K=GM0K=GM_0 is the strength of the terrestrial field, r0r_0 the radius from which the material is thrown (6,721 km in the example), and ω\omega the rate of the parent’s rotation. The term 2K/r02K/r_0 is the square of the escape speed from a body at rest. The second term, ω2r02\omega^2r_0^2, is the share already paid by the rotation: material on the equator of a turning surface moves sideways with that surface at ωr0\omega r_0 before it is thrown, and that sideways motion is part of its energy. The radial throw has to supply only the rest. Below 10.748 km/s the material is bound and comes back; above it, it leaves.

The same sideways speed explains why the material lands to the west (§K.1). Thrown straight up, it keeps the sideways speed it had on the surface; but as it rises, that speed carries it through a smaller angle per second than the surface turns, because the same speed at a larger radius covers a smaller angle — a child who walks outward on a turning roundabout falls behind the boards beneath her. When the material comes down, the ground beneath it has moved on eastward, and the landing lies west of the opening: about 1,032 km west for a throw at 5 km/s, after 28 minutes in the air. Energy conservation also gives the return speed: with no drag and a field that has not changed, material coming back to the radius it left has the same total energy and the same sideways speed, so its radial speed is again uu, and relative to the turning surface it lands at the speed it was thrown.

Remaining gravitationally bound to the Earth does not guarantee a landing on the shell. Material can land on another region, be swallowed by the parent or be altered on encountering the developing body. For a bound component in a central field, a general and broader bound on the relative velocity is:

vrel<2Kr+|𝐯target|.v_{\mathrm rel}<\sqrt{\frac{2K}{r}}+|\mathbf v_{\mathrm target}|.\tag{9}

In words: a body still bound to the field can at a radius rr move no faster than the escape speed at that radius, 2K/r\sqrt{2K/r}; the surface it lands on moves at 𝐯target\mathbf v_{\mathrm target}; and the largest possible closing speed is the sum of the two, as when two cars meet head-on. The bound is not necessarily attainable in a particular ejection geometry. The old shock ceilings of 113 or 97 GPa are not ceilings for all material originating in the Earth–Moon system. They depended on the family of paths and the material chosen, and in part lay beyond the range of the shock approximation. A sample above such a number therefore does not by itself set aside every terrestrial source.

The Hill radius is a dynamical scale, not a boundary at which the Moon’s gravity vanishes. It is the distance from a small body within which its own pull on a nearby particle outweighs the tidal pull of the larger body it orbits; inside it, the small body holds its own material, and outside it the larger body takes over. In the late reference of 17,631 km, with the full lunar mass and a body density of 2,600, one obtains rH≃2,822r_H\simeq2{,}822 km and a body radius of about 1,890 km. A fall from rest from rHr_H in the lunar gravity alone gives about 1.31 km/s, but this is a limited computational experiment and not a ceiling for a returning body already in motion. This comparison concerns a later state and cannot be combined with the early field used in the tables as though both described the same state.

What we now have: a throw at a few kilometres per second needs u2/2u^2/2 per kilogram at the opening, supplied by the pressure from the Earth with the volatiles assisting; the material returns at the speed it was thrown, lands west of its opening, and, landing on basalt, produces a contact pressure of tens of gigapascals; the vapour can halve that speed, so the size of the returning body and the gas content enter the test together; and every throw below about 10.748 km/s stays bound. K.1 Return to the shell at the same altitude

The question here is simple: material is thrown straight up from the equator of the turning parent and comes back down to the same height. How fast does it hit the ground, and where?

Denote ω=2π/T\omega=2\pi/T, the angular speed of the surface, where TT is the rotation period (5.5 hours in the example; see the environment table). Material ejected radially from the surface, straight up, also has an initial tangential velocity ωr0\omega r_0: the sideways speed it shared with the turning ground at the moment it left, r0r_0 being the radius of the ejection point. Two quantities stay fixed during a flight in a field that does not change and without air drag. The second is the angular momentum per unit mass, ℓ\ell: the tangential speed times the distance from the centre, which measures how much “turning” the material carries with it. K.4Where the body’s own ejecta land

Sections K.1–K.3 dealt with material thrown by the parent. This section turns to material thrown by the body itself, and shows how it sets the pattern of craters by longitude that Parts C and F–I measure.

The flights of Parts J–K start on the parent. Once the body had risen on the connection, its own openings — its maria — threw material as well, most of it from near its equator. That ejection was neither strong nor far. Material thrown from the body at its own escape speed or less — about 2.0 km/s at 1,300 kg/m³ and 2.3 km/s at release — falls back on it, and the turning of the attached body decides where it falls and how hard. (The escape speed is the speed at which material leaves a body for good; below it, the body’s gravity brings the material back. These speeds are a few times smaller than the 5–8 km/s flights of §K.1, so the body’s ejecta stayed close to home.)

The series follows the standard convention: the leading half of the Moon, the half that faces its orbital motion, is centred on 90°W; the trailing half on 90°E. The convention rests on synchronous turning: the Moon turns once about its axis in the time it takes to go once around the Earth, so the same face always points to the Earth (the near side, centred on 0°), the opposite face always points away (the far side, 180°), one limb always faces forward along the path, and the other always faces backward. The attached body turned in the same way, carried round with the Earth, and had the same four faces.

The body turned with the Earth. Material thrown up from the trailing half comes down while that half moves away from it: it catches up with the ground from behind, and lands gently. Material thrown up from the leading half comes down while that half moves on into it: the ground runs into the falling material, and the landing is hard. The difference lies in the landing speed, which is always the speed of the material relative to the ground it meets. When the ground moves away from the falling material, the ground’s motion is taken from that speed; when the ground moves toward it, the two add. A ball caught while the hand draws back lands softly in it; the same ball caught while the hand pushes forward strikes it. Its landing is the hardest of the three, for the reason given below.

Two predictions follow. The trailing half collects its own ejecta and those that drift from the far side, and so holds the most craters (§C.3). The leading half meets its ejecta head-on, and so is the half landed on hardest: its craters should carry the marks of a hard landing (§F.1–L.4). The far side’s landings were harder still, the hardest of all, made while the body was attached, under the combined pull of the Moon and the Earth (§G.2, §I.1). The near side stands apart from both: it lies by the large maria, and its craters were shaped from below by them as well (§G.1).

The first prediction is tested by a count in §C.3. In plain words, the test asks whether the excess at 90°E could be an accident of counting: a crater count scatters by about its square root, so two regions can differ by chance alone. The ratio of trailing to leading density there is 1.79, and the range within which it lies with 95% confidence, 1.56–2.05, stays well above one, the value of no difference; resampling whole cells of the map rather than single craters, to allow for craters that cluster, gives the same answer. The second prediction is tested by the shape of the craters in Parts F–I. What we now have is the geometry behind both: soft landings and crowding behind, hard landings in front.

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

Parts C to K read the craters by their number, their place and their shape. This Part reads what they are made of. The shape of a crater records how the returning material landed; its chemistry records where that material came from. Four questions are asked in turn. Did the material around each mare come from that mare (§L.1–L.3)? Is the inside of a crater one material, or layers dug from below (§L.4)? Did a body from outside leave its mass in the craters, as the impact reading of the literature requires (§L.5)? And what was the material that kept arriving from the Earth after release made of, and where did it fall (§L.6–L.7)? The chemistry of the floors of the large craters on the basalt is read with the floors themselves, in §G.3: the floors that froze high stood up while they were still rich in volatiles, which carried the light material up with them, and they are poorer in iron than the floors that sank back to the level of the ground and settled with the heavier material.

L.1Mare material in rings around the mare

This section carries out the source test stated in §C.5. The model holds that the maria are the openings from which the returning material was thrown (§C.1): most of the craters near a mare were made by that mare. If so, what a mare threw should be found on the ground around it, most near it and less farther out, as the ash of a chimney lies thickest next to the chimney. The literature holds instead that the craters were dug by bodies from outside, which have nothing to do with the maria; in that reading the ground near a mare is enriched only where later craters mixed a little of the mare sideways into it, a few kilometres to some tens of kilometres [S26]. The two readings therefore part on the reach: a mare that threw its own material reaches far; sideways mixing does not.

The ground is read in rings: bands at a set distance from the mare edge, 0–25 km, 25–50 km and outward to 300–600 km. In each ring, craters of 5–60 km were chosen, and the ground around each crater was read at 3–4 crater radii from its centre. That distance lies beyond the crater’s own ejecta blanket, the material it threw out, which reaches to about 2 radii; so what is read is the ground the crater landed on. Every pixel of mapped basalt is removed, so that what is measured is the land around the mare and not lava that spread over it. The controls are craters of the same sizes more than 600 km from any mare: their ground is the level of the highland where no mare reached.

The main result: mare material in rings around the mare. The point of the test is the ground around each mare, read ring by ring outward. If the mare threw its material around it, the heavy elements of the mare — iron and titanium — must be found in a ring around it, and less of them the farther the ring. They are. Around the six maria, pooled, the iron of the ground (3–4 crater radii, basalt removed) falls from 6.9 wt% at 0–25 km to 3.1 at 100–150 km, and then holds at 3.3–3.4 out to 600 km, 1.1 wt% above the controls; the titanium falls from 0.90 to 0.51, against 0.46 in the controls (Table, Figure 11). In plain numbers: the ground next to a mare holds three times the iron of the far highland (6.9 against 2.3), and even 300–600 km away it still holds about one and a half times as much. Around Smythii and Moscoviense the iron falls by half within 50–100 km; around Crisium it stays near 4 wt% out to 300 km. The two exceptions are in the iron alone and far from the mare: beyond 150 km the ground southwest of Nectaris rises again, toward the iron-rich southern highlands of the near side, while its titanium stays flat; Moscoviense, on the far side, is a small mare whose ring reaches the far-side level, 2 wt%, within 100 km. In both exceptions the titanium, or the iron near the mare, still falls as expected; what departs is the far ring, where the ground beyond the reach of the mare has its own level. The plains are read apart. In the model a plain — Procellarum, Tranquillitatis, Fecunditatis — is not an opening over a feed dome but crust melted in place (Paper 5, §C.2); it had no throat, and it threw nothing and spilled nothing. It is the maria — apart from the maria of the contact — that spilled their melt over their margins, in addition to throwing out large material. Around Procellarum, the largest plain, the iron falls from 10.9 to 2.4 wt%, the level of the controls, at 300–600 km — a short reach, not the reach of a throw; Tranquillitatis and Fecunditatis lie among other basalt and stay high.

Why the iron is highest at the rim. A mare sealed — its surface froze and kept the composition it then had — at the concentration it had reached at the end, about 17 wt% FeO. The ground around it was sealed earlier, while the mare was poorer. In the first stage the mixed material of the mare flowed out over long distances: light material and volatiles tend to float, and being more fluid they ran far. As the light material left, the mare concentrated in iron and its viscosity — its resistance to flow, the property that makes honey slower than water — rose, and what it threw ran shorter distances. A sauce reducing on the stove behaves in the same way: while it is thin it splashes far from the pot; as it thickens, what leaves it drops beside the pot. The ground far from the mare was therefore sealed low in iron, and the ground near it high. The same history dates the craters and the maria: near a mare, old craters carry less iron than new ones — though most new craters are small, made by volatiles, and carry the lighter material — and a mare rich in iron was concentrated longer and sealed late, a mare poor in iron sealed early, without a strong concentrating mechanism.

The literature explains the halo as mare material mixed sideways into the highland by later cratering; the model says the mare threw its own material onto the ground around it, and that material made the craters there (§C.1, §C.5). L.2The crater and its ground

§L.1 found that the ground near a mare carries the mare’s iron, and that the crater sitting in that ground is a little poorer than it. This section states what was measured, what the measurement shows, and how the model reads it.

What is measured. The Kaguya Multiband Imager reads the light reflected by the uppermost surface layer, and from it gives a concentration: the weight of FeO in every 100 grams of that layer (wt%). It gives neither an amount of material nor the depth to which the material reaches. The crater interior and the ground at 3–4 crater radii are read on the same maps, at the same resolution and in the same unit, crater by crater, each crater against its own ground. The comparison is therefore like-for-like: two concentrations of the same surface layer, one inside the crater and one on the ground around it.

What it shows. Around the six maria the crater interior is a little poorer in iron than its own ground at every distance band: −0.26-0.26, −0.10-0.10, −0.05-0.05 and −0.13-0.13 wt% FeO at 0–50, 50–150, 150–300 and 300–600 km (§C.5). Within 50 km the 95% interval, −0.34-0.34 to −0.19-0.19, lies wholly below zero. In the far controls, more than 600 km from any mare, the difference is −0.01-0.01. The difference is small — a few tenths of a percent against the 5.96 wt% of the ground within 50 km — and its sign is the point: near a mare the crater is a little poorer than its ground, far from any mare it is not. It does not change the main result of §L.1, the fall-off of the mare’s signature with distance: inside the craters the iron follows the ground ring by ring, from 5.88 to 3.30 wt%, and the small step between crater and ground rides on that fall-off without altering it.

How the model reads it. We propose that the difference belongs to the same history as the fall-off (§L.1, Why the iron is highest at the rim). The ground around a mare was sealed step by step as the mare concentrated in iron, so what a surface holds depends on when it was sealed. Near a mare, old craters carry less iron than new ones, though most new craters are small, made by volatiles, and carry the lighter material. The floor of a crater is the ground the material landed on — its layers — and the crater interior records the material and the layer of its own time. This reading is to be tested by dividing the craters near each mare into age classes and comparing the difference from the ground class by class: the older craters should carry the larger deficit.

L.3Thorium and the returning material

Thorium and the returning material. Thorium is the third element of the source test. It does not fit into the crystals of the common minerals, so it stays in the last liquid of a cooling melt, and in the series it marks the contact with the Earth, where the Moon last touched it (Paper 5, §J.3). Around the maria the thorium of the ejecta also falls with distance, from 2.16 ppm within 50 km to 1.39 at 300–600 km, against 1.02 in the controls (Study 2). But thorium is a regional value: the Lunar Prospector map is smoothed over about 60 km, larger than every crater measured, so it shows the halo of the mare but does not separate a crater from its surroundings. A crater of 20 km on this map is like a face in a photograph taken badly out of focus: the colour of the scene shows, but not the face. The share of the returning material itself is not identifiable from these maps. The next step is set in Paper 12 (research topics): a measure that sees the returning material — melt and glass in the crater, the crater’s interior read zone by zone, or a higher-resolution thorium — in place of the ground.

L.4The inside of the crater

§L.2 compared the crater with the ground around it. This section looks inside the crater itself. The two readings predict different things for the inside. If the crater is a hole through layers, its centre, its walls and its rim should differ from one another and from the surface around it. L.5The foreign mass that is not there

The sections so far asked where the material of the craters came from on the Moon. This section asks the question that separates the model most sharply from the literature: did anything come from outside?

If the craters of the Moon were made by external impacting bodies, the bodies brought their own mass, and part of it stayed. The literature reads that part in the highly siderophile elements — iridium, osmium, platinum, ruthenium — which the Moon’s own silicate almost lacks, since its differentiation sent them to the core, so that a small addition stands out at once. Siderophile means “iron-loving”: when a body melts and its metal sinks to form a core (its differentiation), these elements dissolve in the metal and go down with it, leaving the silicate shell almost without them. A body from outside of chondritic composition — the stony meteorites that never melted, with their metal still mixed through them — carries them at a far higher level. A little of such a body mixed into the crust is therefore seen at once, as a few drops of ink are seen in a glass of clear water. If the craters are of returned terrestrial material, as this paper proposes, there is no foreign mass to find: the returning material is of the same kind as the shell it landed on. The question is therefore a measurement: how much foreign mass the crater record requires, where it should lie, and what is found.

Behind the scenes. The test was first framed as a budget for the whole Moon, and on that scale a foreign mass is a small fraction of the lunar mass and says little. A mass that lands stays near the surface, so the comparison was moved to where it should lie, the upper crust. And since the presence of the siderophile elements alone does not tell where they came from — the Earth’s mantle carries them in chondritic proportions too — the isotopes of those elements were added as the deciding measure.

The isotopes are the fingerprint. Atoms of one element can differ in the number of neutrons in their nucleus; these forms are its isotopes. They behave alike in chemistry, so melting and mixing do not change their proportions, but the proportions differ slightly between the bodies that formed in different parts of the young solar system. Two samples can therefore hold the same amount of ruthenium and still tell, by its isotopes, whether it formed with the Earth or with a meteorite from elsewhere, as two people may say the same words in different accents.

L.6The terrestrial material after release

The craters read so far were made while the Moon was still attached to the Earth, or by its own openings. This section turns to the craters made after release, and asks what the material that made them was.

At release the Moon was round, with a rigid outer shell; it had flattened and rounded again while it was still attached (Paper 7). The pear — the bulge of the Earth whose tip the Moon had been — was gathered back into the Earth by the sorting, slowly, over tens to hundreds of millions of years after release, leaving wrinkles on its surface (Paper 9). The Earth did not fall quiet: its openings went on throwing material, most of which fell back to it, and part of which reached the Moon and landed on it (§E.4).

The prediction. A mare is a cut through the layers of the tip of the pear. The middle of the cut holds the iron, and with it the thorium that came up from deep in the Earth; the spike that ran into the body may hold more of the light material. The material that the Earth went on throwing after release was another thing: the part of the Earth’s outflow that could escape it. The heavy material sank back down the pear toward the Earth, and the light rose (Paper 3, §C.10); what left the Earth and reached the Moon was the lighter part. Escaping the Earth was not easy: to reach the Moon the material had to leave the Earth’s surface at 8.8 km/s or more, rising toward the Earth’s escape speed as the Moon receded (§E.4). The craters made on the maria after release (Part E) were built of it. The model therefore predicts that the material of these craters — the ejecta, read apart from the mare floor they landed on — is poorer in iron than the mare, the richest in iron on the whole Moon.

What it shows. Material from the Earth landing on the Moon after release shows that the Earth stayed active after the Moon left it; the activity waned with time — the pieces that reached the Moon grew smaller, still hot (§E.4) — but it went on.

The phenomenon. The craters on the near-side basalt were made after release by material from the Earth — some while the floor was still hot (melted and raised), the sharp ones after it had frozen (Studies 5 and 9). The gap in knowledge. What that material is made of, against the mare it landed on, has not been measured crater by crater. The research question: is the material thrown out of the craters on the basalt poorer or richer in iron and thorium than the basalt around them, on the floors of the maria and on the plains?

Iron and titanium are read from the Kaguya Multiband Imager [S10], thorium from Lunar Prospector [S11]. In the literature the ejecta of a crater on a mare are the target it dug: the basalt, and the highland material beneath it where the crater reached through the basalt [S26]. Soils at the Apollo 12 site tied to the rays of Copernicus are rich in thorium and poorer in iron than the local basalt [S30]. The literature reads the rays of a crater as bright streaks of its ejecta that run outward from it, sometimes for hundreds of kilometres; in the model a ray is a record within the ground beneath the surface, formed only where material landed on a floor that had already cooled (Further work, item 7). What is new here: every crater on the near-side basalt is read against the basalt just around it, the floors of the maria and the plains apart, by class and size, with a control for how deep the crater dug.

The material from the Earth was its lighter part; the ejecta of the craters on the basalt are therefore poorer in iron than the basalt around them, on the floors of the maria and on the plains alike.

Type of research: quantitative, crater by crater, with a control of excavation depth. The sample: the 3,478 craters of 3 km and more on the near-side basalt (Study 5), 324 on the floors of the maria and 3,154 on the plains; thorium for the 62 of 60 km and more. The tools: the Kaguya iron and titanium maps at 128 pixels per degree, the Lunar Prospector thorium map, the LROC mare map; the ejecta read at 1.2–2 radii, the mare around at 3–4 radii, mare pixels only; and a published map of basalt thickness to flag the craters that dug through it. The control of depth matters because of the literature’s reading: a crater that cut through the basalt throws up the poorer highland beneath it, and its ejecta would be poor in iron for that reason alone. The craters that stayed within the basalt are the clean test.

The craters and their classes were fixed before the chemistry was read. Every crater was kept; craters whose surroundings carry too little mapped mare were set apart and reported.

All craters. The ejecta are poorer in iron than the mare around them in every class and size: by 0.3–0.9 wt% FeO for the craters of 5–10 km, and by 1–6 wt% for the larger ones. Maria and plains. On the floors of the maria the ejecta are poorer than the basalt around them by a median of 0.20 wt% (two of three craters poorer), from −0.11-0.11 at 3–5 km to −3.8-3.8 at 20 km and more; on the plains by 0.36 (71% poorer), from −0.13-0.13 to −2.9-2.9. Craters inside the basalt. Among the craters whose ejecta lie wholly on mapped mare and that did not dig through the basalt, the difference is small: −0.11-0.11 wt% for the sharp craters (97), +0.02+0.02 for the smeared (6), −0.03-0.03 for the melted (36), within the calibration scatter of the maps (0.81 wt%). The sharp, smeared and melted classes are those of §D.2, set by depth against highland craters of the same size: a melted crater, shallower than 95% of them, landed on the active ground and melted into it; a smeared one is shallower than 75% of them; a sharp one was made after the ground froze. Thorium. For the craters of 60 km and more the ejecta carry the thorium of the mare around them, within about 0.1 ppm. Samples. The Apollo 12 soils tied to Copernicus carry 7.6–9.4 ppm thorium against 0.83 in the local basalt, and 14.0–15.7 wt% FeO against 20.5.

Result. The hypothesis is supported in the full sample: the material of the craters on the basalt is poorer in iron than the basalt, on the floors of the maria and on the plains alike, and the sample tied to a crater made on the mare after it froze is poorer in iron and richer in thorium than the local basalt. Limitations. The literature reads the poorer ejecta as highland material dug from under the basalt; among the craters that stayed within the basalt the difference falls within the scatter of the maps, so the two readings are not separated by these maps; the thorium map is too coarse for the small craters. Directions for further research. The returned samples of the ejecta of single craters made on the maria, read for iron, thorium and the ruthenium isotopes together.

PART M

Dating

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

a crater’s age is read in the literature from crater density and a constant flux. The approach: the populations are ordered by when they landed, without converting counts to years.

The Parts before this one asked where the returning material came from, where it landed, how hard, and what it left. This Part asks when, and it answers in a particular way. In the literature the age of a surface is read from the number of craters on it. The crater density is the number of craters per unit area: a surface exposed for longer has gathered more craters, as a car left longer under a tree gathers more fallen leaves. To turn that count into years, the literature assumes a known and steady rain of bodies from outside, a constant flux, calibrated on the samples brought back by the missions. This paper replaces that rain. In the model almost all the craters were made by returning material, whose rate rose and fell with the activity of the openings and of the Earth; a count of craters can therefore still rank two surfaces, the more crowded being the longer exposed, but no fixed rate of arrival can turn the count into years.

What the paper keeps is an order. The crater populations of the earlier Parts can be placed one after another by what lies on what and by the state the craters are in, without a number of years attached to any of them:

  • first, the craters made by the far-side sources that worked early and were then buried with them under the layers of the far side (§D.1);

  • then, the craters made while the body was attached, which rose with the shell and are the record of the highlands and of the far side (Parts B, J–K);

  • then, the craters that landed on the floors of the maria while those floors were still active, and melted into them (§D.2, Part E);

  • last, the sharp craters on the frozen floors and the small fresh craters on the land, made after release (§D.2, §L.7), together with the captured population named in §M.1 below.

For scale only, the construction as a whole took about 600 Myr and ended with release at 3.873 Ga (Paper 3); the order above falls within that span and after it, and is read without dividing it further.

M.1Captured ejecta after release

This section names the last population in that order, the one made after the Moon had left the Earth.

The material treated so far returned to a shell that still lay on the parent, and rose with it; its record is the far side. A second population follows. At release the Moon was cut off from the Earth’s pressure, and with it the internal pressure of the Moon and the ejections of its maria stopped; the near-side maria were then hot and of low viscosity, and froze slowly, from the outside inward. On the Earth’s side the ejections continued, smaller, while the Moon was already a separate body receding from a few Earth radii. Most of what left fell back to the Earth; a fraction, leaving near the ceiling of eq. (8) and in the Moon’s direction, was captured by the Moon’s gravity and landed on it. The ceiling of eq. (8) is the largest ejection speed, about 10.748 km s−1^{-1}, at which material still stays bound to the Earth (§J.5); material leaving just below it climbs far from the Earth before it turns back, and if the Moon lies on its way, the Moon’s own pull can take it. This is not returning material in the sense of Parts J–K — it never came back to the shell — and it is named separately here: captured ejecta. Its target was the surface exposed last, the contact disc and its maria (Paper 11, §B.8); the few and recent craters on the maria are read here as this population, the captured ejecta.

Two things distinguish it from an asteroid. Its velocity at the Moon is bounded above by what is left of the Earth’s escape speed after the climb — of the order of a few km s−1^{-1} at a lunar distance of a few Earth radii, plus the Moon’s own escape speed of 2.4 km s−1^{-1} — against 15–20 km s−1^{-1} for an asteroid; and its composition is the Earth’s — the light part of the outflow, poorer in iron than the maria (§L.6), with no foreign elements. The second speed enters because a body falling onto the Moon from far away is accelerated by the Moon’s own gravity on the way in, and arrives at least at the Moon’s escape speed. The predictions follow: on the maria, craters small for the mass that made them, shock pressures below the asteroidal range, and ejecta poorer in iron than the mare, with no foreign signature. The first two follow from the lower speed, the third from the source. A crater grows with the speed of what makes it (eq. (1)), so a slow body of a given mass digs a smaller crater than a fast one. The shock pressure grows steeply with speed (eq. (6)): about 12.96 GPa at 2 km s−1^{-1} and 45.56 GPa at 5 km s−1^{-1} in the basalt example of §J.2, and far more at the speeds of an asteroid. And a body of the Earth’s light material leaves ejecta poorer in iron than the mare it lands on, and no foreign signature (§L.6). A mare crater of this age carrying the shock record of an asteroidal velocity is not this population; the population as a whole carries the shock of the lower speed. The capture fraction — what part of the small ejections of that time reached the Moon rather than the Earth — is an orbital calculation left to the Further work.

What we now have is an order of the crater populations, from the buried far-side record to the captured ejecta on the maria, read from superposition and state rather than from a rate of arrival.

PART N

Statistics

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

every comparison depends on what is compared with what. The approach: the samples, the bins and the measures are stated, with what is measured and what is assumed.

This Part states how the comparisons of the paper were made and how far they can be trusted. Two ideas run through all of them. The first is like for like: two groups are compared only when they differ in the one thing under test and are alike in everything else that could move the result. The craters are compared within the same size bin, because a large crater is naturally shallower for its width than a small one; the maria and South Pole–Aitken are removed from both sides of every comparison of the ground, because a mare erases craters and is rich in iron, and a sample that held more mare than its control would differ for that reason alone; and the samples were chosen and frozen before the quantity under test was read, so that the choice could not follow the answer. The second idea is beyond chance. Every measured difference scatters a little from sample to sample. A difference is counted as real only when it is larger than that scatter: when its 95% interval — a range that would hold the true value in 95 of every 100 repetitions — does not include zero (for a difference) or one (for a ratio), or when the probability of a result at least as strong, if there were no effect at all, is small.

N.1How the craters were compared

This section describes the measurements behind the shape of the craters (Parts F–I, Study 6). The second prediction — after the count of §C.3 — is that the landings were harder on one half than on the other (§K.4), and a harder landing should leave a crater of a different shape.

The second prediction is read on the shape of the craters. In the four caps of §C.3, with maria and South Pole–Aitken removed, 1,457 craters of 20–100 km from the Robbins catalogue [S6] were measured on the SLDEM2015 elevation model [S7], at about 60 m. The elevation model is a map of the height of the surface, built from laser altimetry and stereo images, in which a point is resolved every 60 m or so; a crater of 20 km is thus read on several hundred points across. For each crater five things were measured:

  • the rim: the height of the rim crest along 72 azimuths, its irregularity around the circle and its jump from one azimuth to the next, and the elongation of the rim;

  • the central peak: present or absent;

  • the floor: the share of the floor that is flat (slope below 2°) and its roughness;

  • the three heights of the crater, set out below;

  • the chemistry inside the crater, by zones, in a separate sample (§L.4).

The 72 azimuths are 72 directions around the crater, one every 5°, so that the rim is read as a closed line and not as a single number. Its irregularity measures how much the crest rises and falls around that line, and its jump measures how much it changes from one direction to the next: a rim broken by a hard landing is both. The three heights are those of §A.2: the rim above the land, the floor below the land, and the rim to the floor.

The comparisons on these measures were built to be like for like in three ways. Craters were compared only within the same size bin (20–30, 30–50 and 50–100 km, §A.2). The irregularity of the rim and the roughness of the floor were compared within the deeper half of each bin, so that a rim softened by time or a floor partly filled would not pass for a weaker landing. And the four caps are of equal size and lie on the equator, so that latitude, which crowds craters toward the poles, is the same for all four (§C.3). A difference between two regions is given as a ratio or a difference with a 95% interval found by resampling (§N.2); where the interval excludes zero, or one for a ratio, the difference is beyond chance. In the craters of 20–30 km, for example, the leading half’s rims are more irregular than the trailing half’s by a ratio of 1.25, with an interval of 1.00–1.48, and they stand 4.5 thousandths of the diameter lower, with an interval that excludes zero (§F.1, Study 6).

Two of the named tests need a word. The chi-square test asks whether the shares in a table of counts — here the share of melted craters in each zone — differ more than chance would make them if the share were the same everywhere. The Wilcoxon test compares each crater with its own control and asks whether the differences lean one way more often and more strongly than chance would make them; it uses the ranks of the differences, so that one extreme crater cannot drive the result.

N.2What is measured and what is assumed

Every measurement rests on choices, and this section names them, so that a reader can judge what each result depends on.

The central peak and the rim crest are classified by an automated reading of the elevation model, not by a manual geological audit; the prominence threshold, the window of the rim search and the cuts between the deeper and shallower halves and between the better- and less-preserved rims are operational and stated with the code. “Operational” means that each line was set by a stated rule chosen before the comparison, not by a judgement made crater by crater; the prominence threshold is how far a central rise must stand above the floor to count as a peak, and the window of the rim search is the band, between 0.85 and 1.15 of the radius (§A.2), in which the crest is looked for. The same rule was applied to every region, so that whatever error it carries, it carries alike on both sides of each comparison. The deeper half used for the rim irregularity and the floor roughness is read from the depth; the three heights are therefore compared over all craters. Selecting the deeper half by depth and then comparing depths would compare a quantity with itself, so the heights are read on the whole sample. The footprint of the central peak is traced by an automated rule; in 425 craters it is broad or reaches the edge of the floor, and the results hold without them. Removing those craters and finding the same result shows that the floor measures are not driven by peaks that spill onto the floor. The elongation figure is from the whole Robbins catalogue without the mask.

The intervals are bootstrap intervals over craters; they do not model spatial correlation between neighbouring craters. A bootstrap interval is found by drawing the sample again many times from itself, at random and with replacement, recomputing the result each time, and keeping the middle 95% of the results. Neighbouring craters are not fully independent — a source scatters several craters in one place — and an interval that treats them as independent can be somewhat narrower than the truth; for this reason the count of Study 1 and the count of Study 9 were also resampled by whole cells of the surface, and their intervals stayed clear of one (§N.1). The ring chemistry of §L.4 is read in fixed fractions of the radius, not on a mapped wall and floor of each crater, and its intervals do not include the calibration scatter of the maps. That scatter is 0.81 wt% FeO and 0.43 wt% TiO2_2 (§L.4), larger than every difference measured inside the craters; a difference smaller than it is read as “one material”, not as a measured gradient. Mare pixels inside the crater were kept, and removed only from the ejecta and the surroundings. Inside the crater nothing was removed, so that each crater is read whole; the ejecta and the surroundings serve as the reference ground, and removing the mare from them keeps that reference on the land, as the like-for-like rule requires.

What we now have is the measure of trust for each result of the paper: what it was compared with, on how many units, and how chance was set aside.

What each test reads. Each test reads what its claim requires.

  • Ejection, passage and landing. The model needs one set of conditions in which the energy left after the lift (eq. (5)), the passage through the vapour (eq. (7)) and the landing pressure (eq. (6)) all work together; the test is to fix that shared set.

  • A trend in a series of events. Eq. (14) says how the landing speed changes with the height of the target, but only for one source, one spin and one field (§K.3). The test reads a trend against what a stated version predicts, not against a single general trend, which the model does not claim.

  • Terrestrial source. The returning material came from the Earth at a known stage of its differentiation (Paper 3). A mixing model splits a crater’s material into the part of the body that landed and the part of the target; the test asks for the terrestrial composition that supplies the first part.

  • The maria as the source. If the maria threw the returning material, the ground beside them carries their iron, more than ground far away; this is what was measured (§C.5).

  • The ring of active spray. If volatiles drove the spray that built the rims of the maria, the maria richest in volatiles — read in their pyroclastic deposits, the dark ash of explosive eruptions, and in their hydration — carry the ring of small craters. A general ring on every rim was not found (Paper 5, §M.1); the test on the volatile-rich maria alone is open.

  • Buried feed domes. A dome that was buried under the layers of the far side carries the cover on top, not its own material; its core is therefore no more mafic than its surroundings — no richer in iron and magnesium — as measured (§D.1).

  • The longitudes. The body’s own ejecta fell back gently on the trailing half and were met head-on by the leading half (§K.4); the trailing half is therefore the more crowded, and the leading half the harder landed on.

  • The missing foreign mass. Bodies from outside bring the siderophile elements — the iron-loving metals such as iridium and ruthenium, which the Moon’s own crust almost lacks — and the isotopes of ruthenium tell an outside body from the Earth. The model expects the Earth’s isotopes in the crater melts, and no more outside metal than space dust supplies.

  • A record carried on the shell. The crater record of the highlands formed on the shell, survived, and rose with it (Paper 3). The test is that the time of its formation, the time it could be preserved and the time of the rise overlap.

  • Size and state. A melted crater lost its rim with its floor, because it sank into soft ground; a flooded crater keeps its rim and has only its floor filled. The height of the rim tells the two apart.

  • Material after release. What reached the Moon from the Earth after release was the lighter part of the Earth’s outflow (§L.6); among craters that dug only into the basalt, the ejecta are therefore poorer in iron than the mare.

  • The last scatterer. A source facing the near side leaves more of the last small craters there; the test counts fresh craters of 1–2 km, near against far, with the secondary craters of the young rayed craters removed.

  • Raised floors. A floor raised by a landing on soft ground and frozen in place can stand at or above the basalt around it; a floor filled from that basalt cannot. The test reads each floor against the basalt outside.

  • Iron of the high and low floors. A floor that froze high held its volatiles and the lighter material, and one that sank back was the denser; high floors are therefore poorer in iron than the low.

The chemical rows carry a condition of their own. Concentrations of siderophile elements must be derived from a defined source state. A deficiency must not be predicted and a chondritic abundance then also interpreted as confirmation through an undocumented change of the degree of differentiation. In plain words, the model may not predict too little metal and then, if a normal (chondritic, meteorite-like) amount is found, explain it after the fact by changing how far the Earth had differentiated; the source state is fixed first. The measured composition of the product of the landing includes mixing with target material; it does not describe the returning body alone. The comparison must also include differentiated external impacting bodies, that is, bodies from outside that had themselves separated a core and so carry little metal in their outer parts.

The geometric claim is kept briefly: the craters attributed to this stage are a record that rose with the shell and reached the far side and the limb, with a possible exception in the contact region. Their absence on young basalt does not prove such an exception, and circularity alone is no argument against transport: a crater that rose on the shell kept its round shape, so a round crater is no evidence against its having been carried. The mechanics and the mapping are detailed in Papers 3 and 11; the same prediction is not counted here as an additional independent confirmation.

Beyond the Moon. The crater mechanism of this paper is not particular to the Moon. A moon that was built from its parent and released from it remains, while the parent goes on throwing out material, under a rain of material returned from that same parent; its craters are made mainly by that material, not by bodies from outside. We therefore predict that the craters of every moon of this kind carry the composition of its parent planet, not that of outside bodies. Phobos and Deimos, which Paper 10 reads as fired from Mars, are the first case: the material of their craters should be Martian. The samples that the MMX mission is to return from Phobos test this directly: the model expects the material of its craters to be mainly Martian, with no more outside material than space dust supplies.

The rim records the forces at the moment of landing: lifted outward on the far side, pressed low on the leading half; and the height of the peak follows the rim. The floor records the ground the material landed on: the layers of the far side, crushed and melted, let it sink; the maria of the near side filled it from below. The halves differ in the way the body’s own ejecta predict. The trailing half, where the material came down gently, gathered the most craters and the smaller ones. The leading half, where the ground ran into the falling material, has fewer craters but harder ones: more central peaks, toothed and elongated rims, rough floors. The far side, its thrown-up material lifted outward by the shared rotation and its layers crushed and melted beneath the landing, holds the highest rims and the lowest floors. The near side, pressed against its ground and filled from below by the large maria, holds the lowest rims and the flattest and highest floors. What the landing did not change is the material: inside the crater, at every longitude, lies the returning material that made it, spread evenly from the centre to the rim.

A specialist may object that the leading half should be the more crowded, since a Moon that moves along its orbit meets more bodies from outside on its leading side; the literature predicts an excess there of some tens of per cent [S22, S23]. The count goes the other way (§C.3), by a margin that chance does not explain, and the shapes say why: the craters of the leading half are fewer but harder, as ground running into falling material makes them, and the trailing half holds the many, gentler ones. A flux from outside gives a leading-half excess and no reason for the trailing half’s gentler craters; the body’s own ejecta, falling back on a turning body, give both.

What we now have is one process read in three records: where the material landed, set by the turning of the attached body; how hard it landed, written in the centre and the rim; and what ground it landed on, written in the floor.

P.2What remains outside the paper

This section states the limits of the paper, so that what it claims is read at its right size.

This paper does not determine the origin of each individual crater or of SPA. The crater size distribution remains a constraint still to be computed: the ejected size and velocity distributions must be propagated through models of break-up, landing, saturation and burial. The size distribution is how many craters there are of each size; to predict it from the model, the sizes and speeds of what was thrown must be carried through every step that changes them — breaking up in flight, landing, and the later erasing of old craters by new ones (saturation) or by cover. Until that is done the distribution limits the model but does not test it. Neither saturation in every size range nor a time-constant flux underlying crater counts can be assumed. A comparison between radiometric age and model age can test chronology, but a mismatch does not by itself identify terrestrial magma: a radiometric age dates the cooling of a sample, and an age read from craters dates exposure under an assumed flux, so that a disagreement between them says that one of the two assumptions is wrong, not which source made the craters.

A construction rate must not be inferred from the number of craters remaining without computing how many were erased, how many returning bodies left material and how many caused loss. The craters seen today are what survived, not all that landed; counting them alone would give too low a rate. Material from the deep source that was ejected and returned is counted in the balance of Paper 3 once. Its later rise with the shell does not turn it into a new source of mass: the same material is not counted again when it is carried up.

P.3 The novelty and the contribution to the series

Ballistic flight, secondary cratering and shock relations are existing physics [S1–S5]. Ballistic flight is the path of a thrown body under gravity alone; secondary cratering is the making of craters by material thrown out of a larger crater; shock relations, such as eq. (6), give the pressure of a landing from its speed. The paper applies them to internal ejection onto the shell of a rising dome, and formulates the requirements the scenario must pass. Its independent contribution is the distinction between the return velocity and the capacity of the ejection source, the calculation of the velocity relative to the surface, and the inclusion of target gravity and atmospheric passage in the cratering constraints. In plain words: the speed at which material comes back is not the same question as how much the opening can throw (§J.1, eq. (11)); what makes a crater is the speed relative to a surface that is itself turning (§K.2); and a crater on the parent forms under the parent’s gravity and after a passage through its vapour (§C.6, §J.3), not under the gravity of the Moon today. It does not repeat the construction of the body from within, which is the subject of Papers 2 and 3. What Paper 6 adds to the series is the crater record read as the record of the maria and of the Earth: the source of the craters, the sorting of their landings by the turning of the attached body, and the order in which the floors froze.

Conclusions

THE PROBLEM AND THE APPROACH

what the paper has shown, in order. The approach: one line for each result, with the part that carries it. Each conclusion below states first what was found and then what it means for the picture of the craters; the part or section named in brackets is where the calculation or the measurement and its test are given in full.

The paper set out to answer one question: if the craters of the Moon were not dug by bodies from outside, what made them, and what do they record? Its answer is that a crater is the place where returning material landed — material thrown out of the openings, the maria, that came back down under gravity — and the parts of the paper have tested that answer in two ways. The first five conclusions concern the physics of the landing itself: how fast the material comes back, what pressure it makes, and what a source must supply for it to happen (Parts J and K). The last four concern the record on the Moon today: the foreign mass that the craters should have left and did not, the maria as the source, the craters that were lost, and the difference between the longitudes (Parts C–I and L). Conclusion 6 joins the two: it names the tests that decide whether the crater population belongs to the mechanism.

Q.1What the paper has shown

  1. A returning body lands as fast as it was thrown (§K.1). In the central field tested, material returning to a corotating surface at the same radius lands at a relative velocity equal to the ejection velocity. Gravitational return does not require a slow landing. The reason is simple: the material leaves with the speed of the turning surface plus its own upward speed uu; when it comes back to the same distance from the centre, gravity has returned exactly the speed it took during the climb, and the surface below is still turning at the same rate, so the only difference left between the two is the speed uu (equation 11). Material thrown at 2, 3, 5 or 8 km/s comes back at 2, 3, 5 or 8 km/s. What it means: falling back does not soften a landing. Material thrown up from the openings returns with the full speed it was thrown with, and that is enough to make a crater; the question is therefore not whether returning material can make craters but whether the source can throw it fast enough (conclusion 3).

  2. At these speeds the landing makes shock pressures of tens of gigapascals (§J.2). In the ideal basalt example, 2–5 km/s give contact-pressure estimates of about 13–46 GPa (12.96 GPa at 2 km/s, 45.56 GPa at 5 km/s, from the measured shock behaviour of basalt [S3]). These are pressures at the point of contact in a head-on landing of like on like, not pressures across the whole crater. The result requires matching of material and geometry before application to a lunar sample. What it means: a landing of returning material at a few km/s is a shock event, of the kind that leaves a record in the minerals; it is not a gentle settling. To compare the number with a particular lunar sample, the actual pair of materials (for example molten material landing on the cooler shell), the angle of arrival and the porosity must be put in; that is a stated step of the test, not a weakness of the result.

  3. The source must throw the material fast, the material must cross the vapour, and it must arrive in one piece (§J.1, J.3, J.5). The exit velocity, atmospheric passage and the survival of a coherent returning body are the three requirements this test hands to Paper 3. To throw a kilogram at 2–8 km/s takes 2–32 MJ (equation 5). The pressure from the Earth supplies it: the spin and the sorting create the pressure in the Earth’s interior, and it passes through the pear to the openings and sprays the material out of them. The gas dissolved in the melt — the volatiles, chiefly water — assists: as the melt rises and the pressure on it falls, the gas expands and adds its push, as a shaken bottle drives out its contents when opened. The full energy account, after the costs of lift, flow and load support (the lift cost of 139.78 GPa in Paper 3), is a requirement of the model. On the way back the body must cross the vapour atmosphere of the scenario; a body one kilometre across crossing a column at 100 bar keeps about half its speed (§J.3). And material thrown faster than the escape speed, about 10.7 km/s in the example, does not come back at all (§J.5). What it means: the three requirements define one window — fast enough to make a crater, slow enough to stay bound, large and dense enough to cross the vapour — and the pressure from the Earth, assisted by the volatiles, must supply that window. The volatiles both help the ejection and thicken the atmosphere the body must cross; one domain of conditions must satisfy both, and that is how the requirement is handed to Paper 3.

  4. A shock trend or a shock ceiling depends on the path, not on the source alone (§K.3, J.5). A decreasing shock trend and a numerical shock ceiling belong to a defined path and source; they are not universal signatures of all returning terrestrial material. The landing speed falls with height up to the corotation radius (about 15,883 km in the early-stage data here) and rises beyond it (equation 14); but whether a series of samples shows a trend in time depends on how the ejection speed, the rotation, the target and the angle changed between events. What it means: a single shock number cannot rule the terrestrial source in or out. Earlier versions of the series gave ceilings of 113 or 97 GPa and a trend of 102→86 GPa; these held for one family of paths, and a sample above such a ceiling does not by itself set the source aside. The test is therefore stated per path and per source (§O.1, “a trend in a series of events”).

  5. A crater made on the parent must be computed in the parent’s conditions (§C.6, J.4). Creation of craters on the parent body requires using the gravity and rheology of the target at that time, and then computing preservation and transport according to Paper 3. Rheology is the way the ground flows or breaks under load — whether it behaves as a stiff solid or as a soft, slowly flowing one. On the parent the gravity was about 8.25 m/s², five times the Moon’s today; the same crater there needs a returning body several times more massive than on the Moon now (§C.6). The receiving surface must also be thick and strong enough to keep a crater of that size, and the crater must be carried, with the shell it lies on, to where it is seen today (Paper 3). What it means: the craters of the far side cannot be read with the scaling laws of today’s Moon alone. Each crater of the record passed three stages — it was made on the parent, it survived, and it rose with the shell — and each stage is part of the calculation.

  6. The paper defines the test, and the tests of §O.1 decide the attribution. Paper 6 has an independent role: it defines a test for the landing of the returning material. Attribution of the crater population to the mechanism is decided by the tests of §O.1, among them — ejection and flight, the trend in a series of events, the terrestrial source, the maria as the source, the ring of active spray, the buried feed domes, the longitudes, the missing foreign mass, and the record carried on the shell — each stated with what it reads. What it means: the physics of Parts J–K shows that the landing can happen; the measurements of Parts C–I and L show whether it did. Several of those tests have now been run — the maria as the source, the buried feed domes, the longitudes and the missing foreign mass — and their results are the four conclusions that follow. Each test reads its own measurement, so that the attribution is checked measurement by measurement.

  7. The craters carry no foreign mass (§L.5). The foreign mass is not there. Read as external impacting bodies, the visible craters require up to 6.7 times the foreign mass the crust holds, and the literature’s whole bombardment fourteen to eighteen times more than crust and mantle hold (an earlier version gave “up to six times” and “seven to seventeen times”); the literature closes the gap by assuming that some 93% of the delivered mass vanished. The ruthenium of the lunar crater melts, where measured, is the Earth’s (§L.5). The measure is the highly siderophile elements — iridium, osmium, platinum, ruthenium, the “iron-loving” elements that went to the core when the Moon separated into layers, so that the crust holds almost none of its own and any addition from outside stands out. In the impact reading the visible craters require 4.44.4–5.0×10195.0\times10^{19} kg of foreign mass, 2–6% of the upper 5 km of the crust; the crust holds 0.9–2% at most. The whole bombardment of the literature requires 3.7×10203.7\times10^{20} kg; crust and mantle together hold 2.12.1–2.7×10192.7\times10^{19}. The literature bridges the gap with a low retention (a fifth instead of about three fifths) and with loss to the core before about 4.35 Gyr. The isotopes of ruthenium tell which reservoir of the solar system the element came from, and in the two samples measured they match the Earth’s silicate [S14]. What it means: in the impact reading the missing mass must be assumed away, crater by crater. In the model there is nothing to explain: the material that made the craters was the Earth’s, of the same kind as the shell it landed on, and its siderophile elements carry the Earth’s isotopes.

  8. The maria are the source, and the craters were lost in two ways (§C.1, C.5, L.1; D.1–D.2). The maria are the source of the returning material: the ground around each mare carries its iron and titanium, falling off with distance (§C.1; measured in §C.5 and §L.1). Around the six maria tested, the iron oxide (FeO) of the ground is 5.96 wt% within 50 km of the edge against 2.30 wt% in control craters more than 600 km from any basalt, and it stays above the controls out to 600 km. Craters were lost twice: on the far side, buried with the sources that made them (§D.1), and on the floors of the maria they melted, 49 of 79 of 10 km and more, with one sharp crater of 20 km and more on 1.2 million km2^2, against 207 per million km2^2 on the highlands (§D.2); on the maria of the contact the large ones vanished altogether (§D.2). On the 703,000 km2^2 of the floors of the maria of the contact not one crater of 20 km and more remains. What it means: a mare is not a target that collected craters but a source that threw material around it — the iron is highest beside the mare and thins outward, the mark of a source. The same activity that made the craters also erased them: on the far side the sources were covered by the layers they and the parent laid down, and the craters went under with them; on the open maria the craters landed on a hot floor and melted into it, about three in five (62%). The craters seen today are the last record written, not the first.

  9. The two halves of the Moon record the body’s own ejection (Parts F–I, §K.4). The longitudes record the body’s own ejection: the trailing half, 90°E, is the more crowded, and the leading half, 90°W, the one landed on hardest — more central peaks, more jagged rims in the small craters, rougher floors; the far side the highest rims, the leading half the lowest; the near side, by the maria, the flattest and highest floors; and inside the crater, at every longitude, one material. The centre records the energy of the landing, the rim the forces at that moment, the floor the ground beneath (Parts F–H). The leading half is the half that faces the Moon’s motion in orbit, centred on 90°W; the trailing half faces backward, centred on 90°E. The density of craters, maria and South Pole–Aitken removed on both sides, is 249–252 per million km2^2 at 90°E against 139–175 at 90°W. Material thrown from the attached, turning body at its own escape speed or less fell back on it, and the turning decided where and how hard: on the trailing half it came down gently and often, on the leading half the ground ran into it head-on, as a runner meets rain on the face and not on the back. What it means: the difference between the halves is not a difference of what fell, since the material inside the crater is the same at every longitude, but of how it landed. One process, read in three parts of each crater, gives a consistent picture across the whole Moon.

Taken together, the nine results describe one process seen from two sides. The physics shows that material thrown from the openings returns at the speed it left, makes shock pressures of tens of gigapascals, and requires a source that throws it fast enough and a body that crosses the vapour intact. The record shows that the maria were the source, that the crater record was written and erased by the same activity, that the craters carry no foreign mass, and that the two halves of the Moon were landed on in the way the body’s own turning predicts. The deciding tests are those of §O.1, each with its measurement.

  4. Shock in a hot, layered target. Impedance matching for a dense returning body on a cold sialic shell over a mobile base, and the resulting crater scaling at g=8.25g=8.25 m/s², would replace the basalt-on-basalt estimate of §J.2. Impedance matching is the calculation of how the shock pressure is shared at the contact between two different materials, which depends on the density and stiffness of each; the sialic shell is the light crust, rich in silicon and aluminium, of the parent.

  5. The longitude record. A manual audit of central peaks and rims in the four caps of §N.1, the join with the catalogue of lunar melt flows [S8], and the same measures by crater age, would fix how much of the difference between 90°W and 90°E is landing strength and how much is the maria. The measures of Parts F–I were made automatically on the elevation model; checking them by eye, and adding the melt flows that a hard landing produces, separates the two causes.

  6. Siderophile signatures. A mixing model of terrestrial returning body and terrestrial target for the oldest landing melts, to distinguish returning material from asteroidal impacting bodies by their siderophile-element patterns. Since in the model both the returning body and the target are terrestrial, the melt should carry one pattern; an asteroidal body would add a second, foreign one. The individual craters of known size named in §L.5 are the natural first samples.

  7. Rays and the cooling of the near-side floors (the author’s proposal). The near-side maria stayed liquid for a long time, warmed by the Moon’s internal heat and by the Earth’s heat; the basalt plains, such as Oceanus Procellarum, where Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus lie, were exposed to the same great heat of the Earth and are in this very similar to the mare floors. Material that landed on these floors while they were hot lost its record. The author proposes that rays form only when material lands on a floor that has already cooled; on a hot floor they do not form at all. The rays are a record within the ground beneath the surface, not material lying on it. As the floors cooled they came to carry rays: when they had cooled, Copernicus, Kepler and Aristarchus landed hard and left the spider-like rays; Diophantus and Delisle landed while the floor was not yet fully frozen and left no rays; many others landed on a hot floor and left no mark. The study: count the small and medium craters on the near side, on the maria and on the basalt plains, with and without rays. The author expects those without rays to outnumber those with rays, because the amount of material landing on the Moon fell as the Moon receded.
